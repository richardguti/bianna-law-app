# CHAPTER 1: THE STRUCTURAL CONSTITUTION — FEDERALISM

## I. The Foundation: McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)

### McCulloch v. Maryland
**17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819)**

**FACTS:** Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States. Maryland imposed a tax on all banks not chartered by the state — effectively targeting the federal bank. James McCulloch, the cashier of the Baltimore branch, refused to pay. Maryland sued.

**ISSUE:**
1. Does Congress have the power to incorporate a bank?
2. May a state tax a federal instrumentality?

**RULE:**
- **Necessary and Proper Clause (Art. I, § 8, cl. 18):** Congress may enact laws that are a "necessary and proper" means to execute its enumerated powers.
- **Supremacy Clause (Art. VI):** Federal law is supreme; states cannot impede valid federal action.

**APPLICATION:**

*Issue 1 — The Bank:*
Chief Justice Marshall rejected Maryland's argument that "necessary" means "absolutely indispensable." He read it broadly:

- **"Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the Constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consistent with the letter and spirit of the Constitution, are constitutional."**
- Congress has enumerated powers over taxation, borrowing, commerce, and war. A national bank is a convenient, useful, and appropriate means to execute those powers.
- The Necessary and Proper Clause is listed among Congress's **powers**, not its **limitations**.

*Issue 2 — The Tax:*
- **"The power to tax involves the power to destroy."** If Maryland can tax the bank, it can tax it out of existence — giving one state a veto over federal policy.
- The people of Maryland did not elect the federal government; the people of the *whole nation* did. Maryland cannot use its taxing power to control an entity answerable to a different sovereign.

**CONCLUSION:** Congress has implied powers beyond those enumerated. The bank is constitutional. Maryland's tax is unconstitutional under the Supremacy Clause.

---

### 🏛️ Fisher Trap: The "Necessary" Spectrum

| Interpretation | Meaning | Proponent |
|---|---|---|
| **Strict/Narrow** | "Absolutely indispensable" | Maryland / Jefferson / Strict Constructionists |
| **Broad/Liberal** | "Convenient, useful, conducive to" | Marshall / Hamilton / Implied Powers |

> **Fisher Alert:** On an exam, if a professor asks "Does Congress have the power to do X?", **always start with McCulloch**. Congress doesn't need an enumerated power for every specific action — it needs an enumerated *end* and a rational *means*.

---

### The "Pretext" Limitation

Marshall added a crucial limit: Congress cannot use the Necessary and Proper Clause as a **pretext** to achieve ends not entrusted to it.

- **The Test:** "Should Congress, under the pretext of executing its powers, pass laws for the accomplishment of objects not entrusted to the government, it would become the painful duty of this tribunal... to say that such an act was not the law of the land."
- **Modern Application:** This "pretext" limit was revived in *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) when the Court rejected the individual mandate as a valid exercise of the Commerce + N&P power.

## II. Modern Necessary & Proper: United States v. Comstock (2010)

### United States v. Comstock
**560 U.S. 126 (2010)**

**FACTS:** A federal statute (18 U.S.C. § 4248) allowed the government to civilly commit "sexually dangerous" federal prisoners *beyond the end of their prison sentence*. Graydon Comstock, a federal prisoner whose sentence was about to expire, challenged the law, arguing Congress had no enumerated power to civilly commit people.

**ISSUE:** Does the Necessary and Proper Clause give Congress the authority to enact a civil commitment statute for sexually dangerous federal prisoners?

**RULE:** The Necessary and Proper Clause grants Congress broad authority to enact laws that are "rationally related" to the implementation of a constitutionally enumerated power, provided the law does not violate an independent constitutional prohibition.

**APPLICATION:**

Justice Breyer, writing for the 7-2 majority, identified **five considerations**:

1. **Breadth of the N&P Clause:** McCulloch established that "necessary" does not mean "indispensable." Congress has wide latitude.
2. **Long History:** The federal government has been responsible for the mental health of federal prisoners for over a century (since 1855).
3. **Sound Reasoning:** Congress was acting as a "custodian" — it put these people in prison, it knows they are dangerous, and releasing them without treatment would endanger the public.
4. **Accommodation of State Interests:** The statute requires the government to notify the state where the prisoner is domiciled and give the state the first shot at commitment.
5. **Narrow Scope:** The statute is limited to federal prisoners already in federal custody.

**CONCLUSION:** The statute is constitutional. It is a "necessary and proper" means to effectuate the federal criminal justice system.

### The Dissents

- **Justice Thomas (joined by Scalia):** Argued the majority conflated "convenient" with "necessary." He demanded a tighter link: Congress must show the law is "carrying into execution" a specific enumerated power, not just a general "custodial" responsibility.
- **Justice Kennedy (Concurrence):** Agreed with the result but wanted stricter scrutiny — the law must not be a "substantial expansion" of federal authority.
- **Justice Alito (Concurrence):** Focused on the "accommodation" prong — the law was acceptable because it deferred to states.

---

### 🏛️ Fisher Tip: Comstock vs. Lopez

| Feature | Comstock (Valid) | Lopez (Invalid) |
|---|---|---|
| **Federal Nexus** | Federal prisoners in federal custody | Students in local schools |
| **History** | 150+ years of federal custodial responsibility | No history of federal school-zone regulation |
| **State Accommodation** | States get first shot at commitment | No coordination with states |
| **Scope** | Narrow (only federal prisoners) | Broad (all school zones nationwide) |

## III. Term Limits & the Structural Constitution

### U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton (1995)
**514 U.S. 779 (1995)**

**FACTS:** Arkansas amended its state constitution to prohibit anyone who had already served three terms in the U.S. House or two terms in the U.S. Senate from appearing on the ballot for re-election.

**ISSUE:** May a state impose term limits (or additional qualifications) on its members of Congress beyond those listed in the Constitution?

**RULE:** The Qualifications Clauses of Articles I, §§ 2 and 3 set forth the **exclusive** qualifications for membership in Congress: Age, Citizenship, and Residency. States may not add to, subtract from, or alter these qualifications.

**APPLICATION:**

Justice Stevens, writing for the 5-4 majority:

- **Textual Argument:** The Constitution lists three qualifications. If the Framers intended states to add more, they would have said so (expressio unius).
- **Historical Argument:** At the time of the Framing, the dominant view was that the people should choose their representatives freely, without state-imposed barriers.
- **Structural Argument — "The People of the Nation":** Members of Congress represent the *national* interest, not just their state. Allowing states to add qualifications would create a "patchwork" of different eligibility rules, undermining national unity.
- **The Powell Precedent:** In *Powell v. McCormack* (1969), the Court held that even *Congress itself* could not add qualifications beyond those listed. If Congress can't do it, surely a state can't.

**CONCLUSION:** The Arkansas amendment is unconstitutional. Only a constitutional amendment (like the 22nd Amendment for the President) can impose term limits on Congress.

### Justice Thomas's Dissent (Critical for Fisher)

Thomas argued for a "Reserved Powers" view:
- **10th Amendment:** Powers not delegated to the federal government are reserved to the states *or the people*.
- **Original Understanding:** The states existed before the Constitution and retained the power to set qualifications for their own representatives unless the Constitution explicitly prohibited it.
- **"The People of the States":** Thomas rejected the "national constituency" idea. He argued that each state's people elect their own representatives, so each state should have the power to set qualifications.

---

### 🏛️ Fisher Alert: The "Source of Power" Debate

| View | Who Holds Sovereignty? | Proponent |
|---|---|---|
| **National Sovereignty** | "We the People" of the whole nation | Stevens (Majority) |
| **State Sovereignty** | "We the People" of each individual state | Thomas (Dissent) |

> **Exam Tip:** If you see a question about state power vs. federal structure, *Thornton* is your cite for the principle that the **federal Constitution sets the floor AND the ceiling** for federal qualifications. States cannot freelance.

# CHAPTER 2: THE COMMERCE POWER — FULL CASE BRIEFS

## I. The Modern Commerce Power Framework

After *Gibbons v. Ogden* (1824) established Congress's broad power over interstate commerce, the modern era recognizes **three categories** of regulable activity under the Commerce Clause:

1. **Channels** of interstate commerce (highways, waterways, internet)
2. **Instrumentalities** of interstate commerce (trucks, trains, planes, people/things moving in commerce)
3. **Activities with a substantial effect** on interstate commerce

> **Fisher's Golden Rule:** Categories 1 and 2 are "easy wins" for Congress. Category 3 is where all the fights happen.

---

## II. The Civil Rights Era Commerce Cases

### Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States (1964)
**379 U.S. 241 (1964)**

**FACTS:** The Heart of Atlanta Motel, located near interstate highways in downtown Atlanta, had 216 rooms and refused to rent to Black guests. It solicited patronage from outside Georgia through national advertising and approximately 75% of its guests were from out of state. The motel challenged Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which prohibited racial discrimination in "public accommodations" that affected interstate commerce.

**ISSUE:** Does Congress have the power under the Commerce Clause to prohibit racial discrimination in a private motel that serves interstate travelers?

**RULE:** Congress may regulate local activities that have a "substantial and harmful effect" on interstate commerce, even if the specific business is "local" in nature.

**APPLICATION:**

Justice Clark, writing for a unanimous Court:

- **The Record:** Congress compiled an extensive record showing that discrimination in hotels and motels deterred Black citizens from traveling interstate, which suppressed commerce.
- **Rational Basis:** The Court applied a highly deferential standard: if Congress has a *rational basis* for finding that a regulated activity affects interstate commerce, the Court will not substitute its judgment.
- **The "Moral vs. Commercial" Debate:** The motel argued this was really a "moral" regulation, not a "commercial" one. The Court responded that Congress's *motive* is irrelevant — as long as the *means* (regulating commerce) are within Congress's power, the *ends* (eliminating discrimination) are permissible.

**CONCLUSION:** Title II is a valid exercise of the Commerce Power. The motel must serve guests of all races.

---

### Katzenbach v. McClung (1964)
**379 U.S. 294 (1964)**

**FACTS:** Ollie's Barbecue was a family-owned restaurant in Birmingham, Alabama. It was located 11 blocks from an interstate highway and had no out-of-state customers. However, approximately 46% of its food was purchased from a supplier who procured it from out of state. Ollie's refused to serve Black customers in its dining room.

**ISSUE:** Can Congress regulate a purely local restaurant under the Commerce Clause simply because it purchases food that has moved in interstate commerce?

**RULE:** Same as *Heart of Atlanta* — Congress may regulate local activities if it has a rational basis for concluding they substantially affect interstate commerce.

**APPLICATION:**

- **The "Aggregate" Theory:** Even though Ollie's was one small restaurant, the Court looked at the *aggregate effect* of all restaurants that discriminate. Together, they suppress demand for food shipped interstate, deter interstate travel, and reduce the tax base.
- **The Supply-Side Link:** Ollie's purchased meat from a supplier who got it from out of state. This "supply chain" link was enough to establish a connection to interstate commerce.

**CONCLUSION:** Ollie's Barbecue must comply with Title II. Congress has the rational basis to conclude that discrimination in restaurants collectively affects interstate commerce.

---

### 🏛️ Fisher Tip: The Commerce Clause "Side-Door"

Remember the *Civil Rights Cases* (1883)? The Court said Congress **could not** use the 14th Amendment to regulate private discrimination. So how did Congress finally do it?

- **The Side-Door:** Congress used the **Commerce Clause** (Article I, § 8) instead of the **14th Amendment** (§ 5).
- **The Result:** The same goal (ending private discrimination) was achieved through a different constitutional power.
- **The Lesson:** If one power source fails, look for another. This is classic "Fisher-level" structural analysis.

## III. The Rehnquist Revolution: Limiting the Commerce Power

### United States v. Lopez (1995)
**514 U.S. 549 (1995)**

**FACTS:** Alfonso Lopez, a 12th-grade student in San Antonio, Texas, carried a concealed .38 caliber handgun to school. He was charged under the federal Gun-Free School Zones Act of 1990 (GFSZA), which made it a federal crime to possess a firearm within 1,000 feet of a school.

**ISSUE:** Does the Gun-Free School Zones Act exceed Congress's power under the Commerce Clause?

**RULE:** Under the Commerce Clause, Congress may regulate:
1. The channels of interstate commerce;
2. The instrumentalities of, or persons/things in, interstate commerce; and
3. Activities that **substantially affect** interstate commerce.

For Category 3, the activity must have a **substantial effect** on interstate commerce, not merely an attenuated or theoretical one.

**APPLICATION:**

Chief Justice Rehnquist, writing for the 5-4 majority:

- **Not Commercial Activity:** Gun possession in a school zone is not an "economic" or "commercial" activity. It has nothing to do with buying, selling, or producing goods.
- **No Jurisdictional Element:** The statute contained no requirement that the firearm had "moved in interstate commerce."
- **No Congressional Findings:** Congress made no formal findings explaining the link between guns near schools and interstate commerce.
- **The "Infinity" Problem:** The government argued: Guns → Violence → Bad Education → Less Productive Workers → Lower Commerce. Rehnquist rejected this "costs of crime" reasoning because under that logic, Congress could regulate *anything* (marriage, divorce, child custody) — obliterating the line between federal and state power.

**CONCLUSION:** The GFSZA is unconstitutional. It exceeded Congress's Commerce Clause power because gun possession near schools is not economic activity and has no substantial effect on interstate commerce.

---

### The Critical Concurrences & Dissents

**Justice Kennedy (Concurrence):** Emphasized that the case was about *federalism* — preserving the states' traditional role in education and criminal law. He warned that if the federal government can regulate local school safety, there is nothing left for states to do.

**Justice Thomas (Concurrence):** Went further. He argued the "substantial effects" test itself should be abandoned because it has no limiting principle. He would return to the original meaning: Congress can only regulate the actual *movement* of goods across state lines.

**Justice Breyer (Dissent):** Argued the majority was ignoring "empirical reality." He cited studies showing that gun violence costs the economy billions and that education quality directly impacts the national workforce. He would use a much more deferential rational basis test.

---

### 🏛️ Fisher Decision Tree: Commerce Clause Analysis

```
STEP 1: Is Congress regulating a CHANNEL of interstate commerce?
  → YES → Valid (Heart of Atlanta)
  → NO → Go to Step 2

STEP 2: Is Congress regulating an INSTRUMENTALITY or person/thing IN commerce?
  → YES → Valid
  → NO → Go to Step 3

STEP 3: Does the regulated activity SUBSTANTIALLY AFFECT interstate commerce?
  → Ask Sub-Questions:
    (a) Is the activity ECONOMIC/COMMERCIAL in nature?
        → YES → Congress may aggregate (Wickard/Raich) → Likely VALID
        → NO → Go to (b)
    (b) Does the statute have a JURISDICTIONAL ELEMENT?
        → YES → Strengthens the case
        → NO → Weakens the case
    (c) Did Congress make FINDINGS linking the activity to commerce?
        → YES → Strengthens (but not dispositive)
        → NO → Weakens
    (d) Is the link to commerce ATTENUATED (requires a chain of inference)?
        → YES → Likely INVALID (Lopez/Morrison)
        → NO → Likely VALID
```

### United States v. Morrison (2000) — Commerce Clause Prong
**529 U.S. 598 (2000)**

> **Note:** Your V2 covers Morrison's § 5 analysis in detail (lines 4070-4097). This supplement covers the **Commerce Clause** prong that V2 does not brief.

**FACTS:** Same as V2 — Christy Brzonkala sued under VAWA. Congress also relied on the Commerce Clause as a power source, arguing gender-motivated violence costs the economy $5-10 billion annually.

**ISSUE (Commerce Prong):** Does gender-motivated violence "substantially affect" interstate commerce?

**APPLICATION:**

Chief Justice Rehnquist applied the *Lopez* framework:

- **Not Economic Activity:** Gender-motivated crimes of violence are not "economic activity" in any meaningful sense. They are violent crimes — traditionally the domain of state criminal law.
- **The "Attenuated" Chain:** The government's argument (violence → medical costs → lost productivity → less commerce) was the same "costs of crime" reasoning rejected in *Lopez*.
- **The "Aggregate" Limit:** Rehnquist held that the aggregation principle (*Wickard*) only applies to **economic** activity. You cannot aggregate non-economic conduct.
- **Congressional Findings Don't Save It:** Even though Congress compiled a massive record of gender violence's economic impact, the Court held that "simply because Congress may conclude that a particular activity substantially affects interstate commerce does not necessarily make it so."

**CONCLUSION:** VAWA's civil remedy is also unconstitutional under the Commerce Clause.

---

### Gonzales v. Raich (2005)
**545 U.S. 1 (2005)**

**FACTS:** Angel Raich and Diane Monson grew marijuana at home for personal medical use, as permitted under California's Compassionate Use Act. Federal agents seized and destroyed Monson's plants under the Controlled Substances Act (CSA). The plaintiffs argued that purely local, non-commercial cultivation of marijuana for personal medical use was beyond Congress's Commerce Clause power (citing *Lopez*).

**ISSUE:** Can Congress regulate the purely local cultivation and possession of marijuana for personal medical use under the Commerce Clause?

**RULE:** Congress may regulate purely local activity if it is part of a broader **"comprehensive regulatory scheme"** and failure to regulate the local activity would undercut the larger scheme.

**APPLICATION:**

Justice Stevens, writing for the 6-3 majority:

- **The Wickard Principle:** Just as Filburn's homegrown wheat affected the national wheat market, homegrown marijuana affects the national drug market. If people grow their own, they won't buy from the interstate (illegal) market, undercutting Congress's ability to regulate that market.
- **Economic Activity:** Unlike the guns in *Lopez* or the violence in *Morrison*, marijuana cultivation is **economic** in nature — it involves the production of a commodity (even for personal consumption).
- **The "Comprehensive Scheme" Rationale:** The CSA is a comprehensive scheme to regulate all controlled substances. If California can carve out an exception, other states will too, creating a "Swiss cheese" effect that destroys the federal regulatory structure.
- **Rational Basis:** Congress need only have a rational basis for believing that homegrown marijuana, in the aggregate, substantially affects the interstate drug market.

**CONCLUSION:** The CSA is a valid exercise of Commerce Power as applied to local medical marijuana. *Lopez* and *Morrison* are distinguished because those cases involved **non-economic** activity.

---

### 🏛️ Fisher Trap: The Lopez/Raich Distinction

| Factor | Lopez (Invalid) | Raich (Valid) |
|---|---|---|
| **Nature of Activity** | Gun possession (NOT economic) | Marijuana cultivation (IS economic — production of a commodity) |
| **Regulatory Scheme** | Stand-alone statute | Part of comprehensive CSA |
| **Aggregation** | Cannot aggregate non-economic conduct | CAN aggregate economic conduct |
| **State Role** | Education = traditional state function | Drug enforcement = shared federal/state |

> **Exam Tip:** If the activity is **economic** (production, distribution, consumption of a commodity), Congress can aggregate under *Raich* and will almost always win. If it is **non-economic** (violence, gun possession, family law), Congress loses under *Lopez/Morrison*.

## IV. The ACA and the "Outer Limit": NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)

### National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius
**567 U.S. 519 (2012)**

**FACTS:** The Affordable Care Act (ACA) included an "individual mandate" requiring most Americans to purchase health insurance or pay a "shared responsibility payment." Congress argued this was valid under the Commerce Clause (regulating the massive health insurance market), the Necessary and Proper Clause, and the Taxing Power.

**ISSUE:** Does the Commerce Clause or the Necessary and Proper Clause authorize Congress to compel individuals to purchase health insurance?

**APPLICATION (Commerce Clause — REJECTED 5-4):**

Chief Justice Roberts:

- **Activity vs. Inactivity:** The Commerce Clause regulates existing *activity*. It does not compel people to *enter* commerce. Not buying insurance is **inactivity** — the absence of commerce.
- **"Everyone is in the market":** The government argued everyone will eventually need health care. Roberts responded: "The Commerce Clause is not a general license to regulate an individual from cradle to grave."
- **The "Broccoli" Hypothetical:** If Congress can force you to buy insurance because it's good for you, can they force you to buy broccoli? The limiting principle disappears.

**APPLICATION (Necessary & Proper — REJECTED):**

- Roberts invoked McCulloch's **"pretext"** limitation. If the underlying Commerce power doesn't authorize the mandate, the N&P Clause cannot save it. The N&P Clause is a supplement, not an independent font of power.
- A law that "creates the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" is not a proper exercise of that power.

**APPLICATION (Taxing Power — UPHELD):**

- The "shared responsibility payment" functions like a **tax**: it is collected by the IRS, produces revenue, and applies to taxpayers who file returns.
- Under the Taxing Power, Congress need not rely on the Commerce Clause. A tax is valid if it is not so punitive as to be a "penalty."

**CONCLUSION:** The individual mandate cannot be sustained under the Commerce Clause or the Necessary and Proper Clause. However, it is valid as a **tax** under the Taxing Power.

---

### The Medicaid Expansion (7-2 Rejection)

The ACA also expanded Medicaid, threatening to withdraw **all** existing Medicaid funding from states that refused to expand coverage.

- **The "Gun to the Head":** Roberts held this was unconstitutional coercion — not a mere "inducement" like *South Dakota v. Dole*. Threatening to withdraw an existing, massive program (10% of state budgets) left states with "no real choice."
- **The Dole Line:** Congress can attach conditions to **new** funding. It cannot threaten to take away **existing** funding as punishment for not accepting new conditions.

### 🏛️ Fisher Flowchart: Taxing & Spending Power

```
TAXING POWER:
  Is the "penalty" collected by the IRS? → YES
  Does it produce revenue? → YES
  Is it imposed on taxpayers filing returns? → YES
  Is it so punitive it functions as a criminal sanction? → NO
  → VALID TAX (NFIB)

SPENDING POWER (South Dakota v. Dole):
  (1) Is spending for the "general welfare"? → YES
  (2) Are conditions stated "unambiguously"? → YES
  (3) Are conditions related to the federal interest? → YES
  (4) Is the spending independently constitutional? → YES
  (5) Is the financial pressure so great it's COERCION? → IF YES → INVALID (NFIB)
```
