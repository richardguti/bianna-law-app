**ESSAY 1: POLICY QUESTION — Mixed Theory of Punishment & Three Strikes Legislation**

**GOAL**

The prompt invites an examination of whether the so-called “Three Strikes” sentencing regime, particularly its imposition of life imprisonment upon a third felony conviction, can be reconciled with a Mixed Theory of Punishment. A Mixed Theory, properly understood, does not merely aggregate retributive and utilitarian justifications; it demands internal coherence, ensuring that each punishment is simultaneously *deserved* (retribution) and *efficacious* (utilitarian). The central doctrinal tension is whether a mandatory life sentence for a non-violent third felony—such as petty theft with a prior record—can satisfy both the backward-looking demand for proportional desert and the forward-looking goals of deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Architecture of the Mixed Theory**

The Mixed Theory, as articulated by scholars such as H.L.A. Hart and refined in the Model Penal Code’s sentencing provisions, posits that the justification for punishment as an institution rests on utilitarian grounds (crime prevention), while the *distribution* of punishment—the quantum imposed on a particular offender—must be constrained by retributive principles of proportionality. See MPC § 1.02(2) (stating purposes include “to prevent the commission of offenses” and “to give fair warning of the nature of the sentences that may be imposed”). This is not a mere compromise; it is a structural imperative. Retribution provides the ceiling (no punishment beyond what is deserved) and the floor (no punishment for the innocent), while utility fills the interior, selecting the precise sanction that maximizes social benefit within those bounds.

Three Strikes laws, however, invert this hierarchy. By mandating a life sentence upon a third qualifying felony, the legislature has effectively ceded proportionality to a rigid, arithmetic formula. The question is whether this formula can survive scrutiny under the Mixed Theory.

**II. The Retributive Challenge: Proportionality and Desert**

Retribution demands that punishment be proportionate to the *moral blameworthiness* of the offender and the *harm* of the offense. This is not a matter of simple lex talionis; it requires a nuanced assessment of culpability. A third strike for a non-violent property crime—say, stealing a pair of socks from a department store—presents a palpable tension. The offender’s blameworthiness, even with a prior record, does not escalate geometrically. The principle of proportionality, embedded in the Eighth Amendment’s Cruel and Unusual Punishment Clause (*Solem v. Helm*, 463 U.S. 277 (1983)), suggests that a life sentence for a minor property crime is grossly disproportionate.

Some courts, however, take Path A: they defer to legislative judgment, holding that recidivism itself enhances blameworthiness. The argument is that the offender, by persisting in criminal conduct despite prior punishment, demonstrates a heightened contempt for the law, thus warranting escalated desert. *See, e.g., Rummel v. Estelle*, 445 U.S. 263 (1980) (upholding life sentence for three non-violent felonies totaling $229.11). Other courts take Path B: they insist on a categorical proportionality review, examining whether the crime of conviction is so minor that the sentence “shocks the conscience.” *See, e.g., Solem*, 463 U.S. at 290-92 (requiring comparison of the gravity of the offense to the severity of the penalty). The doctrinal incoherence is manifest: the Supreme Court has never clearly resolved which path governs.

Under the Mixed Theory, Path B is more defensible. Retribution is not merely a tally of prior convictions; it is a moral judgment. A life sentence for a non-violent third strike may satisfy a crude notion of “just deserts” if one believes the offender has forfeited his claim to liberty. But the more refined retributivist position—associated with Kant and Hegel—insists that punishment must respect the offender’s dignity as a rational agent. Imposing a life sentence for a trivial theft treats the offender as a mere vector of risk, not a moral agent whose specific act must be condemned proportionally.

**III. The Utilitarian Challenge: Deterrence, Incapacitation, and Cost**

The utilitarian pillar of the Mixed Theory requires that punishment produce a net social benefit. Three Strikes laws are often defended on incapacitation grounds: locking away repeat offenders for life prevents future crimes. This has intuitive appeal, but the empirical evidence is equivocal. Because many offenders “age out” of crime by their forties, a life sentence imposed on a 35-year-old third striker may incapacitate them for decades during which their criminal propensity has naturally declined. The marginal deterrent effect is also questionable. A rational offender facing a potential life sentence for a third strike may be *over-deterred* from minor crimes but *under-deterred* from violent ones, since the penalty cannot escalate further. This is the “last period problem” familiar from game theory.

Furthermore, the fiscal cost is staggering. Incarcerating an elderly prisoner costs tens of thousands of dollars annually, funds that could be directed toward rehabilitation programs or crime prevention. The Mixed Theory, with its utilitarian component, cannot ignore this. If the state is spending resources to achieve negligible marginal deterrence and incapacitation of low-risk offenders, the punishment fails the utility calculus.

**IV. The Principle of Legality and Notice**

Finally, the Principle of Legality demands that citizens receive fair warning of the consequences of their actions. Three Strikes laws, on their face, satisfy this: the statute announces the penalty. But the *application* often raises concerns. Many Three Strikes statutes give prosecutors enormous discretion to charge or not charge prior strikes, and to choose which felony to file. This discretion can lead to arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement, undermining the rule of law. The Mixed Theory, which values consistency and predictability, must condemn such arbitrariness.

**CONCLUSION**

The Mixed Theory of Punishment cannot comfortably accommodate mandatory Three Strikes sentences for non-violent third felonies. The retributive principle of proportionality is violated when a life sentence is imposed for a minor property crime, and the utilitarian calculus is undermined by the high cost and low marginal benefit of incarcerating aging, low-risk offenders. A more defensible approach would retain Three Strikes as a *presumptive* sentencing enhancement, but allow judicial discretion to depart downward when the third strike is non-violent and the offender’s criminal history does not suggest a high risk of future harm. This would honor the Mixed Theory’s demand that punishment be both deserved and useful, rather than a mechanical exercise in legislative vengeance.

Analyze the potential criminal liability of Dwayne, Marcus, and Elena under the Model Penal Code and common law for the death of the armored car guard during a botched robbery. The central issues are: (1) whether the homicide constitutes murder or manslaughter; (2) the applicability of felony murder; (3) accomplice liability; and (4) the availability of defenses, particularly renunciation. The facts present a classic case of a plan gone awry, with a tragic death that may or may not have been intended.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. Dwayne’s Liability**

Dwayne is the principal actor. He entered the bank, brandished a firearm, and during the course of the robbery, the guard was shot and killed. The critical factual ambiguity is whether Dwayne intentionally shot the guard, or whether the guard was killed accidentally during a struggle or by a ricochet.

*Path A: Intentional Killing.* If Dwayne intentionally shot the guard, he is liable for murder. Under MPC § 210.2(1)(a), murder is committed when the actor “purposely or knowingly” causes death. Dwayne’s act of pointing a firearm at the guard and pulling the trigger would satisfy the “knowingly” requirement if he was aware that death was practically certain to result. At common law, this would be murder with malice aforethought, either express (intent to kill) or implied (intent to cause grievous bodily harm). The death penalty or life imprisonment would be potential sentences, depending on jurisdiction.

*Path B: Accidental Death During Felony.* If the guard was killed accidentally—for example, Dwayne’s gun discharged during a struggle—the analysis shifts to felony murder. At common law, any death caused during the commission of a dangerous felony (robbery is a classic predicate) is murder, regardless of intent. MPC § 210.2(1)(b) adopts a more limited version: the actor is guilty of murder if he “is engaged in the commission of, or an attempt to commit, or flight after committing or attempting to commit robbery” and “in the course of such crime or flight, he … causes the death of another.” However, the MPC adds a crucial defense: the actor must be shown to have acted with “recklessness” or “extreme indifference to the value of human life.” If the death was purely accidental and Dwayne took reasonable precautions (e.g., he did not point the gun at anyone), a court might find insufficient recklessness.

Under the common law felony murder rule, Dwayne would almost certainly be liable for murder, as the death occurred during the commission of a robbery. The rule is strict; no mens rea regarding the death is required. This is a doctrinal relic that the MPC sought to reform, but many states retain it.

**II. Marcus’s Liability**

Marcus is the getaway driver. He did not enter the bank, nor did he shoot anyone. His liability hinges on accomplice liability. Under MPC § 2.06, a person is an accomplice if he “aids” or “agrees or attempts to aid” another in planning or committing an offense, and he acts with the “purpose of promoting or facilitating the commission of the offense.”

*Path A: Accomplice to Robbery Only.* If Marcus’s role was limited to driving the getaway car, he is an accomplice to the robbery. The question is whether he is also an accomplice to the homicide. Under the common law “natural and probable consequences” doctrine, an accomplice is liable for all crimes that are the natural and probable consequence of the planned offense. If a death during an armed robbery is foreseeable, Marcus could be liable for murder as an accomplice. *See, e.g., People v. Luparello*, 187 Cal. App. 3d 410 (1986).

*Path B: MPC’s Purpose Requirement.* The MPC rejects the natural and probable consequences doctrine. Under MPC § 2.06(3), an accomplice must have the *purpose* to promote or facilitate the specific offense. Marcus’s purpose was to facilitate the robbery, not the homicide. Therefore, under the MPC, Marcus would be liable for robbery, but not for murder, unless the state can prove he acted with “recklessness” regarding the death. If Marcus knew Dwayne was armed and likely to use the weapon, a court might find that Marcus’s conduct manifested extreme indifference to human life, satisfying the MPC’s murder provision. However, this is a stretch; mere knowledge of a weapon does not equate to purpose to kill.

**III. Elena’s Liability and the Renunciation Defense**

Elena initially agreed to participate as a lookout but withdrew before the robbery occurred. Her liability depends on whether her withdrawal constitutes a valid defense.

Under MPC § 2.06(6)(c), an accomplice may avoid liability if she “terminates her complicity” prior to the commission of the offense and either (i) “neutralizes” her prior assistance, or (ii) “gives timely warning to the law enforcement authorities” or “otherwise makes proper effort to prevent the commission of the offense.” Elena’s act of calling Marcus and saying “I’m out” is insufficient under the MPC. She did not neutralize her assistance (she had already agreed to be a lookout, and her agreement may have emboldened the others). She did not call the police. Therefore, she likely remains liable as an accomplice to the robbery.

At common law, the defense of renunciation is even more limited. An accomplice must take “affirmative action” to prevent the crime, such as physically intervening or summoning authorities. A mere change of heart is not enough. *See, e.g., People v. Brown*, 26 N.Y.2d 88 (1970).

**IV. The Felony Murder Rule and the “In Furthermore” Requirement**

A final nuance: under the MPC, the death must occur “in the course of” the robbery. If the guard was killed during the robbery, this is satisfied. However, if the guard was killed after the robbery was complete and during flight, the MPC’s language covers flight as well. The common law is split. Some courts hold that the felony murder rule extends to deaths occurring during flight from the scene, while others require the death to occur during the commission of the felony itself.

**CONCLUSION**

Dwayne is likely liable for murder, either as an intentional killing or under the felony murder rule. Marcus, under the common law, may be liable for murder as an accomplice under the natural and probable consequences doctrine, but under the MPC, he is likely liable only for robbery. Elena’s withdrawal was insufficient to constitute renunciation; she remains liable as an accomplice to the robbery, but not to the homicide, absent proof of purpose or extreme recklessness. The case illustrates the profound doctrinal incoherence that persists in American homicide law, particularly the tension between the MPC’s mens rea requirements and the common law’s strict liability approach to felony murder.
