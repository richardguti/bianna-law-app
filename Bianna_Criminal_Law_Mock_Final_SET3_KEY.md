**Question 1**

The primary distinction between the M'Naghten cognitive test and the Durham "product" test is that:

(A) M'Naghten requires a total deprivation of reason, while Durham requires only a partial impairment.
(B) M'Naghten focuses on the defendant's knowledge of right and wrong, while Durham focuses on whether the act was the product of mental disease or defect.
(C) M'Naghten is a federal standard, while Durham is used exclusively in state courts.
(D) M'Naghten requires expert testimony, while Durham relies solely on lay witness observations.
(E) M'Naghten applies only to felony offenses, while Durham applies to misdemeanors.

**Correct Answer: (B)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Incorrect.** M'Naghten does require a "defect of reason" such that the defendant did not know the nature/quality of the act or that it was wrong. This is a high threshold, but the phrase "total deprivation" overstates it. Durham requires that the act be "the product of mental disease or defect," which is broader but not accurately described as requiring only "partial impairment." The key distinction is not about degree of impairment.
- **(B) Correct.** This is the fundamental difference. M'Naghten is a cognitive test: did the defendant know what they were doing or know it was wrong? Durham is a causal test: was the act caused by (the product of) a mental disease or defect? This distinction is the core of the policy debate you will address in Essay 1.
- **(C) Incorrect.** Neither test is exclusively federal or state. M'Naghten is the majority rule in US states. Durham was adopted by the DC Circuit (federal) and a few states but has been largely abandoned. The Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984 created a federal standard similar to M'Naghten but with a volitional prong removed.
- **(D) Incorrect.** Both tests typically rely on expert psychiatric testimony. Durham arguably *increases* the reliance on experts because they must opine on the causal link ("product"), which critics argued invaded the province of the jury.
- **(E) Incorrect.** Neither test is limited by the type of offense. Insanity is an affirmative defense applicable to any crime requiring *mens rea*.

**Question 2**

Under the Model Penal Code's "control" or "volitional" prong, a defendant may be found not guilty by reason of insanity if, as a result of mental disease or defect, he:

(A) Did not know the nature and quality of his act.
(B) Did not know his act was wrong.
(C) Lacked substantial capacity to appreciate the criminality of his conduct.
(D) Lacked substantial capacity to conform his conduct to the requirements of the law.
(E) Was unable to control his temper.

**Correct Answer: (D)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Incorrect.** This describes the first prong of M'Naghten (cognitive).
- **(B) Incorrect.** This describes the second prong of M'Naghten (cognitive/moral).
- **(C) Incorrect.** This is the MPC's *cognitive* prong (appreciation of criminality), which is broader than M'Naghten's "knowledge" but still cognitive.
- **(D) Correct.** This is the MPC's *control* or *volitional* prong. It addresses the defendant's capacity to *control* their behavior, not just their knowledge. It asks: even if they knew it was wrong, could they stop themselves? This is the "policeman at the elbow" test.
- **(E) Incorrect.** "Unable to control his temper" is not a recognized legal standard for insanity. The MPC requires a "lack of substantial capacity" resulting from a "mental disease or defect." Mere lack of emotional control, absent a diagnosed condition causing a volitional impairment, does not satisfy the control prong.

**Question 3**

In a jurisdiction following the M'Naghten test, which of the following defendants would most likely be found NOT guilty by reason of insanity?

(A) Darla, who suffers from depression and shoplifts expensive handbags because she believes they will make her happy.
(B) Edward, who has a severe intellectual disability and cannot understand the concept of "property" or that taking someone else's property is wrong.
(C) Frank, who has antisocial personality disorder and robs a bank because he wants money and does not care about the law.
(D) Gina, who has schizophrenia and kills her neighbor because she genuinely believes the neighbor is a demon who will destroy the world if not stopped.
(E) Hank, who is intoxicated on methamphetamine and hallucinates that he is fighting monsters when he attacks a police officer.

**Correct Answer: (D)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Incorrect.** Depression, while a serious mental illness, does not typically negate knowledge of right and wrong under M'Naghten. Darla knows stealing is wrong; she does it because she wants the bags. This is a motive, not a cognitive defect.
- **(B) Incorrect.** This is a close call. M'Naghten requires a "defect of reason from disease of the mind." Intellectual disability may qualify as a "disease of the mind." However, the question asks who is *most likely* to be found NGRI. Edward's inability to understand the concept of property might negate *mens rea* (he lacks the intent to steal) rather than serving as an insanity defense. He might be found incompetent or lacking criminal intent, not insane. Option (D) is a clearer case of the classic M'Naghten delusion.
- **(C) Incorrect.** Antisocial personality disorder is often explicitly excluded from the definition of "mental disease or defect" for insanity purposes. It is a personality disorder, not a psychotic condition. Frank knows right from wrong; he simply chooses to disregard it.
- **(D) Correct.** This is the classic M'Naghten scenario. Gina suffers from a psychotic disorder (schizophrenia) and has a delusion that directly negates her knowledge of the wrongfulness of her act. If she truly believes the neighbor is a demon, she may not know that killing the neighbor is morally or legally wrong. She may believe she is saving the world.
- **(E) Incorrect.** Voluntary intoxication is generally not a defense to criminal conduct, and it is not a "mental disease or defect" for insanity purposes. Hank may have a diminished capacity defense in some jurisdictions, but not an insanity defense. Involuntary intoxication might be different, but that is not the fact pattern.

**Question 4**

The "irresistible impulse" test is best described as:

(A) A test that focuses on whether the defendant knew the act was wrong.
(B) A test that focuses on whether the defendant's act was the product of a mental disease.
(C) A volitional test that asks whether the defendant was unable to control their conduct.
(D) A test that requires the defendant to have planned the crime carefully.
(E) A test that is identical to the Durham rule.

**Correct Answer: (C)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Incorrect.** This describes the M'Naghten cognitive test.
- **(B) Incorrect.** This describes the Durham "product" test.
- **(C) Correct.** The irresistible impulse test is a historical volitional test. It asks whether the defendant, knowing the act was wrong, was nonetheless compelled to do it by an irresistible impulse—a force they could not resist. It is a narrower, more sudden version of the MPC's "lack of substantial capacity to conform conduct."
- **(D) Incorrect.** Planning suggests deliberation and control, which is the opposite of an irresistible impulse. The test is about loss of control, not premeditation.
- **(E) Incorrect.** The Durham test is a causal test (product of mental disease), while the irresistible impulse test is a volitional test (inability to control). They are distinct.

**Question 5**

Which of the following is a primary criticism of the Durham "product" test?

(A) It is too narrow and excludes many mentally ill defendants.
(B) It is too vague and gives juries and experts insufficient guidance, leading to inconsistent verdicts.
(C) It places too much emphasis on the defendant's cognitive abilities.
(D) It requires the defendant to prove insanity beyond a reasonable doubt.
(E) It only applies to property crimes.

**Correct Answer: (B)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Incorrect.** Durham is criticized for being *too broad*, not too narrow. By asking only if the act was the "product" of mental disease, it arguably allows any criminal act by a person with a mental illness to be excused.
- **(B) Correct.** This is the famous criticism by Judge David Bazelon (who authored Durham) himself. The term "product" is vague. It invites experts to testify about ultimate legal conclusions ("the act was a product of the disease") without clear standards. This leads to a "battle of the experts" and inconsistent jury verdicts.
- **(C) Incorrect.** Durham de-emphasizes cognitive abilities. It does not require a showing of cognitive defect; it only requires a causal link.
- **(D) Incorrect.** The burden of proof is a separate issue. Most jurisdictions require the defendant to prove insanity by a preponderance of the evidence. Some require the prosecution to disprove it beyond a reasonable doubt. This is not a specific criticism of the Durham test itself.
- **(E) Incorrect.** Durham applies to all crimes.

**Question 6**

Under the Model Penal Code test for insanity, a defendant must prove that, as a result of mental disease or defect, he lacked substantial capacity to:

(A) Know the nature and quality of his act.
(B) Know that his act was wrong.
(C) Appreciate the criminality of his conduct OR to conform his conduct to the requirements of the law.
(D) Control his emotions and impulses.
(E) Form the specific intent required for the crime.

**Correct Answer: (C)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Incorrect.** This is part of the M'Naghten test. The MPC uses "appreciate" which is broader than "know."
- **(B) Incorrect.** This is part of the M'Naghten test. The MPC combines this with the nature/quality prong into "appreciate the criminality of his conduct."
- **(C) Correct.** This is the exact language of the MPC § 4.01. It combines a cognitive prong (appreciate criminality) and a volitional prong (conform conduct) with the conjunction "OR," meaning either is sufficient. The "substantial capacity" language makes it broader than M'Naghten or the irresistible impulse test.
- **(D) Incorrect.** The MPC does not use the phrase "control his emotions." The volitional prong is about conforming *conduct* to the law, not controlling internal emotional states.
- **(E) Incorrect.** This describes a "diminished capacity" or "partial responsibility" defense, which is different from an insanity defense. Diminished capacity negates a specific *mens rea* element, while insanity is a complete excuse.

**Question 7**

The case of *United States v. Hinckley* is most significant for which of the following reasons?

(A) It established the M'Naghten test as the sole federal standard.
(B) It led to the adoption of the Durham test in federal courts.
(C) It prompted Congress to pass the Insanity Defense Reform Act, which eliminated the volitional prong from the federal insanity defense.
(D) It was the first case to recognize the "guilty but mentally ill" verdict.
(E) It abolished the insanity defense entirely in federal courts.

**Correct Answer: (C)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Incorrect.** *Hinckley* was tried under the MPC test (which was the DC Circuit standard at the time). The public outcry over his acquittal led to reform, not the adoption of M'Naghten.
- **(B) Incorrect.** Durham was already abandoned in the DC Circuit before *Hinckley*. The DC Circuit used the MPC test.
- **(C) Correct.** John Hinckley Jr. was found NGRI for the attempted assassination of President Reagan. The public was outraged. In response, Congress passed the Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984, which created a federal insanity defense based on a cognitive test only ("unable to appreciate the nature and quality or the wrongfulness of his acts") and explicitly eliminated the volitional prong.
- **(D) Incorrect.** The "guilty but mentally ill" (GBMI) verdict was adopted by some states in response to the same public pressure, but it was not a direct result of the *Hinckley* case itself, nor was it established by the case. It was a legislative creation.
- **(E) Incorrect.** The insanity defense was not abolished; it was narrowed.

**Question 8**

A defendant who is found "Guilty but Mentally Ill" (GBMI) is:

(B) Sentenced to prison but must receive mental health treatment while incarcerated.

(D) Eligible for immediate release upon being declared sane.

**Correct Answer: (B)**

- **(A) Incorrect.** This describes an NGRI acquittee, who is typically committed to a mental hospital. A GBMI defendant is sentenced to prison.
- **(B) Correct.** The GBMI verdict is a compromise. It acknowledges the defendant's mental illness but holds them criminally responsible. The defendant is sentenced to prison (or other criminal penalty) but the court orders that they receive appropriate mental health treatment while in custody.
- **(C) Incorrect.** This describes an NGRI verdict. GBMI defendants are found responsible and guilty.
- **(D) Incorrect.** GBMI defendants serve their criminal sentence. They are not released upon being declared sane; they must serve their term.
- **(E) Incorrect.** GBMI is a criminal verdict, not a civil commitment. The defendant is sentenced within the criminal justice system.

**Question 9**

The "appreciation" standard in the MPC's cognitive prong differs from M'Naghten's "knowledge" standard in that:

(A) Appreciation is a purely intellectual understanding, while knowledge is emotional.
(B) Appreciation requires a deeper, more affective understanding of the consequences of one's actions, while knowledge is a more superficial awareness.
(C) Appreciation is easier for the prosecution to disprove.
(D) Appreciation applies only to the nature of the act, not its wrongfulness.
(E) There is no meaningful difference between the two terms.

**Correct Answer: (B)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Incorrect.** It is the opposite. "Knowledge" is the purely intellectual awareness. "Appreciation" is broader and includes an emotional or affective component.
- **(B) Correct.** The drafters of the MPC intended "appreciate" to be broader than "know." A defendant might know, in a technical sense, that killing is wrong, but due to a delusion, they may not *appreciate* or fully grasp the significance or consequence of their act. For example, a schizophrenic defendant who kills a stranger because God commanded it might know that killing is generally illegal, but due to his delusion, he does not appreciate the criminality of *his* specific act.
- **(C) Incorrect.** It is harder for the prosecution to disprove because it is a broader, more subjective standard.
- **(D) Incorrect.** The MPC's cognitive prong applies to "appreciate the criminality [wrongfulness] of his conduct." It covers both the nature and the wrongfulness.
- **(E) Incorrect.** The distinction is intentional and significant, as explained above.

**Question 10**

Which of the following is NOT a recognized criticism of retaining the volitional (control) prong in the insanity defense?

(A) It is impossible to reliably distinguish between a "cannot conform" and a "will not conform" to the law.
(B) It relies too heavily on the subjective opinion of psychiatric experts.
(C) It is too narrow and fails to excuse defendants who act under an irresistible impulse.
(D) It risks allowing dangerous individuals to be found NGRI and released prematurely.
(E) It is based on a flawed understanding of human behavior and free will.

**Correct Answer: (C)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Incorrect.** This is a major criticism. How can a jury or expert determine if a defendant *could not* control themselves or simply *did not* want to? This is the central problem of the volitional prong.
- **(B) Incorrect.** This is a related criticism. Because the standard is vague, the trial becomes a "battle of the experts" where psychiatrists give conflicting opinions on the defendant's capacity for control.
- **(C) Correct.** This is the *opposite* of a criticism. The volitional prong is criticized for being *too broad*, potentially excusing too many defendants. The "irresistible impulse" test (a narrower volitional test) was criticized for being too narrow. The MPC's "substantial capacity" test was designed to be broader.
- **(D) Incorrect.** This is a common policy criticism. If a defendant is found NGRI because they could not control their violent impulses, they are a danger to society. The public fears they will be released from commitment too soon.
- **(E) Incorrect.** This is a philosophical criticism. The volitional prong assumes that some behavior is determined by mental illness and is not a product of free will. Critics argue this is a metaphysical question that the law cannot answer.

**Question 11**

In the context of the insanity defense, "diminished capacity" (or "partial responsibility") refers to:

(A) A complete defense that results in an acquittal.
(B) A defense that allows the defendant to be found guilty of a lesser offense because they lacked the specific intent required for the greater offense due to mental illness.
(C) A defense that requires the defendant to be treated in a mental hospital instead of prison.
(D) A verdict that is the same as "guilty but mentally ill."
(E) A defense that is available in all US jurisdictions.

**Correct Answer: (B)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Incorrect.** Diminished capacity is a *partial* defense, not a complete defense. It reduces the level of culpability but does not result in acquittal.
- **(B) Correct.** Diminished capacity is a doctrine (used in a minority of states) that allows a defendant to introduce evidence of mental illness (short of insanity) to negate the *mens rea* (specifically, specific intent) of a crime. For example, a defendant with schizophrenia might be found guilty of manslaughter instead of first-degree murder because his delusions prevented him from forming the premeditation and deliberation required for first-degree murder.
- **(C) Incorrect.** This describes the disposition after an NGRI verdict, not the diminished capacity defense itself.
- **(D) Incorrect.** GBMI is a verdict that acknowledges mental illness but holds the defendant fully responsible. Diminished capacity reduces the degree of the offense.
- **(E) Incorrect.** Diminished capacity is a controversial doctrine and is rejected by many jurisdictions (including the federal system and most states). It is not universally available.

**Question 12**

The primary justification for the "guilty but mentally ill" (GBMI) verdict was to:

(A) Provide better treatment for mentally ill offenders than the insanity defense.
(B) Reduce the number of defendants found not guilty by reason of insanity.
(C) Eliminate the need for mental health treatment in prisons.
(D) Allow defendants to choose their own punishment.
(E) Abolish the insanity defense entirely.

**Correct Answer: (B)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Incorrect.** GBMI does not guarantee better treatment. In fact, critics argue it is a "label without a remedy" because prisons are often ill-equipped to provide adequate mental health treatment.
- **(B) Correct.** The GBMI verdict was created in response to public outrage over NGRI acquittals (like *Hinckley*). It gives juries a "middle ground" option. Jurors who believe a defendant is mentally ill but do not want to set them free (as they believe an NGRI verdict would do) can choose GBMI. This reduces the number of NGRI verdicts.
- **(C) Incorrect.** GBMI requires that treatment be provided, not that it be eliminated.
- **(D) Incorrect.** Defendants do not choose their verdict.
- **(E) Incorrect.** GBMI is an *alternative* to the insanity defense, not its abolition. The insanity defense remains available.

### SECTION II: ESSAY QUESTIONS

#### ESSAY 1: POLICY ESSAY – THE CONTROL PRONG

**Question:** The Model Penal Code's insanity defense includes both a cognitive prong (lacks substantial capacity to appreciate the criminality of his conduct) and a volitional/control prong (lacks substantial capacity to conform his conduct to the requirements of the law). The federal Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984 eliminated the control prong. Which approach is preferable? Argue for one side, addressing the strengths and weaknesses of both positions.

**Model Answer (Approx. 900 words)**

The proper scope of the insanity defense has long vexed criminal law. The central debate concerns whether the defense should excuse only those whose cognitive faculties are so impaired that they cannot understand the wrongfulness of their actions, or whether it should also extend to those who, despite understanding that their actions are wrong, are driven to commit them by a mental illness that destroys their capacity for self-control. The Model Penal Code (MPC) embraces the latter view, incorporating a volitional or "control" prong. The federal Insanity Defense Reform Act (IDRA) of 1984, reacting to the public outcry over John Hinckley Jr.'s acquittal, rejected it. While the arguments on both sides are powerful, the preferable approach is the one adopted by the MPC: the insanity defense should include a control prong. To exclude it is to ignore a fundamental aspect of human agency and to create an arbitrary and unjust line between those who are excused and those who are not.

The case for the control prong rests on the bedrock principle of criminal law: *actus non facit reum nisi mens sit rea*—an act does not make one guilty unless the mind is guilty. Culpability requires not only a cognitive awareness of wrongdoing but also the capacity to choose otherwise. A person who is compelled by an internal force they cannot resist is, in a meaningful sense, not a moral agent. They are not exercising free will; they are a vessel for their illness. To punish them is to punish a symptom, not a choice. The MPC's control prong, requiring a "lack of substantial capacity to conform conduct to the requirements of the law," captures this reality. It recognizes that a person with severe psychosis, who hears commanding voices that threaten death if they do not comply, may know that killing is illegal but be utterly powerless to resist. To hold such a person fully responsible is to demand a standard of behavior they are, by dint of their disease, incapable of meeting. This is both unjust and inconsistent with the retributive goal of punishment, which demands that the punishment be proportionate to the actor's moral blameworthiness.

Furthermore, the cognitive prong alone creates an arbitrary distinction. Consider two defendants, both suffering from the same delusional disorder. Defendant A believes his victim is a demon who will destroy the world. He kills the victim, believing it is a morally necessary act. Under the cognitive prong, he may be excused because he did not know his act was wrong. Defendant B also believes his victim is a demon, but he knows that killing is legally and morally wrong. However, the demon is commanding him to kill, and the terror of disobedience is so overwhelming that he cannot resist. Under a purely cognitive test, Defendant B is fully responsible. This distinction is morally indefensible. Both defendants are equally driven by their illness. The only difference is the specific content of their delusion. The law should not turn on such a lottery of symptomatology. The control prong corrects this arbitrariness by focusing on the defendant's functional capacity for choice, not just the content of their beliefs.

However, the arguments against the control prong are formidable and cannot be dismissed. The most powerful criticism is epistemological: it is impossible to reliably distinguish between a "cannot conform" and a "will not conform." As Judge Bazelon, a proponent of broader insanity tests, himself noted, the line between an irresistible impulse and an impulse that was simply not resisted is a matter of metaphysical speculation, not scientific fact. Psychiatric experts cannot provide a reliable, objective test for volitional impairment. This leads to a "battle of the experts" where juries are forced to make decisions based on conflicting, subjective opinions, resulting in inconsistent and arbitrary verdicts. The Hinckley verdict, where experts for the defense and prosecution painted wildly different pictures of the defendant's capacity for control, is a classic example. This lack of reliability undermines the legitimacy of the criminal justice system and fuels public cynicism.

A second, related criticism is that the control prong is too broad and risks excusing dangerous individuals who are simply bad actors. Antisocial personality disorder, for example, is characterized by a profound disregard for the rights of others and a seeming inability to conform to social norms. If the control prong is interpreted too loosely, it could be used to excuse recidivist criminals who simply lack the will to obey the law. This would erode personal responsibility and public safety. The IDRA's rejection of the control prong was a clear statement that the risk of false positives—excusing the undeserving—outweighs the risk of false negatives—punishing the truly blameless.

Despite these powerful objections, the MPC approach remains preferable. The epistemological problem, while real, is not unique to the control prong. The cognitive prong also requires juries to make difficult, subjective judgments about what a defendant "knew" or "appreciated," which is equally a matter of inference from behavior and expert testimony. The difficulty of the task does not justify abandoning the principle. Furthermore, the risk of overbreadth can be managed through careful definition. The MPC's requirement of a "mental disease or defect" that causes a "lack of *substantial* capacity" provides a meaningful gatekeeping function. It excludes mere personality disorders and momentary lapses of will. It requires a severe, diagnosed condition that fundamentally undermines the defendant's capacity for choice. The federal system's approach, by contrast, creates a bright-line rule that is administratively simple but morally crude. It sacrifices justice for efficiency.

In conclusion, while the control prong presents significant practical challenges, it is a necessary component of a just insanity defense. A system that punishes individuals who lacked the capacity to control their actions punishes the morally blameless. The difficulty of drawing the line between "cannot" and "will not" is a challenge the law must embrace, not a reason to abandon the inquiry. The MPC's approach, with its careful language and emphasis on substantial incapacity, offers the best framework for navigating this difficult terrain. It is preferable to the IDRA's approach, which prioritizes administrative convenience and public fear over the fundamental principle that punishment must be reserved for the morally culpable.

#### ESSAY 2: FACT PATTERN ANALYSIS – DOMESTIC TRAGEDY

**Question:** Analyze the potential criminal liability and defenses of Eleanor, Leo, and Dr. Alistair based on the fact pattern provided. For each individual, identify the most serious crime(s) they may have committed, the elements of those crimes, and any viable defenses (including but not limited to insanity, self-defense, defense of others, necessity, duress, and intoxication). Assume the jurisdiction follows the Model Penal Code for both substantive crimes and the insanity defense.

**Fact Pattern (Domestic Tragedy):**

Eleanor and Richard have been married for 15 years. Richard is a successful but controlling and emotionally abusive husband. For the past year, Eleanor has been having an affair with Leo, a charismatic but manipulative man. Leo has been subtly encouraging Eleanor to believe that Richard is a threat to her life, exaggerating Richard's controlling behavior and suggesting Richard has a hidden violent past.

Eleanor's mental state deteriorates. She begins seeing a psychiatrist, Dr. Alistair, for severe anxiety and depression. Dr. Alistair, overworked and distracted, prescribes a powerful sedative and an antidepressant. He fails to review her chart, which notes a history of adverse reactions to sedatives. The combination of the drugs triggers a psychotic episode in Eleanor. She becomes paranoid, believing Richard is a government agent sent to kill her.

One evening, during a psychotic delusion, Eleanor sees Richard reach for a TV remote. She genuinely believes he is reaching for a weapon to kill her. She grabs a fireplace poker and strikes him repeatedly, killing him.

Leo, unaware of the killing, arrives at the house shortly after. He finds Eleanor standing over Richard's body, covered in blood, muttering about "government agents." Leo, seeing an opportunity, tells Eleanor, "You did the right thing. He was going to kill you. We need to get rid of the body." He helps her clean the scene and wrap the body in a tarp. He then drives the body to a remote location and dumps it.

Dr. Alistair, the next day, hears a news report about Richard's disappearance. He realizes the potential connection to the drugs he prescribed. Panicked about a malpractice suit and losing his license, he deletes Eleanor's electronic medical records and alters his paper notes to show he warned her about the risks of the sedative.

**Model Answer**

This fact pattern presents a complex web of criminal liability, involving issues of insanity, self-defense, accomplice liability, and obstruction of justice. The analysis will proceed for each actor under the Model Penal Code (MPC).

**I. ELEANOR**

**A. Most Serious Crime: Murder (Criminal Homicide)**

Under the MPC § 210.1-210.2, Criminal Homicise is committed when a person "purposely, knowingly, recklessly or negligently causes the death of another human being." Murder is a form of criminal homicide committed "purposely or knowingly" or "recklessly under circumstances manifesting extreme indifference to the value of human life."

Eleanor purposely or knowingly caused the death of Richard by striking him repeatedly with a fireplace poker. The *actus reus* is the striking. The *mens rea* is purposeful or knowing: she intended to kill him or knew her actions were practically certain to cause his death. The prima facie case for murder is easily established.

**B. Defenses**

**1. Insanity (MPC § 4.01)**

This is Eleanor's strongest defense. The MPC test requires that "at the time of such conduct as a result of mental disease or defect [the defendant] lacks substantial capacity either to appreciate the criminality [wrongfulness] of his conduct or to conform his conduct to the requirements of the law."

- **Mental Disease or Defect:** Eleanor was suffering from a "psychotic episode" triggered by a drug interaction. The MPC's definition of "mental disease or defect" excludes "an abnormality manifested only by repeated criminal or otherwise antisocial conduct." A drug-induced psychosis, while caused by an external substance, is a temporary but genuine mental disease. The key question is whether it was *involuntary* intoxication (see below).
- **Cognitive Prong (Appreciate Criminality):** Eleanor believed Richard was a "government agent sent to kill her." She believed she was acting in self-defense against an imminent lethal threat. Under this delusion, she may not have appreciated that her act was *wrongful*. She may have believed it was a justified act of self-preservation. This is a classic M'Naghten-style delusion, and under the MPC's broader "appreciation" standard, she likely lacked substantial capacity to appreciate the criminality of her conduct.
- **Volitional Prong (Conform Conduct):** Even if she knew, in some abstract sense, that killing is wrong, her psychotic state likely destroyed her capacity to conform her conduct to the law. She was acting on a delusional imperative.

**Conclusion on Insanity:** Eleanor has a strong claim for NGRI. The psychosis was severe, directly caused her actions, and negated her moral culpability.

**2. Self-Defense (MPC § 3.04)**

The MPC justifies the use of deadly force if the actor "believes that such force is immediately necessary for the purpose of protecting himself against the use of unlawful force by such other person on the present occasion."

- **Subjective Belief:** Eleanor genuinely believed Richard was reaching for a weapon to kill her. This belief was sincere.
- **Objective Reasonableness:** The MPC's self-defense is partially subjective. The force must be "immediately necessary," which has an objective component. However, the MPC § 3.09(2) addresses the use of force based on a mistaken belief. If the belief is unreasonable, the actor may be guilty of a reckless or negligent homicide. Here, Eleanor's belief was objectively unreasonable. A reasonable person would not believe a TV remote is a weapon. However, her unreasonableness was caused by her psychosis.
- **Interaction with Insanity:** The insanity defense is more appropriate here. Self-defense requires a belief in the necessity of force. Her belief was delusional, not just mistaken. The law of self-defense is not designed to excuse actions based on psychotic delusions. Insanity is the proper vehicle.

**3. Involuntary Intoxication (MPC § 2.08)**

The MPC distinguishes between voluntary and involuntary intoxication. Involuntary intoxication is a defense if it "negatives an element of the offense" or if it causes a condition that "is akin to insanity."

- **Was it Involuntary?** Eleanor was prescribed the medication by her doctor. She did not knowingly take an intoxicating substance for an improper purpose. The psychosis was an *unforeseen side effect* of a prescribed medication. This is a classic case of involuntary intoxication.
- **Effect:** The MPC § 2.08(4) states that if intoxication is involuntary, it is a defense if it negates *mens rea* or if the actor, as a result, is "not guilty of the offense." More importantly, § 2.08(3) provides that if the intoxication causes a mental condition that "meets the test for mental disease or defect," the actor is entitled to the insanity defense.
- **Conclusion:** Eleanor's drug-induced psychosis qualifies as a "mental disease or defect" under the MPC's insanity test. Therefore, the insanity defense is available to her via the involuntary intoxication doctrine. This is a powerful alternative or supplement to her direct insanity claim.

**C. Ultimate Liability:** Eleanor is most likely **Not Guilty by Reason of Insanity**. Her actions were the direct product of a severe, involuntary psychotic episode that destroyed her capacity to appreciate the wrongfulness of her conduct.

**II. LEO**

**A. Most Serious Crime: Accomplice to Murder / Hindering Apprehension**

Leo did not participate in the killing. However, his actions after the fact make him liable.

**1. Accomplice Liability (MPC § 2.06)**

A person is an accomplice if, "with the purpose of promoting or facilitating the commission of the offense," he "aids or agrees or attempts to aid such other person in planning or committing it." This requires *mens rea* as to his own conduct (purpose to aid) and *mens rea* as to the principal's conduct (purpose that the offense be committed).

- **Analysis:** Leo did not aid Eleanor in the *commission* of the murder. He arrived after the fact. He did not plan it, encourage it at the time, or provide the weapon. Therefore, he is not an accomplice to the murder itself. His statement, "You did the right thing," is after-the-fact approval, not encouragement during the act.

**2. Hindering Apprehension or Prosecution (MPC § 242.3)**

This is the most appropriate charge. The MPC makes it a crime to "hinder the apprehension, prosecution, conviction, or punishment of another for a crime" by:
(a) harboring or concealing the person; or
(b) warning the person of impending discovery or apprehension; or
(c) providing the person with money, transportation, or other means to avoid discovery; or
(d) concealing or destroying evidence of the crime.

Leo helped Eleanor clean the scene (concealing evidence), wrapped the body (concealing evidence), and drove the body to a remote location to dump it (concealing evidence and destroying evidence). He acted with the purpose of hindering her apprehension for the murder. This is a felony.

**3. Accessory After the Fact (Common Law)**

The common law equivalent is "accessory after the fact." Leo's actions fit this perfectly.

**B. Defenses**

**1. Duress (MPC § 2.09)**

Duress is a defense if the actor was "coerced to do so by the use of, or a threat to use, unlawful force against his person or the person of another, which a person of reasonable firmness in his situation would have been unable to resist."
