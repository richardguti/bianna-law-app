### ESSAY 1: POLICY — THE INSANITY DEFENSE: CONTROL PRONG VERSUS COGNITIVE PRONG

**GOAL**

This essay interrogates the doctrinal coherence and normative justifications for the two competing formulations of the insanity defense: the cognitive prong, which excuses a defendant who lacks the capacity to understand the nature or wrongfulness of her conduct, and the control (or volitional) prong, which excuses a defendant who, despite cognitive awareness, is unable to conform her conduct to the requirements of the law. The central question is whether the control prong should be retained, excised, or reformed. I shall argue that while the cognitive prong aligns more neatly with retributive principles of culpability, the control prong is not merely a vestige of antiquated psychiatry but a necessary, albeit fraught, expression of the moral intuition that *can* implies *ought*—and that its abolition risks a palpable tension with the principle of legality.

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Cognitive Prong: The Jurisprudential Mainstream**

The cognitive prong, enshrined in the venerable M'Naghten Rule (1843) and adopted in various forms by a majority of American jurisdictions, focuses on the defendant's *knowledge* or *understanding*. Under M'Naghten, a defendant is excused if, at the time of the act, she labored under such a defect of reason from disease of the mind as not to know the nature and quality of the act, or if she did know it, that she did not know it was wrong. The Model Penal Code § 4.01(1) offers a more modern cognitive formulation, excusing a person who, as a result of mental disease or defect, lacks substantial capacity to appreciate the criminality of her conduct.

The cognitive prong finds its philosophical anchor in retributivism. For punishment to be just, the actor must be a *moral agent*—one who could grasp the law's commands and choose to obey. As Professor Herbert Packer argued, the criminal law presupposes a "free agent confronted with a choice between doing right and doing wrong." If mental illness so severs the defendant's cognitive connection to the law's normative message, she is not a proper subject for blame. The cognitive prong thus serves the principle of legality by ensuring that only those who could *know* the law's prohibition are subjected to its sanctions. This is a clean, if narrow, rule.

**II. The Control Prong: The Volitional Frontier**

The control prong, by contrast, addresses the defendant who *knows* the act is wrong but is, in the vivid clinical phrase, "driven" to commit it. The classic formulation is the "irresistible impulse" test, though the MPC § 4.01(1) more elegantly phrases it as lacking substantial capacity to *conform* conduct to the law. This prong acknowledges a psychological reality that the cognitive prong ignores: volitional impairment can be as disabling as cognitive impairment.

The control prong finds its philosophical support in a more nuanced, utilitarian-retributive hybrid. From a retributive perspective, the intuition is powerful: we do not blame the epileptic for the seizure, nor the sleepwalker for the somnambulant act. Why, then, should we blame the person whose volitional machinery is broken by psychosis? The actor is not *choosing* evil; she is *suffering* it. From a utilitarian standpoint, incapacitation and treatment, not punishment, are the appropriate societal responses to a person who cannot be deterred by the law's threats, for a threat cannot deter a volition that cannot conform.

**III. The Doctrinal Incoherence and the Case for Abolition**

Despite its moral appeal, the control prong has been subjected to withering criticism, leading to its excision from federal law following *United States v. Lyons* (1984) and from many state codes post-*Hinckley*. The core objection is epistemological: psychiatry, critics argue, cannot reliably distinguish between a truly irresistible impulse and an impulse that was simply *not resisted*. As Judge Bazelon, a supporter of the control prong, conceded, the inquiry invites the jury to engage in "moral guessing." This creates a palpable tension with the principle of legality, which demands that legal standards be knowable and administrable. If the line between the "could not" and the "would not" is invisible, the control prong risks becoming a charter for jury nullification or, worse, a lottery based on the persuasiveness of expert witnesses.

Furthermore, some retributivists argue that the control prong is conceptually incoherent. If the defendant knew the act was wrong, and yet did it, we have no basis for distinguishing her from the "ordinary" criminal who also knows the act is wrong but does it anyway. To interpose a psychiatric excuse is to treat the defendant as a passive vessel of her illness, which may deny her the dignity of being a moral agent capable of choice—even if that choice is evil.

**IV. The Case for Retention: The Morality of Punishment**

Yet the abolitionist position is too facile. The control prong serves a deep moral function. Consider the fact pattern of *People v. Wolff* (Cal. 1964), where a 15-year-old boy, diagnosed with schizophrenia, planned and executed a matricide. He knew the act was legally wrong, but expert testimony suggested he was so dominated by a delusional system that his capacity to control his conduct was severely impaired. To punish such a person as a fully culpable murderer seems to violate the retributive principle of proportionality. The punishment would be disproportionate to the actor's *desert*.

Moreover, the epistemological objection proves too much. The cognitive prong itself requires juries to make difficult judgments about what a defendant "knew" or "appreciated," a matter that is also deeply contested. We do not abandon the cognitive prong because it is difficult; we accept the difficulty as a necessary cost of doing justice. The same tolerance should be extended to the volitional inquiry.

**CONCLUSION**

The tension between the cognitive and control prongs reflects a deeper tension within the morality of punishment. A purely cognitive test risks punishing those who are not truly blameworthy, while a control test risks doctrinal incoherence and administrability problems. The optimal resolution, in my view, is not abolition but careful circumscription. The MPC's formulation—requiring a *substantial* lack of capacity—provides a workable standard, and courts should insist on rigorous expert testimony that grounds the volitional impairment in a diagnosed mental disease, not mere passion or impulse. The control prong should be retained, not as a loophole, but as a testament to the law's recognition that human agency is not binary, but a spectrum, and that justice requires we attend to its fractures.

### ESSAY 2: FACT PATTERN — DOMESTIC TRAGEDY (ELEANOR, RICHARD, LEO, DR. ALISTAIR)

**GOAL**

**ANALYSIS**

**I. The Actus Reus and Mens Rea of Homicide**

Eleanor shot Richard in the chest, causing his death. The actus reus of criminal homicide is clearly established. The critical question is her mens rea. Under MPC § 210.2, murder requires that the killing be committed "purposely" or "knowingly." Eleanor stated, "I had to stop him." If she acted with the conscious object of causing Richard's death, she acted purposely. The evidence suggests she retrieved the gun, aimed, and fired—a sequence indicative of purpose. Thus, the prima facie case for murder is strong.

**II. The Insanity Defense: Cognitive Prong**

Eleanor's primary defense will be insanity. The cognitive prong under MPC § 4.01(1) requires that, as a result of mental disease or defect, she lacked substantial capacity to *appreciate the criminality* of her conduct. Dr. Alistair's diagnosis of schizophrenia with persecutory delusions is precisely the type of mental disease the MPC contemplates.

The analysis turns on whether Eleanor appreciated that killing Richard was *wrong*. She believed Richard was a demon. If she believed she was destroying a demonic entity, she may have believed her act was *morally right*—a form of deific decree delusion. Some courts, following *People v. Serravo* (Colo. 1992), hold that the "wrongfulness" inquiry includes both legal and moral wrongfulness. If Eleanor believed God commanded her to destroy the demon, she may have lacked the capacity to appreciate the moral wrongfulness of her act. However, other courts, such as those following *State v. Crenshaw* (Wash. 1983), limit the inquiry to legal wrongfulness. Under this view, even if Eleanor believed the act was morally justified, she knew that the law prohibited killing a human being. She told Dr. Alistair, "I know it sounds crazy." This suggests a residual awareness of societal norms. The cognitive prong is thus a close question, heavily dependent on the jurisdiction's definition of "wrongfulness."

**III. The Insanity Defense: Volitional (Control) Prong**

If the jurisdiction retains the control prong, Eleanor's case is stronger. Dr. Alistair's testimony that Eleanor was "driven by a compulsion" and "could not resist the delusional command" directly supports a claim that she lacked substantial capacity to conform her conduct to the law. The fact that she waited for Richard to fall asleep, retrieved the gun, and fired a single shot might be argued by the prosecution as evidence of planning and control, undermining the claim of compulsion. However, as the court noted in *United States v. Pollard* (9th Cir. 1999), volitional impairment can coexist with instrumental rationality. A person can plan a delusionally-motivated act with great care. The control prong, if available, provides a more plausible path to acquittal than the cognitive prong.

**IV. Affirmative Defenses: Self-Defense and Provocation**

Self-defense is inapposite. Under MPC § 3.04, the use of deadly force is justified only if the actor reasonably believes it is necessary to protect against imminent unlawful deadly force. Even if Eleanor believed Richard was a demon, the belief must be *reasonable*. A reasonable person does not believe her husband is a demon. The Model Penal Code, unlike some common law jurisdictions, does not recognize a "reasonable mistake" standard for self-defense that would incorporate a delusional belief. *See People v. Goetz* (N.Y. 1986) (subjective belief must be objectively reasonable).

Voluntary manslaughter under MPC § 210.3(1)(b) requires an extreme mental or emotional disturbance for which there is a reasonable explanation or excuse. Eleanor's delusional state might constitute such a disturbance, but the "reasonable explanation" is assessed from the viewpoint of a person in the actor's situation under the circumstances as she believes them to be. This language is broader than common law provocation and *might* allow a jury to consider Eleanor's delusional belief system. However, the MPC's commentary suggests this provision is designed for understandable human frailty, not psychotic delusion. It is a weak argument.

**V. The Principle of Legality and the Morality of Punishment**

This case illustrates the profound tension at the heart of the insanity defense. If Eleanor is convicted of murder, retributivists must ask: does she *deserve* punishment? Her volition was hijacked by a disease. To punish her is to treat her as a moral agent when her agency was compromised. Yet utilitarians must ask: does acquittal serve the public? If Eleanor remains dangerous, civil commitment under *Addington v. Texas* (1979) provides a mechanism for incapacitation without punishment. The principle of legality demands that the law's commands be clear. Here, the ambiguity of the cognitive prong and the potential availability of the control prong create a doctrinal thicket.

**CONCLUSION**

Eleanor's strongest defense is insanity under the control prong, if available. The cognitive prong is a more difficult path, depending on the jurisdiction's definition of wrongfulness. Self-defense is unavailable, and the extreme emotional disturbance defense is a poor fit. The case ultimately turns on whether the law recognizes that a person can *know* an act is wrong yet be powerless to stop herself. This fact pattern is a powerful vehicle for examining whether the law's moral vocabulary is adequate to the complexities of the human mind.
