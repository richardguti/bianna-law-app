# CHAPTER 3: THE DORMANT COMMERCE CLAUSE (DCC)

## I. The Concept

The Commerce Clause is not just a *grant* of power to Congress — it is also an *implicit limitation* on the states. Even when Congress has **not** acted, states cannot pass laws that unduly burden or discriminate against interstate commerce.

> **Fisher's One-Liner:** "The Commerce Clause speaks not only to what Congress *can* do, but to what the States *cannot* do even in Congress's silence."

---

## II. The Two-Track Framework

### Track 1: Facial Discrimination Against Interstate Commerce → Per Se Invalid

If a state law *facially* discriminates against out-of-state commerce (treating in-state goods/services better than out-of-state), it is **virtually per se invalid**.

The state must prove:
1. A **legitimate local purpose** exists;
2. **No nondiscriminatory alternative** is available.

> This is essentially **strict scrutiny** — and the state almost never wins.

### Track 2: Evenhandedly Burdensome Laws → Pike Balancing

If a state law is *facially neutral* (applies equally to in-state and out-of-state) but still burdens interstate commerce, apply the **Pike balancing test**:

> "Where the statute regulates even-handedly to effectuate a legitimate local public interest, and its effects on interstate commerce are only incidental, it will be upheld **unless** the burden imposed on such commerce is **clearly excessive** in relation to the putative local benefits." — *Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc.* (1970)

---

## III. Key DCC Cases

### Philadelphia v. New Jersey (1978)
**437 U.S. 617 (1978)**

**FACTS:** New Jersey passed a law prohibiting the importation of most solid or liquid waste from other states into New Jersey landfills. New Jersey claimed this was an environmental/health measure.

**ISSUE:** Does a state law banning the importation of out-of-state waste violate the Dormant Commerce Clause?

**RULE:** A state cannot isolate itself from a national problem by discriminating against out-of-state commerce. Waste is an "article of commerce," even if unwanted.

**APPLICATION:**

Justice Stewart, writing for the 7-2 majority:
- **Facial Discrimination:** The law explicitly treated waste differently based on its *origin* (in-state vs. out-of-state). This is textbook discrimination.
- **Protectionism:** The "evil" of protectionism lies in "differential treatment of in-state and out-of-state economic interests that benefits the former and burdens the latter."
- **Health Pretext:** New Jersey claimed health concerns. The Court responded: waste from NJ is just as dangerous as waste from NY. If the concern is *volume*, New Jersey can cap the total waste going into landfills — but it cannot single out out-of-state waste.

**CONCLUSION:** The law is unconstitutional. It facially discriminates against interstate commerce with no legitimate, non-discriminatory justification.

---

### Dean Milk Co. v. City of Madison (1951)
**340 U.S. 349 (1951)**

**FACTS:** Madison, Wisconsin required all milk sold in the city to be pasteurized within 5 miles of the city center. This effectively excluded all out-of-state milk producers.

**HOLDING:** Invalid. The city had **reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives** — it could have required inspection of out-of-state pasteurization plants or accepted certifications from other states' inspectors.

> **Fisher Tip:** Dean Milk establishes the "least restrictive means" requirement for discriminatory DCC laws. Always ask: "Could the state achieve its health/safety goal *without* discriminating against out-of-state commerce?"

---

### Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc. (1970)
**397 U.S. 137 (1970)**

**FACTS:** Arizona required that all cantaloupes grown in Arizona be packed *in Arizona* before shipment to other states. Bruce Church, Inc. grew cantaloupes in Arizona but packed them at a nearby California facility (just across the border).

**RULE:** The Pike Balancing Test (see above). Even a facially neutral law fails if the burden on interstate commerce clearly outweighs the local benefit.

**APPLICATION:** The Court found that Arizona's real purpose was to protect its *reputation* by having Arizona cantaloupes packed under the Arizona label. This economic protectionism was not a sufficient local benefit to justify the burden of forcing a company to build an entirely new packing facility in Arizona.

**CONCLUSION:** The law is unconstitutional.

---

### 🏛️ Fisher DCC Flowchart

```
STEP 1: Does the state law FACIALLY DISCRIMINATE against out-of-state commerce?
  → YES → Apply STRICT SCRUTINY (Per Se Invalid)
    → Does the state have a LEGITIMATE LOCAL PURPOSE?
      → YES → Is there a NONDISCRIMINATORY ALTERNATIVE?
        → YES → LAW FAILS (Dean Milk)
        → NO → Law *might* survive (very rare)
      → NO → LAW FAILS (Philadelphia v. NJ)

  → NO (law is facially neutral) → Go to Step 2

STEP 2: Does the law INCIDENTALLY BURDEN interstate commerce?
  → Apply PIKE BALANCING:
    → Does the BURDEN on interstate commerce CLEARLY EXCEED
       the PUTATIVE LOCAL BENEFITS?
      → YES → LAW FAILS (Pike)
      → NO → LAW SURVIVES
```

## IV. The Market Participant Exception

**The Rule:** When a state acts as a **market participant** (buyer or seller) rather than a **market regulator**, the DCC does not apply.

- *Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap Corp.* (1976): Maryland offered bounties for scrapping old cars but made it easier for in-state processors to qualify. **Valid** — Maryland was participating in the scrap market, not regulating it.
- *Reeves, Inc. v. Stake* (1980): South Dakota owned a cement plant and gave preference to in-state buyers during shortages. **Valid** — the state was a seller.
- *South-Central Timber Dev. v. Wunnicke* (1984): Alaska required that timber from state lands be processed in Alaska before export. **Invalid** — this was a "downstream" restriction that went beyond the state's role as a seller.

> **Fisher Trap:** The Market Participant Exception has a **limit** — the state can only impose conditions on the immediate transaction, not on downstream commerce.

---

## V. Privileges & Immunities Clause (Article IV, § 2)

**The Text:** "The Citizens of each State shall be entitled to all Privileges and Immunities of Citizens in the several States."

**The Function:** Prevents states from discriminating against out-of-state **citizens** (not corporations) regarding "fundamental" rights (employment, practice of profession, access to courts).

### The Two-Part Test (*United Bldg. Trades v. Camden*, 1984):

1. Does the law burden a **fundamental** privilege or immunity?
2. If yes, is there a **substantial reason** for the discrimination, and is the discrimination **closely related** to that reason?

### Key Distinctions from DCC:

| Feature | Dormant Commerce Clause | Privileges & Immunities |
|---|---|---|
| **Protects** | Interstate commerce (goods & services) | Out-of-state citizens (natural persons) |
| **Corporations?** | Yes | **No** (only natural persons) |
| **Market Participant Exception?** | Yes | **No** |
| **Congressional Override?** | Yes (Congress can authorize discrimination) | No |

> **Fisher Exam Strategy:** If a state discriminates against out-of-state *people* (not just goods), argue **both** the DCC and Art. IV P&I. P&I is especially useful when the Market Participant Exception would block the DCC claim.

# CHAPTER 4: PREEMPTION

## I. The Supremacy Clause Framework

**Art. VI, cl. 2:** Federal law is the "supreme Law of the Land." When federal and state law conflict, federal law wins.

### Three Types of Preemption:

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Express** | Federal statute explicitly says "states may not regulate in this area" | ERISA preempts state regulation of employee benefit plans |
| **Field** | Federal regulation is so pervasive it "occupies the field," leaving no room for states | Immigration (Arizona v. US) |
| **Conflict** | State law directly conflicts with federal law (compliance with both is impossible, OR state law stands as an obstacle to federal objectives) | State law requiring more than federal minimum |

---

### Arizona v. United States (2012)
**567 U.S. 387 (2012)**

**FACTS:** Arizona passed SB 1070 in response to perceived federal inaction on immigration. Key provisions:
- Made it a state misdemeanor to fail to carry alien registration documents (§ 3);
- Made it a state crime for unauthorized aliens to seek employment (§ 5(C));
- Authorized warrantless arrest of anyone an officer had probable cause to believe had committed a deportable offense (§ 6);
- Required officers to verify immigration status during lawful stops (§ 2(B) — the "show me your papers" provision).

**ISSUE:** Are these provisions preempted by federal immigration law?

**APPLICATION:**

Justice Kennedy, writing for the 5-3 majority:

- **§ 3 (Registration):** Preempted by **field preemption**. Congress created a comprehensive federal alien registration scheme. Arizona cannot create a parallel state registration crime.
- **§ 5(C) (Employment):** Preempted by **conflict preemption**. Congress specifically chose to punish *employers* who hire unauthorized workers (IRCA), not the *workers* themselves. Arizona's law stands as an "obstacle" to that congressional choice.
- **§ 6 (Warrantless Arrest):** Preempted. It gave state officers authority that even federal officers didn't have and could lead to the prolonged detention of lawful aliens.
- **§ 2(B) (Status Check):** **Not preempted** (at this stage). The Court found it was not facially invalid because it merely required officers to check status during *otherwise lawful* stops. However, the Court warned it could be challenged "as applied" if it led to racial profiling or prolonged detention.

**CONCLUSION:** Three of four provisions are preempted. Federal immigration law occupies the field.

---

### 🏛️ Fisher Preemption Checklist

```
When you see a state law touching a federal area:

1. EXPRESS PREEMPTION: Does the federal statute say "states cannot..."?
   → If YES → State law is preempted. Done.

2. FIELD PREEMPTION: Is federal regulation so PERVASIVE that it
   "occupies the field"?
   → Look for: comprehensive federal scheme, federal agency involvement,
     dominant federal interest (immigration, foreign affairs, nuclear energy)
   → If YES → State law is preempted.

3. CONFLICT PREEMPTION:
   (a) IMPOSSIBILITY: Can someone comply with BOTH laws simultaneously?
       → If NO → State law is preempted.
   (b) OBSTACLE: Does the state law stand as an OBSTACLE to the full
       purposes and objectives of Congress?
       → If YES → State law is preempted.

4. PRESUMPTION AGAINST PREEMPTION: In areas of TRADITIONAL STATE
   REGULATION (health, safety, family law), courts presume Congress
   did NOT intend to preempt unless clearly stated.
```

# CHAPTER 5: SEPARATION OF POWERS — THE EXECUTIVE

## I. Executive Power: The Youngstown Framework

### Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)
**343 U.S. 579 (1952)** — *"The Steel Seizure Case"*

**FACTS:** During the Korean War, steelworkers threatened to strike. President Truman, fearing a steel shortage would jeopardize the war effort, issued an executive order directing the Secretary of Commerce to seize and operate the nation's steel mills. Truman did **not** invoke the Taft-Hartley Act (which Congress had passed and which gave the President the power to seek an injunction — a tool Truman deliberately rejected). The steel companies sued.

**ISSUE:** Does the President have inherent constitutional authority to seize private property during a national emergency without congressional authorization?

**RULE (Justice Black — Majority):** The President's power to issue orders must stem from an act of Congress or the Constitution itself. There is no "inherent" executive power to seize private property. The President is a law-*executor*, not a law-*maker*.

**RULE (Justice Jackson — Concurrence — THE GOLD STANDARD):**

Jackson's famous **Three-Zone Framework:**

| Zone | Scenario | Presidential Power |
|---|---|---|
| **Zone 1** | President acts with **express or implied congressional authorization** | Power at its **MAXIMUM**. President has his own power + Congress's delegated power. Presumptively valid. |
| **Zone 2** | President acts in the **absence** of congressional grant or denial ("Zone of Twilight") | Power is **UNCERTAIN**. Depends on the "imperatives of events." Congressional inertia may enable executive action. |
| **Zone 3** | President acts **contrary to the express or implied will of Congress** | Power at its **LOWEST EBB**. President can only rely on his own constitutional powers minus any Congress has over the matter. Court will scrutinize carefully. |

**APPLICATION:**

- Truman's seizure was in **Zone 3** — Congress had considered and rejected giving the President seizure power when it passed the Taft-Hartley Act. By choosing a method Congress had specifically denied, Truman was acting against the expressed will of Congress.
- In Zone 3, the President can only prevail if Congress lacks any power over the subject. Here, Congress clearly had power over property seizure and labor disputes.

**CONCLUSION:** The seizure is unconstitutional. The President exceeded his authority.

---

### 🏛️ Fisher Alert: The Youngstown Checklist

> On any Separation of Powers question involving executive action:
>
> 1. **Identify the Zone.** Did Congress authorize, stay silent, or prohibit the action?
> 2. **Zone 1:** Almost always valid. The government will win.
> 3. **Zone 2:** Gray area. Analyze the "imperatives of events" and historical practice.
> 4. **Zone 3:** Almost always invalid. The President is fighting uphill.

## II. Appointment & Removal Powers

### The Appointments Clause (Art. II, § 2, cl. 2)

- **Principal Officers:** Must be appointed by the President with Senate confirmation.
- **Inferior Officers:** Congress may vest appointment in the President alone, the Courts, or Heads of Departments.

**Key Case — Morrison v. Olson (1988):**
- Upheld the Independent Counsel Act. The independent counsel was an "inferior officer" because (1) she could be removed by the AG, (2) her duties were limited, and (3) her jurisdiction was narrow.
- **Scalia's Lone Dissent:** Argued that all executive prosecution power belongs to the President. An independent counsel who can't be fired at will violates Article II. This dissent has become increasingly influential.

### The Removal Power

| Type | Who Can Remove? | Standard | Key Case |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pure Executive Officers** | President alone | At will | *Myers v. United States* (1926) |
| **Officers with Quasi-Judicial/Legislative Functions** | Congress can limit removal to "good cause" | "For cause" protection | *Humphrey's Executor v. United States* (1935) |
| **Inferior Officers** | Congress can limit removal | Reasonable restrictions OK | *Morrison v. Olson* (1988) |
| **Double Layer of Protection** | ❌ UNCONSTITUTIONAL | Congress cannot insulate officers behind *two* layers of for-cause protection | *Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB* (2010) |

> **Fisher Trap:** If a question asks about a federal agency head who "can only be removed for good cause," check: Is this a *single* layer of protection (valid per *Humphrey's*) or a *double* layer (invalid per *Free Enterprise Fund*)?

## III. Executive Privilege & Immunity

### United States v. Nixon (1974)
**418 U.S. 683 (1974)**

- **Holding:** The President has a **qualified** executive privilege to protect confidential communications with advisors. However, this privilege is **not absolute** — it must yield to the demonstrated needs of the criminal justice system (here, the Watergate special prosecutor's subpoena for tape recordings).
- **The Balance:** The need for evidence in a criminal trial outweighs the generalized claim of confidentiality.

### Trump v. United States (2024)
**600 U.S. __ (2024)**

- **Holding:** A former President has **absolute immunity** from criminal prosecution for acts within the "conclusive and preclusive" core constitutional powers of the presidency (e.g., pardons, commanding the military).
- **Presumptive Immunity:** For all other "official acts," there is a presumption of immunity that the prosecution must overcome.
- **No Immunity:** For **unofficial/private** acts, there is no immunity.

> **Fisher Alert:** *Trump v. United States* is the most significant Separation of Powers decision since *Youngstown*. The key exam issue is drawing the line between "official" and "unofficial" presidential conduct.

---

## IV. The Non-Delegation Doctrine

**The Principle:** Congress cannot delegate its legislative power to the Executive Branch. Any delegation must include an "intelligible principle" to guide the executive's discretion.

**The Test (*J.W. Hampton Jr. & Co. v. United States*, 1928):** A delegation is valid if Congress provides an "intelligible principle" to which the executive must conform.

**The Two Historical Failures:**
- *Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan* (1935): Struck down a provision giving the President unlimited power to ban oil shipments. No standards.
- *A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States* (1935): Struck down the NIRA's delegation of power to set "fair competition" codes. No limits on what the codes could contain.

**Modern Reality:** Since 1935, the Court has **never** struck down a delegation. Standards like "public interest," "just and reasonable," and "necessary and appropriate" have all been upheld.

> **Fisher Prediction:** Justice Gorsuch has signaled interest in reviving the non-delegation doctrine. Watch for cases involving broad EPA or agency authority.

# CHAPTER 6: PROCEDURAL DUE PROCESS

## I. The Two-Step Framework

### Step 1: Is There a Protected Interest?

The Due Process Clause protects **life**, **liberty**, and **property**. But "property" doesn't just mean land — it includes government *entitlements*.

**The "New Property" Doctrine (*Goldberg v. Kelly*, 1970):**
- Welfare benefits are a **property interest** if the recipient has a legitimate claim of entitlement under existing rules.
- The government cannot terminate them without Due Process.

**Board of Regents v. Roth (1972):**
- A non-tenured professor had **no** property interest in being rehired because his contract gave him no entitlement to renewal. Property interests must be created by an independent source of law (statute, contract, regulation).

**Perry v. Sindermann (1972):**
- Even without a formal contract, an **implied** promise of continued employment (like an unwritten tenure system) can create a property interest.

### Step 2: What Process is Due?

### Mathews v. Eldridge (1976)
**424 U.S. 319 (1976)**

**FACTS:** George Eldridge's Social Security disability benefits were terminated after an internal review. He was not given an evidentiary hearing *before* termination — only after. He sued, arguing *Goldberg v. Kelly* required a pre-termination hearing.

**ISSUE:** Does the Due Process Clause require an evidentiary hearing before (rather than after) the termination of Social Security disability benefits?

**RULE — The Mathews Balancing Test:**

The Court weighs **three factors**:

1. **The Private Interest:** How important is the benefit to the individual? How severe is the deprivation?
2. **The Risk of Error:** How likely is the current procedure to produce a wrong result? Would additional safeguards reduce that risk?
3. **The Government Interest:** How much would additional procedures cost? What is the administrative burden?

**APPLICATION:**

Justice Powell distinguished *Goldberg*:
- **Welfare (Goldberg):** Recipients are typically on the "brutal edge of subsistence." A wrongful termination could mean starvation. Pre-termination hearing required.
- **Disability (Eldridge):** Recipients can turn to other sources (welfare, savings). The determination is based on **medical records** (objective), not credibility (subjective). A pre-termination hearing would add enormous cost for marginal accuracy improvement.

**CONCLUSION:** No pre-termination hearing required. The existing procedures (written questionnaire + post-termination hearing) provide adequate due process.

---

### 🏛️ Fisher Flowchart: Procedural Due Process

```
STEP 1: Is there a PROTECTED INTEREST?
  → LIFE: Always protected.
  → LIBERTY: Includes physical freedom, reputation (if combined with
     loss of a tangible interest — "stigma plus"), parental rights.
  → PROPERTY: Must have a "legitimate claim of entitlement" created
     by statute, contract, or regulation (Roth).
  → If NO protected interest → NO DUE PROCESS REQUIRED. Stop.

STEP 2: What PROCESS is DUE?
  → Apply MATHEWS v. ELDRIDGE Balancing:
    (1) PRIVATE INTEREST: How important? How severe?
    (2) RISK OF ERROR: Is the current process reliable?
        Would more process help?
    (3) GOVERNMENT INTEREST: Cost and administrative burden.

  → RESULT: The more important the private interest AND the higher
    the risk of error AND the lower the government's cost,
    the MORE process is due (approaching Goldberg's
    full pre-deprivation hearing).
```

---

### The "Stigma-Plus" Doctrine

A government statement that damages your **reputation** alone is NOT a deprivation of liberty. But if the stigma is combined with a **tangible loss** (loss of employment, inability to get another job), then Due Process applies.

- *Paul v. Davis* (1976): Police posted a "shoplifters" flyer with Davis's photo. No tangible loss beyond stigma → No Due Process claim.
- *Wisconsin v. Constantineau* (1971): Being publicly posted as a "drunkard" and banned from buying alcohol → Stigma PLUS loss of a legal right → Due Process applies.

> **Fisher Trap:** Students often think any government attack on reputation triggers Due Process. It doesn't. You need "stigma **plus**" a concrete deprivation.

---

## Summary Table: Key Procedural Due Process Cases

| Case | Interest | Process Required? |
|---|---|---|
| *Goldberg v. Kelly* (1970) | Welfare benefits | YES — Full pre-termination hearing |
| *Roth* (1972) | Non-tenured job (no entitlement) | NO — No property interest |
| *Perry* (1972) | De facto tenure (implied entitlement) | YES — Some process |
| *Mathews v. Eldridge* (1976) | Disability benefits | YES — But post-termination hearing suffices |
| *Cleveland Bd. v. Loudermill* (1985) | Tenured public employment | YES — Pre-termination notice + opportunity to respond |

# CHAPTER 7: PRIVILEGES & IMMUNITIES (14th AMENDMENT) & THE RIGHT TO TRAVEL

## I. The Slaughter-House Cases (1873)

**The Holding:** The 14th Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause protects only a narrow set of **federal** citizenship rights (access to federal offices, protection on the high seas, right to travel to Washington D.C.). It does NOT incorporate the Bill of Rights or protect general civil liberties.

**The Effect:** This decision effectively *gutted* the Privileges or Immunities Clause for over 100 years. That's why the Court later used the **Due Process Clause** (not Privileges or Immunities) to incorporate the Bill of Rights.

> **Fisher Note:** Justice Thomas has repeatedly argued that *Slaughter-House* was wrong and that the Privileges or Immunities Clause should be revived as the proper vehicle for incorporation (*McDonald v. Chicago* concurrence).

---

## II. Saenz v. Roe (1999)
**526 U.S. 489 (1999)**

**FACTS:** California limited new residents' welfare benefits to the amount they would have received in their prior state of residence for their first year. A family moving from a state with low benefits to California would get less than longtime California residents.

**ISSUE:** Does this violate the right to travel?

**RULE:** The **right to travel** has three components:
1. The right to enter and leave another state (Art. IV);
2. The right to be treated as a welcome visitor (Art. IV P&I);
3. **The right of new citizens to be treated the same as established citizens** (14th Amendment Privileges or Immunities Clause).

**HOLDING:** California's law violates component 3. Once you become a citizen of a state, you are entitled to the **same** benefits as every other citizen. A state cannot create a two-tier system based on how long you've lived there.

> **Fisher Alert:** *Saenz* is the **only** modern case to successfully rely on the 14th Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause. It remains an extremely narrow doctrine.
