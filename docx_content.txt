I. The Sweep of Criminal Law in America
Mass Incarceration: The U.S. has the largest penal system globally. Since 1972, the incarceration rate has grown fivefold (approx. 830 per 100,000). The U.S. holds 25% of the world's prisoners despite having only 5% of the population.
Racial and Social Concentration:
African Americans: Comprise ~33% of the incarcerated population but only 13.4% of the total U.S. population.
Sentencing Disparity: Black men receive sentences roughly 19.1% longer than "similarly situated" white men.
Systemic Nature: 1 in 3 Black men can expect to be incarcerated in their lifetime compared to 1 in 17 white men.
Causes of Growth: The rise is not strictly tied to crime rates (which fell in the 90s while incarceration rose). It is attributed to policy choices: aggressive drug enforcement, mandatory minimums, and "three-strikes" laws.
Underenforcement vs. Overenforcement: 
 Overenforcement: Excessive policing and harsh sentencing in poor/minority communities.
Underenforcement: Failure of the state to protect vulnerable groups (e.g., domestic violence, white-collar crime, or "Black-on-Black" homicide).
II. Theories of Punishment
1. Utilitarianism (Consequentialist/Forward-Looking)
Punishment is an "evil" only justified if it prevents a greater evil. The goal is to maximize social utility/happiness.
Deterrence:  classified as a utilitarian principle and justified by its effectiveness rather than its righteousness. 
General: Punishing one to discourage the general public.
Specific: Punishing one to discourage that specific individual from re-offending.
Note: Studies show the certainty of being caught is a stronger deterrent than the severity of the punishment.
Deterrence must have three prerequisites to be properly satisfied:  
∆ must know the of the rule 
∆ must perceive the cost of violation as greater than the perceived benefits
∆ must be willing and able to bring a knowing offense 
Rehabilitation: Correcting the offender so they can return to society. Includes the "Medical Model" (treating crime as a social/mental illness).
Incapacitation: Preventing future crime by physically removing the offender from society ("A thug in prison can't shoot your sister").
Critical Issue: "Selective Incapacitation" relies on predictions of future dangerousness, which often have high "false-positive" rates and can bake in racial bias.
2. Retributivism (Backward-Looking)
Punishment is justified because the offender deserves it. It is a "categorical imperative" (Kant).
Positive Retributivism: The state must punish the guilty to the extent they deserve.
Negative Retributivism: Guilt is a necessary condition for punishment, and the punishment must not exceed what is deserved (sets an upper limit).
Fair Play Theory: Crime is seen as "free-riding." The criminal took the benefits of a stable society without the "burden" of self-restraint. Punishment restores the equilibrium.
Regina v. Dudley and Stephens (1884)
Facts
The Setting: Four crew members of the English yacht Mignonette (Dudley, Stephens, Brooks, and the cabin boy Richard Parker) were cast away in a storm 1,600 miles from the Cape of Good Hope.
The Scarcity: They were in an open boat with no water and only two 1lb tins of turnips. After 12 days, they had no food left. By day 20, they had been without food for eight days and without water for five.
The Proposal: Dudley suggested drawing lots to see who should die to save the others. Brooks refused. Parker (the boy) was not consulted.
The Act: Dudley, with Stephens' assent, killed Parker (who was the weakest and already near death from drinking seawater). The three men fed on the boy's body for four days.
The Rescue: On the fourth day of cannibalism, they were rescued by a passing vessel.
The Finding: The jury found that if they had not fed on the boy, they likely would have died within four days. There was no "appreciable chance" of saving life except by killing someone.
Issue: 
Whether "extreme necessity" (the imminent threat of starvation) serve as a legal justification/defense for the murder of an unoffending, innocent person?
Rule:
 Murder is the intentional killing of a human being. Under English Common Law, "necessity" is not a defense to murder. To justify killing in self-defense, the victim must be an attacker or threat; there is no law that allows a person to choose the life of an innocent over their own.
Analysis: 
 Self-Defense Distinguished: 
The court differentiates this from self-defense. In self-defense, the victim is an assailant. Here, Parker was "weak and unoffending."
The Measurement Problem: Lord Coleridge argues that humans cannot accurately measure the comparative value of lives. If necessity were a defense, who decides who is "more valuable"? Allowing such a plea would be a "cloak for unbridled passion."
The Moral Standard: The law must set standards even if humans struggle to meet them. Though the temptation was "terrible," the court held that "to preserve one’s life is generally speaking a duty, but it may be the highest duty to sacrifice it."
Sovereign Mercy: The court suggests that while the law must find them guilty, the Sovereign (the Crown) has the power of mercy to commute the sentence (which happened—the death sentence was commuted to six months).
Conclusion: The prisoners are guilty of murder. Necessity is not a legal defense to the taking of an innocent life.

I. Rehabilitation
Michael Vitiello (The Evolution of Rehabilitation)
Puritan View: Punishment was based on the "innate depravity" of humans. Its goal was not to save the sinner (who was inherently sinful) but to protect the community from temptation.
Quaker View: Shifted away from corporal punishment to workhouses/penitentiaries. Believed crime was a product of society, not inherent sin. Transformation was achieved through rationality, reflection, and hard labor rather than medical "treatment."
Social Responsibility: Early reformers argued society shared blame for "corrupting" individuals through poor social conditions, creating a moral imperative to offer a chance at transformation.
Michael S. Moore (The Two Rehabilitative Ideals)
Safety-Based Rehabilitation: Justified by making the offender safe for the streets. The benefit is for the public (safer community).
Paternalistic Rehabilitation: Seeks to help the offender lead a "flourishing life." Moore critiques this as:
Misallocating resources away from the "deserving" poor or disabled.
Infringing on liberty (paternalism).
Creating "moral blindness" where "treatments" are inflicted regardless of what the offender actually deserves.
The "Medical Model" (Mid-20th Century Note)
Viewed criminals as "sick" and in need of a "cure."
Used indeterminate sentences (sentences ending only when the "patient" was cured) and gave parole boards wide discretion.
The Fall: Criticized by the Left (biased against low-income/people of color) and the Right (costly/lenient). Led to the 1970s "nothing works" era and the 1984 Sentencing Reform Act.
Andrew Von Hirsch & Lisa Maher (Critique of Modern Rehabilitation)
Argue that effective treatment (e.g., for drug-abusing robbers) often requires intervention far more "drastic" than what is proportionate to the crime.
Warn that a "treatment ethos" can become a facade for unfettered state power and biased decision-making.
II. Incapacitation
Franklin E. Zimring & Gordon Hawkins
Define incapacitation as the most "uncomplicated" and central goal of prison: simply restraining those under control to prevent further crime.
John J. DiIulio, Jr. (The Economic Case)
Argues prisons are "cost-effective" because they restrain known predators.
Claims it costs society twice as much to let a prisoner loose than to lock them up ($25k for prison vs. the high cost of victimization).
Quote: "A thug in prison can't shoot your sister."
John J. Donohue III & Peter Seligman (Empirical Critique)
Diminishing Returns: The "mean criminality" of those leaving prison is lower than the total stock of prisoners; therefore, lengthening sentences doesn't stop as many crimes as estimated.
Replacement Effect: In gangs/rings, one incapacitated member is simply replaced by a new recruit.
Aging Out: Most crime happens in late teens/early 20s. Long sentences (e.g., for a 20-year-old) often extend past the age the person would have stopped offending anyway.
Jacqueline Cohen (Selective Incapacitation)
The theory of targeting "high-rate" offenders for longer stays based on "scientific evidence."
Critiques: Predictions are often "false-positives" (60%+ error rate), and the variables used (employment, education) are proxies for race and class.

III. Retribution
Immanuel Kant (Positive Retributivism)
Categorical Imperative: Punishment must be imposed only because a crime was committed, never as a means to a social "good."
Principle of Equality: The "pointer of the scale" must be balanced. "If you kill another, you kill yourself."
Moral Necessity: Even if a society were to dissolve itself, the last murderer in jail must be executed so that "blood guiltiness" does not remain on the people.
Michael S. Moore (The Moral Worth of Retribution)
Punishment is justified by moral culpability alone.
It is a duty of society to punish, not just a right. It is independent of the victim's wishes or the prevention of future crime.
Herbert Morris (Fair Play/Equilibrium)
Society is a system of "mutual benefits and burdens." Law-abiding citizens accept the burden of self-restraint.
A criminal is a "free rider" who takes the benefits of society but renounces the burden of restraint. Punishment "exacts the debt" to restore equilibrium.
Jeffrie Murphy (Marxist Critique of Retribution)
Argues the "debt to society" model is a fantasy for the poor and marginalized.
If a person (e.g., an impoverished Black man) has been excluded from society’s benefits (jobs, housing, safety), he owes no "debt" of obedience to its rules.
Not a plausible solution the critique is not matched by a positive solution to fix this problem even if its desired it leads to a collapse of society. 
Jean Hampton & John L. Mackie (Critiques of "Fair Play")
Hampton: We don't punish murderers because they "gained an advantage"; we punish them because murder is wrong. Most people don't view the "freedom to murder" as a coveted benefit they are missing out on.
Mackie: The "fair play" model leads to bizarre results (e.g., a businessman who speeds to get a $100k contract should be fined $100k, while a murderer who steals $1 should be fined $1).
Danielle Sered (Retribution and Survival)
Points out the cycle of violence: most people who commit violence are also survivors of violence. State retribution often punishes those the state failed to protect in the first place.
IV. Retaliation and Revenge
James Fitz James Stephen (Vengeance as Social Glue)
Punishment gives "definite expression" to the hatred society feels for a criminal.
The law is to "moral sentiment" what a "seal is to hot wax," stamping a permanent judgment on the offender.
Argues it is morally right for the law to justify and ratify the public's "hatred" of the criminal.
1. Incapacitation: The "Lock and Key" Logic
The Collective Benefit: DiIulio argues that even if prison doesn't "fix" anyone, it is a "bargain" because it prevents "murder and mayhem" by simple subtraction. If an offender is in a cell, they are physically unable to harm the general public.
The "Replacement" Critique: A major academic challenge to incapacitation is the "Recruitment" effect. For crimes like drug dealing or gang activity, locking one person up often just creates a job opening for someone else, meaning the total number of crimes in society stays the same.
The "Age-Out" Factor: Most criminals stop committing crimes as they get older (the "maturation" effect). Critics like Donohue & Seligman point out that long sentences for older inmates provide zero incapacitative benefit because they wouldn't have committed crimes anyway.
False Positives: Actuarial tools (like COMPAS) often result in "over-prediction." If a tool is 75% accurate, that still means 25% of people are being kept in prison for crimes they might have committed but actually wouldn't have.
2. Rehabilitation: The "Medical Model"
Offender-Centric: Unlike other theories, rehabilitation focuses on the internal state of the defendant. It views crime as a social or psychological "malady" that can be treated.
Critique of Cost: While DiIulio and others admit drug treatment is cheaper than prison, the "Nothing Works" movement of the 1970s (led by Robert Martinson) historically soured many lawmakers on rehabilitation, leading to the "get tough" mandatory minimums you will study later.
The Moral Dilemma: Some critics argue that forced rehabilitation is actually more intrusive than a set prison term because the state is trying to "reprogram" a person's mind rather than just punishing their body.
3. Retribution: The "Moral Scale"
Positive Retributivism (The Must): For Kant, punishment is a "categorical imperative." Society must punish the guilty because the "balance of justice" requires it. If you don't punish, you are essentially saying the crime was "okay." You are saying that if society collapses tomorrow you still must 
Negative Retributivism (The Limit): This is the more common modern view. It says that while we might punish to deter others, the "desert" of the offender acts as a ceiling. You can't give someone 20 years for shoplifting just to "scare" other shoplifters, because that would be "unjust" to the individual.
The "Fair Play" Nuance: Herbert Morris views the law as a "contract." When you follow the law, you "pay a price" (you don't steal the thing you want). The criminal is a "free rider" who gets the benefit of a safe society without paying the price of self-restraint. Punishment "evens the score."
Critique of Equality: Jeffrie Murphy argues this "Fair Play" model is a fantasy because many criminals come from backgrounds where they never received the "benefits" of society in the first place, so they don't actually "owe" a debt to a system that failed them.
4. Victim-Impact & Retaliation: The "Resentment" Theory
Emotional Ratification: James Fitzjames Stephen famously argued that the law should be a "seal on hot wax." The public feels "hatred" toward a killer; the law's job is to turn that "transient sentiment" into a "permanent judgment" (the sentence).
The Payne Decision: The Supreme Court in Payne v. Tennessee decided that a victim is not just a "faceless statistic." By allowing Nicholas's grandmother to testify about his grief, the Court allowed the jury to see the "full extent" of the harm, which they linked to the defendant's "moral blameworthiness."
The "Moral Alchemy" Critique: H.L.A. Hart called retribution "moral alchemy"—the weird idea that by adding "suffering" (punishment) to "wickedness" (crime), you somehow create "good." He found the logic mysterious and illogical.
5. Social Cohesion: The "Message" Theory
Moral Reinforcement: Durkheim believed that when a judge sentences a criminal, they are talking to the non-criminals. It tells the "honest people" that their decision to follow the rules is correct and valued.
The Expressive Function: This theory explains why we punish "victimless" crimes or symbolic acts. By punishing, the state "expresses" its values. As Husak noted, the UK's "upskirting" law was less about catching thousands of people and more about the state saying, "We find this behavior abhorrent."
Reconstructivism: Joshua Kleinfeld argues that crime "breaks" the social fabric, and punishment is the "repair job" that restores the community's sense of safety and shared norms.
6. Mixed Theories: The "Compromise"
H.L.A. Hart's Distinction: This is the most important takeaway for a 1L. Hart says we must distinguish between:
General Justifying Aim: Why do we have a system at all? (To prevent crime / Utilitarianism).
Distribution: Who do we punish and how much? (Only the guilty and only what they deserve / Retributivism).
The "Chaney" Problem: Michael Moore uses the rape/robbery case to show that Mixed Theory fails when utility and desert clash. If the defendant is no longer a threat (Utility = 0), but the crime was heinous (Desert = High), the Mixed Theory struggles to explain why we still feel a deep need to lock him up.
7. Sex Work: The "Limits" of Criminal Law
The Harm Principle Crisis: Mill said only "harm to others" justifies law. But in sex work:
The Nordic Model proponents say the "harm" is the exploitation of women and the fuel for trafficking.
The Decriminalization proponents say the "harm" is created by the law (police harassment, lack of safety, underground markets). Retaining the processcution of the purchasesers of the sex work. 
The Consent Debate: This is the core of the Dowling & Bigelsen piece. They argue that in a world of poverty and addiction, "consent" is often an illusion, meaning the "consensual transaction" is actually a form of "violence against the marginalized."

CHAPTER 3: TRINITY OF PUNISHMENT
A. Introduction
The Trinity of Punishment: Three principles limit the state’s power to punish: 
Legality (no crime without law), 
Culpability (no punishment without fault), and 
Proportionality (punishment must fit the crime).
The Model Penal Code (MPC):
Created in 1962 by the American Law Institute (ALI).
Theory/Status: It is not "law" itself but a "model." However, more than half of U.S. states have adapted their codes to match it.
The Theory of Purpose (§1.02(1)): The ALI posits that criminal law should provide "fair warning" (Legality), safeguard "conduct without fault" (Culpability), and "differentiate on reasonable grounds" (Proportionality).
A Critical Question: Does the current record-high incarceration rate suggest we are departing from these three principles?
B. Legality
Commonwealth v. Mochan (Pa. Super. Ct. 1955)
Facts: Michael Mochan was charged with a common-law misdemeanor. Between 2:00 AM and 4:00 AM, he repeatedly called Louise Zivkovich, a married woman. During these calls, he used "scurrilous, opprobrious, filthy, disgusting and indecent language," suggested she was lewd, and made suggestions of sodomy. There was no specific statute in Pennsylvania at the time criminalizing "obscene phone calls." Mochan argued that because no statute prohibited his specific conduct, he could not be convicted.
Issue: In a state that recognizes common-law crimes, can a defendant be convicted for conduct that outrages public decency and morals even if no specific statute or exact judicial precedent prohibits that conduct?
Rule: The common law is broad enough to punish as a misdemeanor any act which directly injures or tends to injure the public to such an extent as to require the state to interfere, specifically acts which injuriously affect public morality or obstruct public justice.
Application: The court applied the "public injury" test. While there was no exact precedent for telephone harassment, the court looked at the nature of the acts. Because the suggestions of sodomy and the lewd language were made over a four-party line (where others could listen) and were heard by members of the victim's household, the court found the conduct "scandalously affected the morals" of the community. The court reasoned that the common law is a living thing and doesn't need a "name" for every crime so long as the act is "injurious to public morals."
Conclusion: Judgment and sentences affirmed. (The dissent by Woodside, J., argued this was a violation of the Separation of Powers, as courts should not "arrogate" the power to define crimes that the legislature has not.)
Summary of Notes (1-5)
The Legality Principle (Nulla poena sine lege): No punishment without law. This serves "fair warning" and controls the discretion of police and prosecutors. It bars retroactivity (punishing past acts) and vagueness (unclear laws).
Institutional Limits: While courts could define crimes prospectively, Judge Woodside argues this still violates the separation of powers.
Old Common-Law Crimes: Crimes like murder and larceny were originally judge-made. While most states now codify these, some still allow common-law definitions to fill gaps.
New Common-Law Crimes: Most states (including PA now) have abolished the court's power to create new crimes.
Theory (Chief Justice John Marshall): Punishment is a legislative power. The legislature, not the court, must define the crime and ordain the punishment (U.S. v. Wiltberger).
Theory (Professor Carissa Byrne Hessick): Argues that moving to statutes hasn't actually helped; it just moved the power from judges to prosecutors, who now use broad statutes to force plea bargains.
Meaning of Legislation: Since most crimes are now statutory, the court's role is narrowed to Statutory Interpretation.

McBoyle v. United States (U.S. 1931)
Facts: McBoyle was convicted under the National Motor Vehicle Theft Act for transporting a stolen airplane from Illinois to Oklahoma. The Act defined a "motor vehicle" as "an automobile, automobile truck, automobile wagon, motor cycle, or any other self-propelled vehicle not designed for running on rails." McBoyle appealed, claiming an airplane is not a "motor vehicle."
Issue: Does the phrase "any other self-propelled vehicle not designed for running on rails" include aircraft, or is it limited to land-based vehicles?
Rule: The Fair Warning Principle: A criminal statute must give fair warning to the world in language that the "common world" understands of what the law intends to do if a line is passed.
Application: Justice Holmes performed a textual analysis. He noted that while "vehicle" could etymologically include things that fly, in "everyday speech," the word evokes a picture of a thing moving on land. He looked at the list provided in the statute (trucks, wagons, motorcycles) and applied a version of ejusdem generis: the specific items listed were all land-based, so the "catch-all" phrase should also be land-based. Holmes emphasized that even if the legislature would have wanted to include planes, the court cannot "extend" the law beyond the "popular picture" the words evoke in the common mind.
Conclusion: Judgment reversed. Airplanes are not "motor vehicles" under this Act.

Yates v. United States (U.S. 2015)
Facts: John Yates was a commercial fisherman in the Gulf of Mexico. A federal officer found 72 undersized red grouper on his boat and put them in crates. Yates ordered his crew to throw the undersized fish overboard and replace them with larger fish to avoid a civil fine. He was charged under 18 U.S.C. §1519 (part of the Sarbanes-Oxley Act), which prohibits the destruction of any "record, document, or tangible object" with the intent to obstruct a federal investigation.
Issue: Does the term "tangible object" in a statute designed to prevent financial record-tampering include a "fish"?
Rule: Statutory interpretation must consider the specific context, the broader context of the statute, and the Rule of Lenity (ambiguity in criminal laws is resolved in favor of the defendant).
Application:
Noscitur a sociis: The term "tangible object" is "known by the company it keeps." It sits next to "record" and "document." Therefore, it should be limited to objects used to record or preserve information (like a hard drive).
Ejusdem generis: General words following specific words are construed to embrace only objects similar in nature. A fish is not similar to a "record."
Statutory Context: The law was passed after the Enron scandal to stop "corporate and accounting deception."
Rule of Lenity: Because the term is ambiguous in this context, the court must choose the narrower interpretation to ensure "fair warning."
Conclusion: Judgment reversed. A "tangible object" under §1519 must be one used to record or preserve information; a fish does not qualify.

Problem: The "Jennifer" Case
Scenario: Adult male poses as "Jennifer" online to get minors to masturbate on camera. Charged with "sexual activity" under §2422(b).

Keeler v. Superior Court (Cal. 1970)
Facts: Robert Keeler intercepted his pregnant ex-wife on a remote road. Seeing her advanced pregnancy (35 weeks), he said, "I'm going to stomp it out of you," and shoved his knee into her abdomen. The fetus was delivered stillborn with a severely fractured skull. Keeler was charged with murder under Penal Code §187, which defined murder as the "unlawful killing of a human being, with malice aforethought."
Issue: Can the judicial definition of "human being" be expanded to include a viable fetus for the purpose of a murder charge without violating the Due Process Clause's ban on retroactive criminal law?
Rule: The Legality Principle (specifically the ban on ex post facto judicial enlargement): An "unforeseeable judicial enlargement of a criminal statute, applied retroactively, operates precisely like an ex post facto law" and violates Due Process.
Application: The court looked at the intent of the California Legislature in 1850. At that time, the common-law "born alive" rule was strictly followed (a fetus was not a "human being" until it was born and had an independent circulation). The court held that to change this definition now to include a fetus—even a viable one—would be an "unforeseeable enlargement." Because Keeler did not have "fair warning" that his act constituted murder at the time he committed it, the court could not apply a new definition to his case.
Conclusion: Writ of prohibition granted; the murder charge was dismissed. (The dissent argued the term "human being" should be a "spectrum" that evolves with medical science).
Summary of Notes (1-3)
Legislative Response: After Keeler, California changed the law to specifically include a "fetus."
Prosecuting Mothers: Fetal-homicide laws are now sometimes used against pregnant women (drug use, etc.).
The Rogers v. Tennessee Exception: * Theory (The Rogers Court): Judges can abolish old common-law rules (like the "year and a day" rule for murder) retroactively as long as the change isn't "unexpected and indefensible."
Dissent Theory (Justice Scalia): It is a "curious constitution" where unelected judges can do what elected representatives are forbidden from doing: making conduct a crime after it happened.
City of Chicago v. Morales (U.S. 1999)
Facts: Chicago's "Gang Congregation Ordinance" required police to order groups of people to disperse if the officer "reasonably believed" one person was a gang member and they were "loitering" in a public place. Loitering was defined as "remaining in any one place with no apparent purpose." Failure to obey the order was a crime. Over 42,000 people were arrested under this law.
Issue: Is a loitering ordinance unconstitutionally vague if it fails to provide fair notice of prohibited conduct and fails to establish minimal guidelines for law enforcement?
Rule: The Void for Vagueness Doctrine: A law is unconstitutional if it (1) fails to provide notice that enables ordinary people to understand what conduct is prohibited, or (2) authorizes/encourages arbitrary and discriminatory enforcement.
Application:
Fair Notice: The phrase "no apparent purpose" is too subjective. An ordinary citizen standing on a street corner cannot know if their purpose (waiting for a bus, talking to a neighbor) is "apparent" to a police officer.
Arbitrary Enforcement: The ordinance provided "absolute discretion" to police. It didn't require the loitering to be "harmful" or "threatening." A cop could order a gang member and his father to move even if they were just looking at a ballpark. This "standardless sweep" allows police to pursue "personal predilections."
Conclusion: Affirmation of the Illinois Supreme Court; the ordinance is unconstitutionally vague.
Summary of Notes (1-4)
Constitutional Foundations: * Theory (Justice Gorsuch): Vagueness is a "parchment barrier" if it doesn't actually stop arbitrary power. Clear laws are required for the Sixth Amendment to work (you can't defend yourself if you don't know what you're accused of).
Discriminatory Enforcement: Vagueness doctrine is different from Equal Protection. Equal Protection requires proof of actual bias (hard to prove). Vagueness looks at the wording of the law and asks if it invites bias.
Vagrancy Laws: Historically used to control "undesirables" (Black people, the poor).

C. PROPORTIONALITY
Core Principle: Punishment must match the seriousness of the offense.
Legal Basis: Found in the Eighth Amendment (prohibiting "cruel and unusual punishment") and state statutes like MPC §1.02(1)(e), N.Y. Penal Law §1.05, and Cal. Penal Code §1170.
The Big Questions: * Is proportionality an absolute limit (retributive) or a sliding scale for efficiency (utilitarian)?
Should we punish based on "wickedness" or "social harm"?
Is a sentence "excessive" if it doesn't result in a net benefit to society?

JEREMY BENTHAM: PRINCIPLES OF PENAL LAW
Rule I: The "Profit-Outweigh" Rule
The Rule: The value of the punishment must never be less than what is sufficient to outweigh the profit of the offense.
The Logic: If a criminal makes $100 from a crime but is only fined $50, they have a $50 "net profit." Bentham argues that without a "net loss," there is nothing to restrain the offender from committing the crime again and again.
Rule III: The "Incentive to Choose the Lesser Crime" Rule
The Rule: When two offenses are in competition (meaning a person could choose to do either), the punishment for the greater offense must be severe enough to induce the person to choose the lesser one.
The Logic: If the punishment for "Theft" is the same as the punishment for "Theft + Murder," you have essentially made murder "free." The criminal will kill the witness to increase their chance of getting away with the theft because there is no additional "cost" for the killing.
Rule IV: The "Micro-Deterrence" Rule
The Rule: Punishment should be adjusted so that for every "part" of the mischief, there is a motive to restrain the offender.
The Logic: If you punish someone the same for stealing $5 as you do for stealing $10, the "second $5" is effectively unpunished. The punishment should scale up with the harm caused (e.g., more money stolen = more years in prison).
Rule V: The "Minimalist" Rule (The Maximum Limit)
The Rule: Punishment should never be more than what is absolutely necessary to satisfy the other rules.
The Logic: Any punishment beyond what is needed to deter is "wasteful" and "evil" in itself. However, Bentham admits that because human desires vary, legislators usually have to "over-punish" slightly to ensure they deter even the most excited or desperate offenders.
Rule VII & VIII: The "Certainty and Proximity" Adjustments
The Rules: Punishment must be increased in magnitude if it falls short in certainty (chance of getting caught) or proximity (how soon the punishment happens).
The Logic: Humans naturally discount future or uncertain pain. If there is only a 10% chance of being caught, the punishment must be 10x more severe to feel "equal" in the mind of the criminal at the moment of the crime.
Important Notes on Bentham’s Warnings
The "Legislative Bias" Warning: Bentham warns that legislators are naturally inclined to "Rule Maximum" (excessive severity) because of antipathy or a lack of compassion for people they view as "vile."
The "Inconvenience of Severity": If you use up your "greatest punishments" (like life in prison) for "small offenses," you lose the power to increase the punishment when that same person commits a truly massive crime.
 HARMELIN V. MICHIGAN (501 U.S. 957)
Facts
Ronald Harmelin was convicted under Michigan law for possessing 672 grams of cocaine. He was a first-time offender. Michigan law mandated a sentence of Life Without the Possibility of Parole (LWOP) for this amount. Harmelin challenged the sentence under the Eighth Amendment, arguing it was "significantly disproportionate" to the crime of "mere possession" and that the mandatory nature of the law prevented the judge from considering his clean record.
Issue: Does the Eighth Amendment’s "Cruel and Unusual Punishments" Clause contain a proportionality guarantee that forbids a mandatory LWOP sentence for a first-time drug possession offense?
Rule: 
* The Scalia View: The Eighth Amendment contains no proportionality guarantee for prison terms; it only bans certain modes of punishment (e.g., the rack).
The Kennedy/Solem View: The Eighth Amendment forbids "grossly disproportionate" sentences. A court should consider: (1) gravity of offense vs. severity of penalty, (2) intra-jurisdictional comparisons, and (3) inter-jurisdictional comparisons.
Application:
Justice Scalia (and Rehnquist): Argued that "gravity" is subjective. Who is a judge to say drug possession isn't as grave as a violent crime? He believes the legislature (the people of Michigan) should decide the severity, not the courts. He would overrule Solem.
Justice Kennedy (Plurality): He agreed with the sentence but disagreed with Scalia on the rule. He held that proportionality review is required but only in "rare" cases of "gross disproportionality." Because 672 grams of cocaine (65,000 doses) causes massive social harm and violence, the sentence is not "grossly" excessive. Therefore, no further comparison to other crimes is needed.
Justice White (Dissent): Applied the Solem test. He argued (1) possession is a "passive" crime less serious than murder or rape, which Michigan punished less severely; and (2) Michigan was the only state in the country with such a harsh penalty for this offense.
Conclusion: The sentence was Upheld. The Court found no Eighth Amendment violation.
1. PROPORTIONALITY AND THE PURPOSES OF PUNISHMENT
The Theoretical Tension: Proportionality review depends on your "Why."
Utilitarianism: A sentence is proportionate if the social benefits (deterrence/incapacitation) outweigh the costs. High sentences for minor crimes might be "proportionate" if they successfully stop a crime wave.
Retributivism: A sentence is only proportionate if it is commensurate with the offender's culpability and the gravity of the crime.
Exam Tip: When analyzing a sentence, ask: "Is this sentence high because the person is 'evil' (retribution) or because we are trying to 'scare' others (deterrence)?"
2. WHO SHOULD DECIDE? (INSTITUTIONAL COMPETENCE)
The Legislative Deference View (Scalia/Kennedy): Legislatures represent the "people" and are better suited to make subjective value judgments about how "bad" a crime is.
The Judicial Review View (White/Solem): The Eighth Amendment exists to check the "irrational punitive instincts" of the majority.
Alternative Voices: * The Jury: Some suggest empaneling special "sentencing juries" to get a true "people's view" rather than relying on "distant legislators" (Meghan Ryan).
Moral Panic: Severe laws (like "Three Strikes") often stem from "passion" or "panic" following a high-profile tragedy (e.g., the Polly Klaas murder), rather than reasoned reflection. Courts may be needed to act as the "sober" second look.
3. PROPORTIONALITY AND THE "WAR ON DRUGS"
The Comparison Paradox: Kelly Patch points out a glaring inconsistency: Harmelin got Life Without Parole for drug possession, but if he had shot two cops in a panic, he might have been eligible for parole in 10 years.
Relativity vs. Subjectivity: Does this prove we must compare different crimes to find justice? Or does it prove Scalia is right—that comparing "apples to oranges" (drugs vs. violence) is too subjective for a judge?
4. PROPORTIONALITY AND RECIDIVISM (REPEAT OFFENDERS)
The Andrade Problem: Leandro Andrade received 50 years to life for stealing $150 in videotapes because of his "third strike."
Justification for Enhancement:
Utilitarian: The person wasn't deterred the first time; we must "up the ante."
Retributive (Youngjae Lee): A recidivist is more "culpable" because they had a specific duty to avoid re-offending after their first conviction and failed it.
The Dissent's Warning: Justice Souter argued that if 50 years for videotapes isn't "grossly disproportionate," then the Eighth Amendment is essentially a "dead letter."
5. OTHER CONTEXTS: MONEY VS. LIBERTY
The Double Standard: The Supreme Court is much more protective of property than liberty.
Punitive Damages (Civil): Courts use Exacting (De Novo) Review—they don't defer to the state. They strike down awards if a "more modest" amount would work.
Prison Sentences (Criminal): Courts use Deferential Review—they almost always defer to the legislature unless the sentence is "grossly" shocking.
The Kennedy Defense: Kennedy argues civil cases need more review because they lack the procedural safeguards (like "beyond a reasonable doubt") of criminal trials.
6. REVIEW OUTSIDE THE U.S. SUPREME COURT
Eighth Amendment "Wins": While rare, some state courts use the Federal 8th Amendment to strike down "shocking" sentences (e.g., 100 years for a first-time child porn offender with no contact, or 25 years for a technical registration error).
State Constitutions (The "Saving Grace"): State courts often interpret their own constitutions more strictly than the US Supreme Court.
Example: Michigan's Constitution says "cruel or unusual" (disjunctive), which allowed the Michigan Supreme Court to strike down the very law the US Supreme Court upheld in Harmelin.
The "Proportionality to Nature" Clause: Some state constitutions (Indiana, Oregon) explicitly require penalties to be "proportioned to the nature of the offense," giving judges a much stronger "hook" to lower sentences.
7. PROPORTIONALITY AND CULPABILITY
The Transition: This note bridges the gap to the next chapter. It asks: Even if we agree a crime is "grave," is it ever fair to punish someone who isn't "morally blameworthy" (e.g., someone with a mental illness or a mistake of fact)?
The Core Question: Is culpability a mandatory prerequisite for any punishment, or can we punish just for "harm" alone?
LOCKYER V. ANDRADE (538 U.S. 63)
Facts
Leandro Andrade stole approximately $150 worth of videotapes from Kmart in two separate incidents. Under California’s "Three Strikes" law, because he had prior non-violent convictions (burglary and drug transportation), these petty thefts were elevated to felonies. He was sentenced to two consecutive terms of 25 years to life (effectively 50 years to life).
Issue: Is a 50-years-to-life sentence for stealing $150 worth of videotapes "grossly disproportionate" under the Eighth Amendment?
Rule: The "Gross Disproportionality" principle (from Harmelin) applies, but it is a "narrow" principle that gives great deference to state legislatures.
Application: The Court (5-4) held that the sentence was not an "unreasonable application" of clearly established federal law. They deferred to California's legislative choice to punish recidivists (repeat offenders) more harshly than first-time offenders to achieve deterrence and incapacitation.
Conclusion: The sentence was Upheld.
IMPORTANT NOTES FROM THE READING
1. Retribution vs. Utility: If the goal is utility (deterrence), almost any sentence can be "proportionate" if it stops crime. If the goal is retribution, the sentence must match the moral culpability of the offender.
2. Institutional Competence: Justice Kennedy argues legislatures are better at deciding penological goals because they reflect the "collective wisdom" and local conditions (e.g., the Detroit drug epidemic).
3. The "War on Drugs" Paradox: As noted by Kelly Patch, if Harmelin had shot two police officers in a panic, he might have been eligible for parole in 10 years, whereas "mere possession" got him life forever. This highlights the "relative" nature of proportionality.
4. Civil vs. Criminal: Ironically, the Supreme Court provides more protection to corporations facing punitive damages (money) than to individuals facing prison. The Court uses "exacting de novo review" for money but "grossly disproportionate deference" for liberty.
5. State Constitutional Safeguards: Because the federal Eighth Amendment is a "dead letter" (hard to win), many defendants turn to state constitutions. For example, the Michigan Supreme Court in Bullock struck down the very law the US Supreme Court upheld in Harmelin, using the Michigan state constitution’s "cruel or unusual" language.
6. Moral Panic: Critics argue that harsh laws (like California's Three Strikes) are often born of "moral panic" (e.g., the Polly Klaas murder) and immediate emotion rather than careful legislative reflection.
HYMAN GROSS: A THEORY OF CRIMINAL JUSTICE (1979)
The "Innocence" Analogy: Gross argues that punishing someone more than they deserve is logically the same as punishing an innocent person.
Punishment Without Guilt: Any "excess" sentence beyond what is deserved for the specific conduct lacks a legal and moral basis. In short, the "extra" years are years served without guilt.
H.L.A. HART & A. HONORÉ: CAUSATION IN THE LAW (1985)
Complex Deterrence: From a utilitarian (deterrence-based) perspective, the scale of punishment depends on three variables:
Harm Prevention: More harmful crimes require more severe repression.
Degree of Temptation: If a crime is easy or tempting to commit, the law must provide a heavier penalty to "counterbalance" that temptation.
Dangerousness: A crime may reveal a character so dangerous that a longer sentence is needed for incapacitation or reform, regardless of the specific act's harm.
A.C. EWING: A STUDY OF PUNISHMENT (1943)
The "Martyr" Effect: If punishment is too severe for a minor crime, the public stops focusing on the criminal's guilt and starts focusing on the criminal's suffering.
Discrediting the Law: Excessive severity makes the offender look like a "hero" or a victim of a cruel system, which actually makes the public less likely to respect the law.
Psychological Barrier to Repentance: Instead of feeling sorry for their crime (repentance), an overly punished offender feels only "self-pity, despair, or bitterness."
H.L.A. HART: LAW, LIBERTY AND MORALITY (1963)
Utilitarian vs. Justice Grounds: Hart notes two reasons to match punishment to "wickedness":
Utility: If the law ignores common moral sense, it confuses people and brings the legal system into disrepute.
Fairness: Principles of justice require treating morally similar offenses alike and morally different offenses differently.
The "Quantum" Distinction: Hart makes a vital 1L distinction: The justification for having a punishment system is to prevent harm, but the quantum (amount) of punishment should be determined by moral gravity.
ANDREW VON HIRSCH & ANDREW ASHWORTH: PROPORTIONATE SENTENCING (2005)
Criticism of Utilitarianism: They argue that if proportionality is only used to prevent crime, it isn't a "real" ethical rule—it will be ignored whenever a harsher sentence seems "more efficient" for crime control.
Punishment as Censure: Punishment is not just a "hit" or a "cost"; it is a formal expression of disapprobation(blame).
The Requirement of Justice: Because punishment carries a moral "stigma," justice requires that the level of blame matches the level of "reprehensibleness." It is a matter of fairness, not just efficiency.

Martin v. State
Facts
The defendant, Martin, was at his home when he became intoxicated. While in this state, police officers arrived at his residence, arrested him, and forcibly took him onto a public highway. While on the highway, Martin manifested his intoxication through loud and profane language.
He was subsequently convicted of being drunk on a public highway. Martin appealed the conviction, arguing that he did not go onto the highway voluntarily.
Issue 
Whether a defendant can be convicted of public drunkenness under Alabama law when their presence in a public place was the result of being forcibly and involuntarily moved there by law enforcement.
Rule 
A criminal conviction for public drunkenness requires an appearing in public while intoxicated. Legal precedent dictates that such an appearance must be a voluntary act (actus reus). A person cannot be held criminally liable for a condition or location that was forced upon them by the state without their consent or volition.
Application 
In this case, Martin was intoxicated within the private confines of his own home. He did not elect to enter the public highway; instead, officers arrested him and physically transported him to the road.
Because the statute's requirement of "appearing" implies a voluntary movement or choice to be in public, the facts do not support the charge. The officers' actions—forcibly carrying a person from a private residence to a public space—cannot be attributed to the defendant as a voluntary criminal act. Without the element of volition, the state fails to meet the burden of proving the necessary conduct for the offense.
Conclusion 
No. The court reversed the conviction, holding that a voluntary appearance in public is a necessary element of the offense which was not present in these circumstances.
1. The Rationale of Martin v. State
Intuitive Fairness vs. Statutory Text: While the reversal feels "fair," the court’s reliance on the "plain terms" of the statute is debatable.
Textual Silence: The Alabama statute did not explicitly use the word "voluntary"; the court read this requirement into the word "appear."
The "Found" Hypothetical: If the statute were re-worded to punish anyone "found" in a public place while drunk, the same moral problem remains: should a person be liable for a status or location they did not choose?
2. The Actus Reus Requirement
Fundamental Principle: Criminal liability generally requires an actus reus—a voluntary physical act prohibited by law.
Model Penal Code §2.01(1): This provision (often cited as the gold standard) states that a person is not guilty of an offense unless their liability is based on conduct that includes a voluntary act.
Background Principles: The Martin court likely applied this unwritten "background principle" of the common law to interpret the specific Alabama statute.
3. Testing the Limits of Voluntariness
The "Perry Passive" Comparison: Unlike a person forcibly drugged and moved, Martin did voluntarily drink and did voluntarily use profanity once in public.
Partial Voluntariness: The legal question is whether all elements of the crime must be voluntary. If Martin chose to scream in public, does it matter that he didn't choose to be in public?
4. Contemporary Applications & Jurisdictional Splits
Courts today struggle with how to apply the Martin rule to different scenarios:
Drugs in Jail (Low vs. Eaton):
California (Low): Upheld conviction because the defendant had a "clear opportunity" to admit to the drugs before entering the jail.
Washington (Eaton): Reversed conviction, finding that the defendant had no real choice other than to incriminate himself on a separate charge, making the "act" involuntary.
Homelessness and "Status" (Jones vs. City of Boise):
Conduct vs. Being: Some argue that laws against sleeping in public punish "conduct" (sleeping).
The 9th Circuit View: If a person has no access to shelter, sleeping is an "unavoidable consequence of being human." Punishing it is seen as punishing "status" rather than a voluntary act.
Forced Return to Jurisdiction (Macias):
The 2nd Circuit applied Martin to reverse a conviction for being "found" in the U.S. when the defendant was forcibly returned by Canadian officials in handcuffs.
5. Defining the "Voluntary" Act
MPC §1.13(2) Definition: Defines an "act" simply as a "bodily movement whether voluntary or involuntary."
Control vs. Condition: This raises the question of whether acts committed while intoxicated (like Martin’s profanity) are truly "involuntary" if the person lost self-control due to their own prior voluntary drinking.
People v. Newton
Facts
Huey Newton, a co-founder of the Black Panther Party, was involved in a roadside altercation with two police officers. During the struggle, one officer was killed and the other was wounded. Newton himself was shot in the abdomen. Newton testified that after being shot, he entered a "reflex band" or state of unconsciousness due to shock and pain, and he claimed to have no memory of the shooting or the events immediately following his injury.
Newton was convicted of voluntary manslaughter. He appealed on the grounds that the trial court failed to instruct the jury on the defense of unconsciousness, which would negate the voluntary act requirement of the crime.
Issue 
Should a trial court provide a jury instruction on the defense of unconsciousness when there is evidence that the defendant acted while in a state of "reflex" or automatism?
Rule 
Under California law (and general criminal law principles), unconsciousness is a complete defense to a criminal charge. This is because a person who is unconscious is not capable of committing a voluntary act (actus reus). Unconsciousness does not necessarily mean a coma or a stupor; it can exist where the subject physically acts but is not conscious of those acts (e.g., somnambulism, fugue states, or physical trauma/shock).
Application 
The court applied the principle that a defendant is entitled to an instruction on any defense for which there is "substantial evidence." Newton provided medical testimony and personal testimony suggesting that the abdominal gunshot wound could have triggered a state of unconsciousness or "shock."
The court noted that if the jury believed Newton was in such a state, he could not be held criminally liable because his bodily movements were not the product of a conscious, volitional mind. The lack of a jury instruction on this point deprived the jury of the ability to consider whether the actus reus—the voluntary act—was actually present.
Conclusion
 Yes. The court reversed the conviction. It held that where evidence of unconsciousness is produced, the trial court must instruct the jury that such a state, if found to exist, constitutes a complete defense to the crime because it negates the requirement of a voluntary act.
NOTES FROM THE NEWTON CASE
1. Rationale of the Voluntary Act Requirement
Deterrence: The law cannot deter movements that a person cannot physically control.
Social Condemnation: Convicting someone for involuntary movements undermines personal security and the legitimacy of the legal system.
Correction vs. Therapy: Involuntary actors are not "criminals" in need of correction/punishment; they are public health or safety problems requiring therapy or custodial commitment.
2. Distinguishing Voluntary vs. Involuntary Acts
MPC Approach (§2.01): Defines voluntariness indirectly by listing involuntary acts:
Reflexes or convulsions.
Bodily movements during unconsciousness or sleep (somnambulism).
Conduct during hypnosis (specific to MPC).
Any movement that is not a product of the effort or determination of the actor.
The "Legal" vs. "Ordinary" Definition: * In legal terms, "involuntary" is much narrower than in common speech.
Bratty v. Attorney-General: Clarified that "involuntary" (automatism) means the mind has no control over the muscles (spasm, reflex, concussion).
Excluded from Involuntariness: * Irresistible impulses ("I couldn't help myself").
Forgetfulness (amnesia).
Unintentional consequences ("I didn't mean to drive dangerously").
Involuntary manslaughter (the result is unintended, but the act of driving was voluntary).
3. Voluntary Acts vs. Blameworthy Acts
Voluntariness is a Floor, Not a Ceiling: A voluntary act is a necessary requirement for liability, but not a sufficient one.
The "Misfire" Distinction (Prof. J.G. Murphy):
Group A (Accident/Duress/Mistake): The person acted, but we mitigate or excuse them because they lacked the intent (mens rea) or were coerced.
Group B (Convulsions/Reflex): We don't even talk about "excuse" because no human action occurred at all. (Comparing an involuntary actor to a "rock falling on a head").
Legal Consequences of the Label:
Burden of Proof: The prosecution must prove a voluntary act beyond a reasonable doubt as an element of the crime. This burden cannot be shifted to the defendant.
Affirmative Defenses: Legislatures can shift the burden of proving excuses (like insanity) to the defendant.
Disposition: An acquittal for lack of a voluntary act leads to immediate discharge (freedom). An acquittal by reason of insanity often leads to civil commitment.
4. Problematic Categories
A. Habitual Acts
MPC View: Habits (like reaching for a phone or using "filler" words) are voluntary.
Rationale: Even if done without conscious thought, they are still considered a product of the actor's "effort or determination."
Distinction: Reaching for a phone is not a "reflex" (an instinctive physical response to a stimulus like a knee-jerk).
B. Hypnosis
MPC §2.01(2)(b): Specifically lists hypnosis as involuntary due to the subject’s "dependency and helplessness."
Jurisdictional Conflict: Most states do not follow the MPC here because of scientific disagreement over whether a hypnotized person truly loses control.
C. Somnambulism (Sleepwalking)
The Cogdon Case (Australia): A mother killed her daughter with an axe while dreaming of spiders/soldiers.
Result: Acquitted. The act was legally "not her act at all."
Disposition Problem: Since it was an actus reus failure, she was released entirely.
Contemporary Issues:
"Sexsomnia": Used as a defense in rape cases (successful in Canada via R. v. Luedecke; largely unsuccessful in the U.S.).
Expert Testimony: Courts are increasingly requiring/expecting expert brain-wave studies to support these defenses (Liao v. Junious).
D. Epilepsy and the "Time-Framing" Debate
People v. Decina: An epileptic driver had a seizure and killed four people.
The "Prior Voluntary Act": The court found him liable not because of the seizure, but because he voluntarily chose to drive knowing he was subject to seizures.
The Time-Framing Conflict:
Mark Kelman: Argues "time-framing" is arbitrary. Why didn't the court in Martin (the drunk guy) use an earlier voluntary act (drinking) to find him liable for being in public?
Michael Moore: Argues there is no "choice" in time-framing. If there is any point where a voluntary act + mens rea + causation align, the defendant is liable. In Martin, the earlier drinking didn't "proximately cause" the police to drag him into the street.
Concurrence. It ensures that the state cannot just find any bad act and any bad thought and mash them together to get a conviction.
1. The Core Definition of Concurrence
Simultaneous Requirement: For a crime to occur, the Actus Reus (voluntary act) and the Mens Rea (culpable state of mind) must happen at the exact same time.
The "Link" Requirement: Concurrence acts as the glue connecting three distinct elements:
Actus Reus: The voluntary act or culpable omission.
Mens Rea: The specific state of mind required by the statute.
Result: The actual harm caused (if the statute requires a result, like death in a homicide case).
Preventing "Life-Long" Liability: Without this requirement, the state could argue a defendant is liable for a crime simply because they voluntarily "got out of bed" or "ate breakfast" on the day of an accident.
2. Case Study: State v. 5 Star Feedlot, Inc. (Colo. 2021)
The Facts:
The Setup: A feedlot operated wastewater ponds for years.
The Event: A massive, "once-in-a-half-century" rainstorm occurred.
The Result: Ponds overflowed, wastewater traveled miles, and 15,000 fish died.
The Charge: Violating criminal statutes against killing wildlife without authorization.
The Court’s Plurality Ruling:
The state failed to prove a voluntary act that caused the fish to die.
Time One (Operation): Running the ponds for years was voluntary and lawful, but it wasn't the act that killed the fish.
Time Two (The Storm): The actual discharge of wastewater was an Act of God (the rainstorm), not a voluntary act performed by the feedlot employees.
Conclusion: Because the discharge wasn't voluntary, the actus reus requirement was not met.
3. Comparing 5 Star to People v. Decina
The text asks why 5 Star isn't treated exactly like the epileptic driver in Decina. Both involve a voluntary act followed by an involuntary disaster.
The Comparison Framework:
Decina: Voluntary Act (Decina starts the car)  Involuntary Event (Seizure)  Harm (Death).
5 Star: Voluntary Act (Feedlot operates ponds)  Involuntary Event (Storm)  Harm (Fish die).
Why the results differ (The Concurrence Check):
Culpable Awareness (Mens Rea): * In Decina, the defendant knew he had seizures and chose to drive anyway. He had a culpable state of mind (recklessness/negligence) at the moment he performed the voluntary act of starting the car.
In 5 Star, the feedlot was operating lawfully for years. There was no evidence they had a culpable state of mind regarding the fish at the time they were merely "operating the ponds."
The Causal Link: * In Decina, the choice to drive was the proximate cause of the danger.
In 5 Star, the "once-in-a-half-century" storm was an intervening force that broke the link between the lawful operation of the ponds and the death of the fish.

William Blackstone (Commentaries)
The Idea: The "Temporal Tribunal" Argument.
The Logic: Human courts are "temporal" (earthly) and cannot "search the heart" or "fathom the intentions of the mind."
The "Why": Intent can only be demonstrated through outward actions.

The Idea: The "Intolerable Society" Argument.
The Logic: If the law punished "sinful thoughts," every human being would be a criminal.
The "Why": Most of our lives would be spent trying and punishing each other for things that can never be truly proven.
Potential Objection: Expanding the law this wide makes it "utterly intolerable" and practically impossible to manage.
G. Dworkin & G. Blumenfeld (Punishments for Intentions)
The Idea: The "Mental Continuum" Argument.
The Logic: It is extremely difficult to distinguish between fantasizing, wishing, and intending.
The "Why": People often change their minds. If we punish the "intention" stage, we ignore the fact that the person might have opted out.
Potential Objection: Constant surveillance of our own thoughts would "stultify the emotional life" and create constant, unnecessary guilt.
Glanville Williams (Criminal Law: The General Part)
The Idea: The "Irresolute Actor" Argument.
The Logic: A "fixed intention" is hard to prove without behavior.
The "Why": Many people have "criminal" thoughts but are too indecisive (irresolute) to ever actually act on them.
Potential Objection: It is undesirable to punish someone for a mental state that would have likely never resulted in an actual crime.
Abraham Goldstein (Yale Law Journal)
The Idea: The "Social Harm and Deterrence" Argument.
The Logic: The act requirement ensures that an "identifiable occurrence" has actually happened.
The "Why": Once a person acts, we know they were not deterred by the threat of prison. Until they act, they might still be deterred.
Potential Objection: Without an act, there is no way to minimize "multiple prosecutions" for the same wandering thought.
Summary
If you are asked why the "to-do list" arsonist isn't a criminal:
Evidentiary: We can't "read minds" (Blackstone).
Psychological: We need the freedom to have "bad" thoughts without being a "bad" person (Dworkin/Blumenfeld).
Locus Poenitentiae: We want to give people the chance to change their minds before they do something they can't take back (Williams/Goldstein).
B. OMISSIONS
Jones v. United States
United States Court of Appeals, District of Columbia Circuit
Facts
The defendant, Jones, was living in an apartment with a friend, Shirley Green, and Green's two young children. One of the children, an infant, died of starvation and neglect. Jones was charged with involuntary manslaughter.
At trial, there was conflicting evidence regarding whether Jones had a legal obligation to care for the child. Some evidence suggested that Jones was paid by Green to care for the children, while other evidence suggested that Jones was merely a houseguest. The trial court's instructions to the jury did not clearly state that for Jones to be found guilty, the prosecution had to prove she had a legal duty to act, rather than just a moral one. Jones was convicted and appealed.
Issue 
Can a defendant be held criminally liable for a failure to act (an omission) if the prosecution does not prove the existence of a specific legal duty to perform that act?
Rule 
Generally, there is no criminal liability for an omission (failure to act) unless there is a legal duty to act. A moral obligation is insufficient. A legal duty can arise in four specific situations:
Statute: Where a statute imposes a duty (e.g., filing taxes).
Status Relationship: Certain relationships (e.g., parent to child, spouse to spouse).
Contract: Where one has assumed a contractual duty to care for another.
Voluntary Assumption of Care: Where one voluntarily assumes the care of another and secludes the helpless person so as to prevent others from rendering aid.
Application 
The court applied the principle that criminal liability for "negative" conduct (omissions) is the exception, not the rule. In this case, the jury was never told that they must first find that Jones owed a legal duty of care to the infant before they could find her guilty of manslaughter.
Because the evidence was disputed—specifically whether there was a contract for paid childcare or a voluntary assumption of care that secluded the child—the existence of a legal duty was a question of fact for the jury. Without a clear instruction that a legal duty (not just a moral one) was a necessary element of the actus reus, the conviction could not stand.
Conclusion 
No. The court reversed the conviction and remanded for a new trial. The court held that the failure to instruct the jury on the necessity of finding a legal duty was a plain error affecting the defendant's substantial rights.
NOTES ON THE JONES CASE 
1. Finding a Legal Duty
General Position: Under Anglo-American law, you are not criminally liable for failing to act unless a specific statute requires it, or another area of law (like Tort law) imposes a duty.
Model Penal Code §2.01(3): Strictly limits liability for omissions to two scenarios:
The law defining the offense explicitly makes the omission a crime.
A duty to perform the omitted act is "otherwise imposed by law" (e.g., parental duty, contracts, etc.).
Key Takeaway: A moral obligation to help is never enough for criminal liability; it must be a recognized legal duty.
2. Omissions and Homicide
Involuntary Manslaughter: This is the most common charge for omissions (as seen in Jones) because the defendant usually lacks a specific "intent to kill," but is criminally negligent.
Murder via Omission: A failure to act can be elevated to murder if specific mental states are present:
The defendant refuses aid with the intent to cause death.
The defendant has full knowledge that the person will die without their aid.
Commonwealth v. Pestinikas (1992): A landmark example where a defendant was convicted of third-degree murder for letting a 92-year-old starve after contractually agreeing to provide food, knowing the victim had no other options.
3. Interpreting the Scope of Duty
Resistance to Liability: Even when a duty exists (via statute or common law), there is often significant social and judicial resistance to punishing people for "not doing something."
Contextual Interpretation: Because the law generally favors individual liberty, courts often interpret the "scope" of a duty narrowly, even when a precedent might allow for a broader interpretation.
Pope v. State
Maryland Court of Appeals
Facts
The defendant, Joyce Pope, provided temporary shelter to a mother, Melissa Norris, and her three-month-old infant. While at Pope's residence, Norris underwent a "religious hallucination" and began a protracted physical assault on the child. Pope was present during the entire episode, witnessing the infant being shaken and struck repeatedly over several hours.
Despite the prolonged battery, Pope failed to intervene, failed to summon emergency medical services, and failed to report the incident to law enforcement. The infant eventually succumbed to the injuries. Pope was charged and convicted as a principal in the second degree to child abuse and misprision of felony. The State’s theory of liability rested on an omission: that Pope had a legal duty to intervene as she had assumed "temporary care or custody" of the child within her own home.
Issue Whether a homeowner/bystander who witnesses a parent abusing a child at the homeowner's residence qualifies as a "person responsible for the supervision of a minor" such that a legal duty to act arises, the breach of which constitutes criminal omission.
Rule Under the Maryland child abuse statute and common law principles regarding nonfeasance:
Actus Reus by Omission: Criminal liability for a failure to act requires the existence of a legal duty—not merely a moral one.
Statutory Class: The duty to act in child abuse cases is restricted to a defined class: parents, adoptive parents, or those with "permanent or temporary care, custody, or responsibility for supervision."
The "Good Samaritan" Limitation: Absent a specific status relationship, contractual obligation, or statutory mandate, a mere bystander (stranger) has no duty to rescue.
Application The Court of Appeals analyzed the statutory construction of the child abuse law. It determined that Pope did not fall within the enumerated class of persons. Although the abuse occurred in her home, the child's natural motherwas present and maintained actual custody throughout the incident.
The court rejected the State's argument that Pope had "sequestrated" the child or assumed a de facto parental role. Because the mother was present and in control of the child's person, the legal responsibility for supervision had not shifted to Pope. Pope remained a mere onlooker. While her passivity was "morally as well as socially reprehensible," the court held that criminal statutes must be strictly construed. Since she was not a member of the class of persons to whom the law delegated a duty of care, her failure to intervene did not satisfy the actus reus element of the offense.
Conclusion Reversed. The court held that Pope had no legal duty to act; therefore, her conviction for child abuse based on an omission was legally unsustainable. The misprision of felony conviction was also vacated as that offense was not recognized under Maryland common law at the time.

1. Bystander Indifference & Legislative Trends
The Catalyst Cases:
New Bedford Gang Rape (MA): Reports of patrons cheering during a rape led legislatures to consider mandatory reporting laws.
Kitty Genovese (NY, 1964): The classic "apathy" case where a woman was stabbed over 35 minutes while neighbors allegedly did nothing.
Revisionist History: Later investigations (Lemann, 2014) showed Genovese accounts were exaggerated; some neighbors called police and one held her as she died.
Modern Data: CCTV studies in Britain/Netherlands suggest bystanders actually intervene in 9 out of 10 public conflicts, and more bystanders often increase the chance of help.
Rhode Island Law: Enacted a misdemeanor for failing to report a rape occurring in one's presence.
Opposition to Duty-to-Aid Laws:
Privacy Concerns: Rape Crisis Centers worry about victim privacy.
Constitutional/Practical Issues: ACLU and legal experts (Arthur Miller) argue these may be as ineffective as jaywalking laws, though they provide "legal effect to a moral principle."
2. Psychological Explanations for Non-Action (Prof. Daniel Yeager)
Physical Fear: Real or imagined danger and fear of retaliation from the assailant.
Lack of Preparation: Bystanders lack a "plan or rehearsal" and struggle to select the right intervention quickly.
The "Bystander Effect":
Diffusion of Responsibility: The more people present, the less individual responsibility each person feels.
Pluralistic Ignorance: Seeing others do nothing leads a bystander to conclude there is no real danger.
The "Choice of Nightmares":
Option A (Do Nothing): Leads to empathic distress, guilt, and public shame.
Option B (Intervene): Risks physical retaliation, ridicule from other bystanders, being mistaken for the perpetrator by police, or becoming entangled in a long legal process as a witness.
3. Arguments For and Against Criminalization
Pro-Criminalization (Yeager): Argues for a formal legal duty for bystanders to assist those in peril.
Anti-Criminalization (Stewart/Guiora):
Critiques include the fear of "legislating morality" or creating a "nanny state."
Potential for police and prosecutors to abuse the discretion of who to charge for "not doing enough."
Argues that making failure to assist illegal doesn't actually help the victims in real-time.
4. Exploitation of Peril (The "Benefit" Problem)
Financial Benefit: News photographers may document an accident or assault before calling for help to secure better footage/profit. This is currently legal in almost all states.
Intangible/Cruel Benefit:
Steubenville Rape Case: Onlookers texted jokes and posted videos/photos to YouTube rather than intervening.
Jamel Dunn Case (FL, 2017): Five teenagers recorded and mocked a disabled man as he drowned in a pond. They joked while he died and posted the video to YouTube.
Legal Reality: * If a bystander cheers or helps, they are accomplices.
If they merely observe, record, and mock, they face no criminal liability in most states (unless a specific state statute like Ohio's requires reporting a felony).
The Moral Gap: There is a growing call for "Good Samaritan" laws specifically targeting those who record life-threatening incidents for gain or amusement instead of summoning help.
John Kleinig analyzes the divide between Continental European law (“Good Samaritan")
 (which criminalizes the failure to be a " Good Samaritan")  and Anglo-American law (which generally does not).
Below are the detailed notes on the arguments, the key proponents, the objections, and Kleinig's final assessment.
The Global Divide
Continental European Law: Since the Russian Code of 1845, most European countries make the failure to rescue a criminal offense.
Anglo-American Law: Remains the "glaring exception." The law distinguishes between "active benevolence" (conscience) and "legal duty" (compulsion).
I. Proponents of a Duty to Rescue
1. Jeremy Bentham (Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation)
The Argument: Bentham poses a rhetorical question: Why shouldn't it be a legal duty to save another from "mischief" if it can be done without prejudice (harm) to oneself?
Logic: He suggests that there is no satisfactory moral or logical answer to justify the law's current silence on this.
2. John Stuart Mill (On Liberty / Social Policy)
The Argument: A person is "justly accountable" for injury caused by inaction just as much as action.
Specific Acts: Mill argues society can rightfully compel:
Saving a fellow creature's life.
Interposing to protect the defenseless against ill-usage.
Logic: If it is "obviously a man's duty" to do something, he should be legally responsible for not doing it. Failing to aid is a form of harming another.
II. The Opponent: Lord Macaulay
Lord Macaulay (Notes on the Indian Penal Code)
The Objection: Macaulay famously criticized the idea of making "homicide by omission" a general offense.
The "Line-Drawing" Problem: He argues that while some omissions are clearly criminal (a jailer starving a prisoner), others are not (a traveler failing to warn someone of a high river).
The Difficulty of Definition:
How rich must a man be before he is legally required to give alms to a beggar?
If it takes 1,000 rupees to save a life, must the man pay? If not, where is the limit?
The "Inconvenience" Counter-Argument: Macaulay attacks Livingston’s Criterion (which says aid is mandatory if there is "no personal danger or pecuniary loss").
Example: A surgeon who refuses to travel to save a life because he is about to leave for Europe to see his family. It isn't "dangerous" or "expensive" to go, but it is extremely inconvenient. Macaulay argues the surgeon is no "murderer" and the law cannot effectively distinguish his "badness" from a rich man refusing an anna (a small coin).
Conclusion: Omissions should only be punishable if they are "on other grounds, illegal" (meaning a pre-existing legal duty already exists).
III. Analysis of Objections
Objection Category
Basis of the Objection
Legal Draftsmanship
It is impossible to draft a formula that covers all circumstances and different types of men.
Livingston's Criterion
Failing to aid only when there is "no danger/loss" is too narrow; it ignores the factor of "extreme inconvenience."
Diminished Freedom
Anglo-American culture is steeped in individualism. The fear is that these laws would encroach on the liberty to be left alone ("You owe me nothing; I owe you nothing").
IV. Kleinig’s Final Assessment (The Author’s Position)
Refuting the "Draftsmanship" Argument: Kleinig argues that courts deal with "judgments of reasonableness" every day. "Reasonableness" is the "bread and butter" of the law; therefore, drafting a statute requiring "reasonable steps" to give aid is entirely possible.
Addressing the Fear of Sacrifice: Kleinig points out that in countries that have Good Samaritan laws, they do not demand "unreasonable sacrifices" of the rescuer.
Challenging "Extreme Individualism": Kleinig identifies the core of the opposition as a "hysteria" regarding individual liberty.
The Author's Ultimate View: He sides closer to Mill and Bentham. He concludes that when the pursuit of one's own interest (like "wanting to stay home") results in a causal factor in another's harm (death/injury), there is a strong, justifiable case for state interference.
NOTES ON KLEINIG’S VIEW:
1. Statutory Duties to Rescue in the U.S.
General Rule: Most U.S. jurisdictions do not have a duty to rescue.
The "Good Samaritan" States: * Minnesota, Rhode Island, and Vermont: The only states with broad criminal penalties for failing to aid someone in "grave physical harm" or an "emergency."
Vermont’s Standard (Vt. Stat. Ann. tit. 12, §519): Requires assistance if it can be done without danger to self or interference with duties to others. The penalty is a minor $100 fine.
Limited Duty States (Florida, Hawaii, Wisconsin): A duty to aid exists only if the peril is caused by a crime.
Florida: Duty is limited specifically to witnesses of sexual battery.
Criticism of the "Crime-Only" Trigger: Critics argue the justification for rescue (saving a life) is just as strong in natural disasters or accidents as it is during a crime.
Enforcement: In all six states, violations are only misdemeanors or petty misdemeanors, and actual prosecutions are extremely rare.
2. The European Contrast
Long-standing Tradition: European law has historically enforced a duty to aid via the criminal code.
Germany (Article 323c): Punishes failure to help in cases of accident or "common danger" with up to one year in prison, provided help is needed and safe to provide.
France (Art. 63(2)): Added in 1945; carries a much stiffer penalty of up to five years' imprisonment. Unlike the U.S., France enforces this frequently, particularly against motorists who fail to stop at accident scenes.
3. Escalating Liability: From Misdemeanor to Manslaughter
The "Omission as Act" Theory: While violating a Good Samaritan statute is usually a misdemeanor, it can serve as the legal duty required for a homicide charge.
State v. Martinez (Hawaii, 2003): * The defendant failed to get medical help for a child being beaten by his girlfriend.
Even though the "duty to aid" statute only carried a petty misdemeanor penalty, the court upheld a reckless manslaughter conviction (10 years in prison).
Logic: The failure to act (omission) plus a legal duty (the statute) equals an affirmative act of killing when the defendant is aware of the danger.
4. Bystander Intervention: Law vs. Social Norms
The "Mind Your Own Business" Norm: Many scholars believe American culture is too resistant to "interfering" for statutes to be effective.
Empirical Skepticism (Prof. David Hyman): Data suggests that passing duty-to-rescue laws does not actually change the number of rescues or accidental deaths in a state.
The "Campus SaVE Act" Approach: Rather than using the "heavy hand" of the law, the federal government requires colleges to use bystander intervention programs (training students how to safely intervene in bullying or sexual assault).
The "Norm-Signaling" Argument (Prof. Sarah Swan): * Argues that educational programs aren't enough because the "no-duty-to-rescue" rule sends a powerful message that staying out of others' business is legally correct.
Conclusion: The criminal law must first signal that intervention is a duty before social norms will truly shift.
5. The Duty to Report (Misprision of Felony)
Historical Context: "Misprision of felony" (merely failing to report a crime) has been largely abolished or ignored in Anglo-American common law (Pope v. State).
Modern Statutory Duties: * Federal Law (18 U.S.C. §4): Requires active concealment; simply staying silent is not enough for a conviction.
State Reporting Laws: Nine states require witnesses to report serious crimes; 19 states require reporting for specific crimes.
Mandatory Reporters: Every jurisdiction requires certain professionals (doctors, clergy, teachers, etc.) to report suspected child abuse.
Arguments Against Mandatory Reporting:
Victim Privacy: Reporting sexual offenses against the victim’s will might violate their autonomy.
Fear of Retaliation: Witnesses in high-crime areas may face physical danger if forced to testify.
Autonomy: The right to remain uninvolved in the state's "police work."
Drug Offense Exception: There is a concern that a general duty to report would unfairly burden those in high-crime neighborhoods, effectively forcing them to choose between being a "snitch" (and risking death) or being a criminal.
1. Family Status Relationships
The Status Relationship Rule: A legal duty to aid is well settled where a "certain status relationship" exists between parties.
Recognized Duties:
Parents to Minor Children: Mandatory duty to aid and protect.
Spouses: Reciprocal duty to aid each other.
Excluded Relationships: Traditionally, no automatic duty is imposed on siblings, parents of adult children, or adult children of elderly parents.
Statutory/Case Law Exceptions:
Adult Children with Disabilities: In Kentucky, statutory law imposes a duty on parents to provide for "wholly dependent" adult children with permanent disabilities (Bartley v. Commonwealth).
Vulnerable Elderly Parents: In Missouri, an adult child who assumes responsibility for an elderly parent who cannot care for herself has a duty to provide care (State v. Gargus).
2. De Facto and Functional Family Members
The Traditional View (People v. Beardsley): The court famously held that a man had no legal duty to aid his mistress when she overdosed in his home, as no formal legal marriage existed.
Criticism: Graham Hughes described the Beardsley morality as "smug, ignorant and vindictive," and modern courts often call it "outmoded".
Functional Equivalent Standard:
Stepparents: In New York, a stepmother was held legally responsible for a child's care because she acted as the "functional equivalent of a parent" (People v. Carroll).
Adult Co-residents: In Kentucky, any adult living with a child who assumes "substantial responsibility" for food, shelter, and protection owes a duty (Staples v. Commonwealth).
The Conservative Approach (State v. Miranda): The Connecticut Supreme Court initially upheld a duty for a live-in boyfriend to protect a child but overruled itself later.
Plurality Reasoning: Extending liability case-by-case beyond "clearly established legal categories" would be too amorphous and might discourage people from entering nontraditional family arrangements for fear of criminal liability.
Proposed Reforms: Dan Markel, Jennifer M. Collins, and Ethan J. Lieb criticize reliance on formal categories and propose a "registry" where roommates or relatives could opt into reciprocal obligations of care.
3. Duty When the Parent is a Victim (Domestic Violence)
The "Calculated to Achieve Success" Standard: In Commonwealth v. Cardwell, a mother was convicted for failing to protect her daughter from sexual abuse by her husband, even though the mother was also a victim of his violence.
The Court's Stance: Taking "any step at all" is not enough; a parent must take steps "reasonably calculated to achieve success," such as reporting to authorities or leaving the home.
Feminist Critique: Dorothy E. Roberts argues that courts wrongly presume a woman's maternal obligation always outweighs her own safety, ignoring the "physical, social, and legal constraints" of battered women.
4. Creation of Peril (The Avery and Jordan Example)
The Criminal Act Rule: One who puts another in danger through an "overpowering criminal act" has a legal duty to preserve that person's life (Jones v. State).
Blame and Intent (Mens Rea): Imposing a duty here is critical because it allows the law to look at the defendant's later state of mind.
Example: If Avery accidentally knocks Jordan into the water, he may only be negligent. However, if he then chooses not to help while Jordan drowns, the omission—combined with his knowledge of certain death—can support a murder conviction.
5. Innocent Acts and Tort-Based Duties
Innocent Imperilment Principle: J.C. Smith argues that whenever a defendant's act (even if lawful and reasonable at the time) imperils another and the defendant becomes aware of it, they have a duty to take reasonable steps to prevent harm.
The Fire Reporting Case (Commonwealth v. Levesque): Defendants who accidentally started a fire but failed to report it were convicted of involuntary manslaughter because they were aware of the danger they created.
The Notice and Vagueness Debate:
NJ Supreme Court View (State v. Lisa): Dismissed an indictment against a man who failed to call 911 for his unresponsive girlfriend.
Reasoning: Civil common law principles or "emanations from a scholarly treatise" (like the Restatement of Torts) do not provide sufficient notice to justify a criminal charge.

POSSESSION 
The Legal Nature of Possession
Possession as an Act: Taking possession of an illegal item) is considered a clear affirmative act.
(e.g., buying a gun and putting it in a pocket
Possession as an Omission: If a person is unaware of an item placed in their belongings by another, liability often hinges on whether they failed to act (terminate possession) once they became aware of it.
The "Awareness" Requirement: Most courts interpret possession offenses to require that the accused be aware of having the item, even if the statute doesn't explicitly state this requirement.
Model Penal Code (MPC) §2.01(4): Possession satisfies the actus reus requirement only if the person was aware of their control for a long enough period to have been able to terminate possession.
The Knowledge Conflict: State v. Bradshaw
The Case: A commercial truck driver was convicted of possessing 77 pounds of marijuana hidden in his trailer, despite a lack of evidence that he knew the drugs were there.
The Washington Supreme Court Ruling: The court upheld the conviction, rejecting the argument that "awareness" of an item's presence is a necessary part of the legal concept of possession.
Implicit Challenges:
The "Act" Problem: If a driver is passive while drugs are hidden and then found, it is difficult to identify a relevant voluntary "act" that triggers criminal liability.
The "Omission" Problem: If liability is based on a failure to get rid of the drugs, the law is imposing a duty to act on someone who had no reason to know action was necessary.
Comparison to Failure-to-Rescue: In most jurisdictions, a bystander who is fully aware of a peril can legally ignore it (e.g., Pope); yet, under the Bradshaw approach, a person can be convicted for an omission regarding a peril they aren't even aware of.
Possession and "Should Have Known" (Constructive Knowledge)
The Suspicion Scenario: The notes pose whether a driver should be liable if they didn't know about the drugs but should have suspected something was wrong 
(e.g., seeing a suspicious loiterer or loose cargo flap).
MPC Application: Under MPC §2.01, a conviction would likely still be barred because the code requires actual awareness of control, not just a reason to suspect it.
Policy Question: The materials question whether a "should have known" standard is a fair basis for a drug-possession offense, given the high priority placed on these crimes by federal and state prosecutors.
Barber v. Superior Court 
California District Court of Appeal
Facts
Herbert Fuller underwent surgery and suffered a cardio-respiratory arrest, which resulted in severe brain damage and left him in a permanently comatose state. He was kept alive by a ventilator and intravenous (IV) tubes for hydration and nourishment. Based on medical opinions that the condition was terminal and with the consent of the family, the physicians (Drs. Barber and Nedl) first disconnected the ventilator.
When Fuller continued to breathe on his own, the doctors—again with family consent—removed the IV tubes providing hydration and nourishment. Fuller eventually died from dehydration and pneumonia. The state charged the doctors with murder and conspiracy to commit murder, arguing that removing the IV tubes was an affirmative act of killing.
Issue 
Can the removal of life-sustaining treatment (specifically IV hydration and nourishment) by a physician, with family consent, be considered an unlawful killing (murder), or is it a legally protected omission to provide further medical treatment?
Rule
Act vs. Omission: A physician’s withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment is characterized as an omission (a failure to provide further treatment) rather than an affirmative act of killing.
Duty to Act: A physician has a legal duty to provide medical treatment only as long as it is proportionate and provides a benefit to the patient.
Ineffective Treatment: There is no duty to continue treatment that has been proved to be ineffective in the opinion of the attending physicians.
Application 
The court held that the doctors did not have a "duty to provide" treatment that was useless. They determined that "medical treatment" includes the provision of IV hydration and nourishment in this context. Because the patient was in a permanent vegetative state with no hope of recovery, the treatment was deemed non-beneficial. Therefore, the doctors' failure to continue that treatment (the omission) did not constitute a breach of legal duty. Since there was no breach of duty, the omission was not "unlawful" and could not support a murder charge.
Conclusion 
The court granted the doctors' petition for a writ of prohibition, effectively dismissing the murder charges. The cessation of "heroic" life support measures is not an affirmative act but a withdrawal of treatment that no longer serves a medical purpose.
I. Theory of Medical Omission vs. Intervener Act
The Theory: The law distinguishes between a doctor withdrawing life support and a third party doing the same.
Key Proponent: Lord Goff (Airedale NHS Trust v. Bland).
The Argument:
Doctor’s Role (Omission): Discontinuing life support is legally categorized as an omission (ceasing further treatment). It is not unlawful unless it breaches a duty to the patient. The doctor is simply allowing the patient to die from a pre-existing condition.
Interloper’s Role (Act): If a malicious third party "switches off" the machine, it is an active intervention. This is because they are interfering with the life-prolonging treatment being administered by the doctor.
Case Relation: In Airedale NHS Trust v. Bland, the House of Lords held that withholding artificial feeding and antibiotics from a patient with no hope of recovery was a lawful omission because the doctor no longer had a duty to provide non-beneficial treatment.
II. Theory of Moral Equivalence in Euthanasia
The Theory: There is no significant moral or practical difference between "letting die" (passive) and "killing" (active) in the medical context.
Key Proponent: John Robertson.
The Argument:
Result Identity: From the patient's perspective, the end goal—relief of suffering and death—is identical regardless of whether the cause is active or passive.
Arbitrariness of Labels: Labeling "pulling the plug" as passive is inconsistent because it is a literal physical act, much like writing a prescription for lethal drugs.
Objections to the Theory:
Invasion vs. Choice: Opponents of assisted suicide argue that refusing treatment avoids a direct physical intrusion on the patient, whereas active euthanasia involves doing something to them.
Protection of Life: Opponents worry that removing the distinction creates a greater social threat to the respect for human life.
Case Relation: While Cruzan v. Director established a constitutional right to refuse treatment (letting die), Robertson argues this legal line is morally vulnerable because the "threats to the poor and elderly" are present in both withholding treatment and assisted suicide.
III. Theory of Mens Rea (Culpable Mental States)
The Theory: An act alone does not create a crime unless the mind is also guilty (Actus non facit reum, nisi mens sit rea).
Key Proponent: Blackstone (historical) and Justice Robert H. Jackson (modern critique).
The Argument:
Broad Sense: Mens rea is synonymous with moral fault or "vicious will". Punishment is inappropriate if a defendant lacked the "choice" to commit a wrong.
Narrow Sense: A technical requirement referring to the specific mental awareness or intention (e.g., purpose, knowledge, or awareness of risk) required by a statute.
Case/Statutory Application:
Receiving Stolen Goods: It is not criminal unless the person knows the goods are stolen.
Manslaughter: Defined by an act done with the awareness of a substantial risk.
Unlawful Assembly: Joining a group is innocent unless there is an intent to commit unlawful acts.
Critique of the Theory: Justice Jackson (Morissette v. United States) criticized the "variety, disparity and confusion" in how courts define the "elusive" mental element, leading to inconsistent legal standards.

Regina v. Cunningham
Court of Criminal Appeal
Facts
The defendant, Cunningham, broke into an empty house to steal money from a gas meter. To access the meter, he tore a gas pipe off the wall. He did not turn off the gas, which subsequently leaked through the wall into the neighboring house. The neighbor, Mrs. Wade, inhaled the gas and her life was endangered.
Cunningham was charged under the Offences Against the Person Act 1861, which made it a crime to "unlawfully and maliciously administer... any poison or other destructive or noxious thing, so as thereby to endanger... life". The trial judge instructed the jury that "malicious" meant "wicked"—implying that if he was doing something wrong (stealing), he was acting maliciously. Cunningham was convicted and appealed.
Issue 
What is the correct legal definition of "malicious" in the context of the Offences Against the Person Act? Does it require a "wicked" intent, or does it require a specific state of mind regarding the risk of harm?
Rule 
The court adopted the definition of malice provided by Professor C.S. Kenny:
An actual intention to do the particular kind of harm that was done; or
Recklessness, defined as the accused foreseeing that the particular type of harm might be done and yet going on to take the risk anyway.
Application 
The Court of Criminal Appeal ruled that the trial judge's instruction was incorrect. "Malicious" does not mean "generally wicked" or "illegal". It requires a subjective mental state.
To be guilty, Cunningham must have either intended to poison the neighbor or realized that by breaking the gas pipe, there was a risk that gas might escape and harm someone, yet he ignored that risk.
Because the jury was told they could convict him just because his act of stealing was "wicked," they never actually determined if he foresaw the risk of the gas leak harming someone.
Conclusion 
The conviction was quashed. The court established that for "malicious" crimes, the prosecution must prove the defendant personally foresaw the risk of the harm occurring.
1. The "Malice" Debate
Ordinary vs. Technical Meaning: Everyday speech equates malice with "wickedness" or "evil motive," but common law generally uses a narrower, technical definition.
The "Default" Rule: Absent clear legislative intent to the contrary, courts presume "malice" means foresight of consequences—specifically, an awareness that one's actions pose a substantial risk of causing the prohibited harm (Cunningham approach).
Case Nuance (U.S. v. Gray): While the common-law presumption is "foresight of risk," courts may adopt the "evil purpose" definition if the statute's specific structure and history demand it.
2. Specific vs. General Intent
This is a high-frequency cold-call topic. The distinction is notoriously inconsistent but generally falls into two categories:
Category
Definition
Key Example
Specific Intent
Doing an act with a specified further purpose or subjective awareness of a specific background fact.
Burglary: Breaking and entering (act) with the intent to commit a felony inside (further purpose).
General Intent
Requires only a voluntary act; the defendant must know the nature of their conduct but doesn't need a further "objective".
Battery: Intentional physical contact (act), regardless of whether you desired a specific long-term consequence.
Attendant Circumstances: In crimes like bigamy, "general intent" might mean you only need to intend the act of marrying; "specific intent" would require actual knowledge that you are already legally married to someone else.
The Trap: Just because a crime is labeled "specific intent" doesn't mean every element of the crime requires subjective awareness. For example, a burglar might be reckless about whether it is "nighttime" but still have specific intent regarding the theft.
3. Proving Intent: Inferences & Presumptions
Circumstantial Evidence: Because we can't read minds, mens rea is almost always proven indirectly through a defendant’s actions.
Mandatory Presumptions (Unconstitutional): Courts cannot tell a jury they must presume a defendant intended the "natural and probable consequences" of their acts (Francis v. Franklin). This unconstitutionally shifts the burden of proof away from the prosecution.
Permissive Inferences (Constitutional): Courts can tell a jury they are allowed to infer intent if it is "more likely than not." (e.g., if you possess recently stolen property, a jury may infer you knew it was stolen).
4. Negligence: Objective Fault
Negligence replaces "what the defendant thought" with "what a reasonable person should have known".
Ordinary (Civil) Negligence: Simple carelessness; a failure to act as a reasonably prudent person.
Applied in State v. Hazelwood: The Alaska Supreme Court allowed this lower standard for an oil spill, arguing that the threat of jail for ordinary negligence "causes the offender to pay attention".
Criminal (Gross) Negligence: A "gross deviation" from the reasonable standard of care.
Applied in Santillanes v. State: The New Mexico Supreme Court required this higher standard for child abuse because "moral condemnation" should only attach to conduct that is truly culpable.

Note: Negligence is an objective standard (no awareness required), whereas recklessness is a subjective standard (requires actual awareness of the risk).

1. Elonis v. United States (2015)
The Facts: Elonis v. United States
The Actor: Anthony Elonis was going through a bitter divorce.
The Conduct: He posted graphically violent "rap lyrics" on Facebook directed at his estranged wife, former co-workers, and even an elementary school.
The Disclaimer: Elonis claimed the posts were "therapeutic," "fictitious," and inspired by Eminem. He often included disclaimers that they were not real threats.
The Harm: His wife felt terrorized and obtained a state-court protection order. Elonis responded by asking if the paper order was "thick enough to stop a bullet."
The Statute: 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) makes it a federal crime to transmit a "communication containing any threat... to injure the person of another." The statute is silent on the required mental state (mens rea).
The Trial: The judge told the jury they only needed to find that a reasonable person would view the posts as a threat (a negligence standard). Elonis was convicted and sentenced to nearly four years in prison.
Issue
In a federal criminal statute that is silent as to mens rea, is proof of negligence (what a reasonable person would think) sufficient for a conviction, or must the government prove the defendant had a subjective intent to threaten?
Rule
The "general rule" of American criminal law is that a guilty mind (mens rea) is a necessary element of every crime. Courts will generally read a scienter (knowledge) requirement into a statute even if it is not explicitly written, because "wrongdoing must be conscious to be criminal."
Application
Negligence is for Torts, not Crimes: The Supreme Court noted that the "reasonable person" standard is used in civil lawsuits (torts) to determine liability. However, in criminal law, we rely on the defendant's actual state of mind.
The "Vicious Will": Chief Justice Roberts emphasized that a defendant must be "blameworthy in mind" to be jailed. If Elonis truly believed his posts were art and not threats, he lacked the "vicious will" required for a felony conviction.
Statutory Silence  No Intent: Just because 18 U.S.C. § 875(c) doesn't say "knowingly" or "intentionally," it doesn't mean those requirements don't exist. To avoid punishing innocent or clumsy behavior, the Court must read in a higher mental state than mere negligence.
Conclusion
Reversed. The Supreme Court held that the "reasonable person" (negligence) standard was insufficient. The government must prove that the defendant intended the communication to be a threat or knew that the communication would be viewed as a threat. (Note: The Court famously "punted" on whether Recklessness would be enough, leaving that for a future case.
The Takeaway: When you see a statute on an exam that doesn't list a mens rea, your first instinct should be: "Under Elonis, mere negligence is not enough." You then move to the MPC default rule § 2.02(3), which suggests Recklessness is the "floor" for criminal liability when a statute is silent.
2. The Model Penal Code (MPC) Culpability Levels
When you see a statute that is silent on mens rea, the MPC provides "Default Rules."
Purposely: It is your conscious object to cause the result. (You aim to kill and you do).
Knowingly: You are practically certain the result will happen, even if it's not your main goal.
Recklessly: You consciously disregard a "substantial and unjustifiable" risk. (The MPC default: if a statute is silent, Recklessness is usually the minimum required).
Negligently: You should have been aware of a risk, but you weren't. This is a "gross deviation" from a reasonable person's standard.
Source: Model Penal Code § 2.02.
3. Willful Blindness (The "Ostrich" Doctrine)
This theory bridges the gap between Recklessness and Knowledge.
Theory Origin: Heavily discussed in United States v. Jewell (9th Cir. 1976).
The Definition: 1. The defendant must subjectively believe there is a high probability a fact exists. 2. The defendant must take deliberate actions to avoid learning that fact.
The "Equal Culpability" Thesis: The law treats someone who "shuts their eyes" to the truth the same as someone who actually knows the truth.
Criticisms:
Justice Kennedy (Dissenting in Jewell and Global-Tech): Argues that "willful blindness is not knowledge." He fears juries will convict people for being merely "reckless" or "stupid" instead of truly "knowing."
The "Duty to Investigate": Critics argue this forces citizens to become private detectives. If you borrow a car, are you now legally required to rip the door panels off to check for drugs?
Source: Global-Tech Appliances, Inc. v. SEB S.A., 563 U.S. 754 (2011).
The Recklessness vs. Knowledge Debate
The Problem: The Supreme Court in Elonis struck down the Negligence standard but didn't specify if Recklessness or Knowledge should replace it.
Default Rules: Justice Alito argues for a "one-size-fits-all" default of Recklessness when a statute is silent. This is controversial because some believe courts should look at the specific context of the crime rather than a blanket rule.
Factors for Guidance:
Severity of Punishment: In Elonis, the defendant faced 5 years. Typically, the harsher the potential prison sentence, the more likely a court should require a higher mens rea (like Knowledge) to prevent "accidental" felons.
Analogous Crimes: Courts look at similar crimes. If "malice" in other statutes is satisfied by recklessness (as in Cunningham), it's a strong argument for recklessness here.
Common Law vs. MPC: Common Law is messy and inconsistent; the MPC tries to fix this with rigid "default rules."
II. The Model Penal Code (MPC) Reforms
The Shift to Clarity
The Chaos of Common Law: Terms like "willful," "malice," and the "general vs. specific intent" distinction are often called "meaningless" or "ineffective" by scholars (e.g., Michael L. Seigel).
The Three MPC Tools:
Manageable Categories: Reducing dozens of messy terms into just four mental states.
Precise Definitions: Giving each of those four states a rigorous, legal definition.
Default Rules: Providing a roadmap (MPC § 2.02(3)-(4)) for when a statute is silent or ambiguous.
III. The Four MPC Levels of Culpability
1. Purpose vs. Knowledge (The "Intent" Split)
Purpose: Your "conscious object" is to engage in the conduct or cause the result. You want it to happen.
Knowledge: You aren't necessarily aiming for the result, but you are practically certain it will happen.
The Difference: In most crimes, this distinction doesn't matter. However, for "Specific Intent" crimes like Attempt, Complicity, or Conspiracy, the MPC often requires a true Purpose.
2. Recklessness (Conscious Risk)
Definition: You are aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk and you "consciously disregard" it.
Subjective Awareness: Unlike negligence, you must actually know the risk exists.
The Jury's Role: The risk must be a "gross deviation" from what a law-abiding person would do. The jury decides if the risk was "unjustifiable" based on the circumstances.
3. Negligence (Inadvertent Risk)
Definition: You should have been aware of a substantial and unjustifiable risk, but you weren't.
The Fault: The "viciousness" here isn't in your intent, but in your failure to perceive danger that a reasonable person would have seen.
IV. MPC Default Rules for Silent Statutes
The "Recklessness Floor" (MPC § 2.02(3))
If a statute is totally silent on mens rea, the MPC assumes you need at least Recklessness to be convicted.
This excludes Negligence as a basis for crime unless the legislature specifically writes it into the law.
The "Travel Down" Rule (MPC § 2.02(4))
If a statute mentions one mental state (e.g., "knowingly"), that requirement applies to every material element of the crime unless the law clearly states otherwise.
V. Advanced Theory: Distinguishing Mindsets
Purpose vs. Motive
Purpose (Intent): The desire to bring about a specific legal result (e.g., stealing money).
Motive: The remoter reason why you did it (e.g., stealing money to pay for a daughter's surgery).
Legal Relevance: Purpose is an element of the crime; Motive is usually irrelevant to guilt but used in sentencing.
Recklessness vs. "Opaque Recklessness"
The Challenge: What if a defendant knows there is "some" risk but doesn't realize just how "substantial" it is? (e.g., a driver dialing a phone who knows it's distracting but doesn't think it's deadly).
Subjective Culpability: Under a strict MPC view, if the defendant genuinely thinks the risk is "slim," they might escape a Recklessness charge and only be found Negligent.
Does it Matter in Practice?
The Data: Research (Ginther et al., 2018) shows that ordinary jurors actually understand these distinctions.
The Statistic: In studies, about 33% (one-third) of subjects tended to find guilt for "Knowledge" even when the defendant was only "Reckless," especially if the defendant "strongly suspected" wrongdoing but proceeded anyway. This is the bridge to the "Willful Blindness" doctrine.
Your 1L Strategy Tip:
When you get an exam question on a statute like the ones mentioned (Burglary or Destruction of Property):
Step 1: Identify the "Material Elements" (Conduct, Circumstances, Result).
Step 2: Look for the mens rea words in the statute.
Step 3: If a word is missing for an element, apply MPC § 2.02(3) to "read in" Recklessness.
United States v. Jewell
The Facts: United States v. Jewell
The Incident: Appellant Jewell drove a car across the Mexico-U.S. border.
The Contraband: Border agents found 110 pounds of marijuana hidden in a secret compartment.
The Defense: Jewell admitted he knew about the secret compartment and suspected something was "shady," but he testified that he did not actually know the marijuana was there because he never looked inside.
The Trial Instruction: The judge told the jury they could convict if Jewell’s ignorance was a result of a "conscious purpose to avoid learning the truth."
The Verdict: Jewell was convicted under a statute requiring he "knowingly" possessed drugs.
Issue
Can the statutory requirement of "knowledge" be satisfied by proof that the defendant was aware of a high probability of a fact's existence but deliberately avoided learning the truth (Willful Blindness)?
Rule
Under the "Willful Blindness" doctrine (also known as the "Ostrich Instruction"), the legal element of knowledge is established if:
The person is aware of a high probability of the fact's existence; AND
The person fails to investigate because they want to remain ignorant to avoid legal liability.
MPC §2.02(7): Knowledge is established if a person is aware of a high probability of a fact's existence, unless they actually believe the fact does not exist.
Application
The Majority (Browning, J.): * Equal Culpability: The court argues that someone who "deliberately shuts their eyes" is just as blameworthy as someone with "positive knowledge."
Statutory Purpose: If the law required absolute, 100% certainty, drug traffickers would simply hire couriers who "don't ask questions," making the law impossible to enforce.
Definition of Knowledge: In common language, "knowing" includes being aware that something is highly likely.
The Dissent (Kennedy, J.):
The High Probability Gap: Kennedy argues the jury instruction was too broad. It allowed a conviction for "avoiding the truth" without requiring that Jewell actually suspected a high probability of drugs.
The Belief Exception: Kennedy emphasizes that if a defendant actually believes (even unreasonably) that there are no drugs, they cannot have "knowledge" under the MPC.
Risk of Negligence: He fears this allows juries to convict someone for being a "reasonable person who should have checked the trunk," which reduces a "knowledge" crime to a "negligence" crime.
Conclusion
Affirmed. The court held that "knowingly" in criminal statutes encompasses "willful blindness." A defendant cannot escape liability by purposefully remaining ignorant of the specific nature of their illegal activity.
Key Takeaways for Your Finals
The Formula: Willful Blindness = Awareness of High Probability + Deliberate Avoidance.
The "High Probability" Floor: You can't be willfully blind to something that is merely a "slight risk." That would be Recklessness. To get to Knowledge, the risk must be a "high probability."
The Subjective Belief Defense: Even if everyone else thinks it’s 99% likely there are drugs in the car, if the defendant actually believes there are none, they aren't "knowing" under the MPC standard.
1. Criminal Law Applications (The "Ostrich" in Practice)
Broad Scope: Willful blindness isn't just for drug mules; it applies to white-collar crimes like mail fraud, money laundering, and insider trading (U.S. v. Goffer).
Insider Trading Example: In Goffer, the defendant was told he was "better off not knowing" where tips came from so he could deny knowledge. The court ruled this "conscious avoidance" was enough for a conviction.
Policy Debate: Some scholars (like Judge Rakoff) argue this theory should have been used more aggressively against executives in the 2008 Financial Crisis.
2. Civil Litigation (Patents and Professionals)
Global-Tech Standard: The Supreme Court ruled in Global-Tech v. SEB that the criminal "willful blindness" doctrine applies to civil lawsuits (specifically patent infringement).
Professional Liability: It is effectively used against lawyers and accountants. Because of their training, juries often find it "unbelievable" that they didn't know what was happening, even if they tried to look away.
Defense Use: Defendants can also use the doctrine to prove a plaintiff "actually knew" a fact, which might disqualify the plaintiff from suing (Intel Corp. v. Sulyma).
3. Recklessness Plus What? (The "Extra" Element)
To move from Recklessness (aware of a risk) to Knowledge (willful blindness), you usually need three things:
Awareness of High Probability: Not just a "substantial risk," but a very high likelihood.
Deliberate Avoidance: A "conscious purpose" to stay in the dark.
No Actual Contrary Belief: (MPC specific) You can't be willfully blind if you genuinely believe the fact is false.

5. Deliberate Avoidance: Acts vs. Omissions
The "Active" Requirement: Under Global-Tech, you must usually do something (or fail to do something obvious) to avoid the truth.
The "Ostrich" Metaphor (U.S. v. Giovannetti): Judge Posner explains that real ostriches don't just "fail to follow through"; they bury their heads.
Janis Example: A landlord who didn't check if his tenants were gambling wasn't "blind"—he was just "not curious." Without "active avoidance," he couldn't be convicted of knowing.
Current Tension: Some courts (U.S. v. Fisch) have allowed convictions even when the defendant simply "didn't ask a lot of questions."
6. The Motive Requirement
Why stay blind? In U.S. v. Heredia, the court debated if the defendant's motive for staying blind must be to "get a legal defense."
Exigent Circumstances: The court held that if you don't investigate because it's unsafe (e.g., pulling over on a dangerous highway), your avoidance isn't "deliberate."
The "Conscription" Critique: Judge Kleinfeld (dissenting) warns that this turns citizens into "investigators." He asks: "Should we be required to rummage through our mother's suitcase just in case?"
7. Contrived Ignorance vs. Probability
** Luban's Distinction:** Willful ignorance is about pre-incident steps (avoiding the truth). The MPC is about the state of mind at the moment of the crime.
The 3 Couriers Example: If a boss says "don't look in the bag," it's willful blindness. But if the boss says "2 out of 3 bags are empty," a courier who doesn't look only has a 1/3 probability of guilt. Under the MPC, that courier is NOT "knowing" because 1/3 is not a "high probability."

Probability: Was it a "High Probability" or just a "Substantial Risk"?
Action: Did they take an active step to stay blind (like the Jewell secret compartment), or were they just lazy/incurious (like the Giovannetti landlord)?
4. Regina v. Prince and the "Moral Wrong" Theory
This is a 19th-century classic regarding Mistake of Fact.
The Theory (Baron Bramwell): If a defendant is already doing something "morally wrong" (like taking a girl from her father), they act "at their own peril." If the girl turns out to be 14 instead of 18, their "reasonable mistake" doesn't save them because the underlying act was already immoral.
Who came up with it? Baron Bramwell in Regina v. Prince (1875).
Criticisms (Justice Brett's Dissent): * It violates the principle of mens rea. If a man's mistake was reasonable, he didn't have a "guilty mind" regarding the specific element of the crime (the age).
Graham Hughes: Argues this is dangerous. It lets juries punish people based on "community ethics" rather than the actual law.
Source: Regina v. Prince, L.R. 2 Cr. Cas. Res. 154 (1875).
5. Acoustic Separation Theory
The Architect: Professor Meir Dan-Cohen.
The Concept: Law speaks to two different audiences:
Conduct Rules (The Public): "Don't take young girls away from their parents." (A broad moral guideline).
Decision Rules (The Judges/Officials): "Only convict if the girl is under 16." (A precise technical limit).
What it means for you: It explains why the law might seem "unfair" in a specific case. The "16-year-old" limit isn't there because being 16 makes the act "okay"; it's there to keep judges from having too much power.
Source: Meir Dan-Cohen, Decision Rules and Conduct Rules: On Acoustic Separation in Criminal Law, 97 Harv. L. Rev. 625 (1984).
Regina v. Prince
The Actor: Henry Prince.
The Conduct: Prince took a girl, Annie Phillips, out of her father's possession without his consent.
The Mistake: Prince believed Annie was 18 years old. In reality, she was 14.
The Jury Finding: The jury found that Annie told Prince she was 18, and that Prince’s belief was honest and reasonable.
The Statute: It was a crime to "unlawfully take any unmarried girl, being under the age of sixteen years, out of the possession and against the will of her father."
The Conviction: Despite his reasonable mistake, Prince was convicted.
Issue
Can a defendant be convicted of a crime even if they made a reasonable mistake of fact regarding a critical element of the offense (the girl's age), provided the underlying conduct was "wrong in itself"?
Rule
The Moral Wrong Doctrine (Bramwell): If a defendant intends to commit an act that is "wrong in itself" (even if not necessarily illegal), they act at their own risk. A mistake of fact is not a defense if the facts as the defendant believed them to be still constitute a moral wrong.
The Dissenting Rule (Brett): A mistake of fact is a defense if the facts, as the defendant reasonably believed them to be, would make the defendant's conduct entirely legal.
Application
Justice Bramwell (The Majority):
Moral Blame: Bramwell argues that taking a "girl of tender years" from her father is morally wrong regardless of whether she is 14 or 18. Because Prince knew he was doing something "wrong," he shouldn't get a "get out of jail free" card just because he miscalculated the girl's age.
Strict Liability for Age: The court reads the age requirement as a strict liability element. Once you decide to do the "wrong" act of taking a girl, you are responsible for her actual age, no matter what you believed.
Analogies: He compares this to assaulting a police officer. If you hit someone, it’s "wrong." If that person turns out to be a cop, you are liable for assaulting an officer even if you didn't know their identity.
Justice Brett (The Dissent):
The Legalist View: Brett argues that if Annie had been 18, Prince would have committed no crime at all. Under English law at the time, taking an 18-year-old was not a criminal offense.
Mens Rea Requirement: He believes you cannot have a "guilty mind" (mens rea) if you think you are doing something legal. To him, Prince lacks the criminal intent necessary for a conviction because Prince thought he was acting within the law.
Conclusion
Conviction Affirmed. The court established that for certain crimes, a "moral wrong" intent is enough to substitute for specific "legal" intent. Prince took the risk when he engaged in morally questionable behavior, and the mistake of fact regarding the girl's age did not excuse him.
Why this matters for your 1L Exam
The "At Your Peril" Rule: This case is often cited to explain why mistakes of fact don't work as a defense in Statutory Rape cases today. If you engage in a "risky" or "immoral" act, the law often says you act "at your peril" regarding the victim's age.
Statutory Interpretation: Notice how the court "read into" the statute. The statute didn't say "knowingly." The court had to decide if "knowingly" was implied.
The MPC Contrast: Remember, the Model Penal Code hates this case! Under MPC § 2.04, a mistake of fact is a defense if it negates the mental state required for the crime.
What this means for your 1L experience:
In your exams, when you analyze a fact pattern, you need to:
Identify the Element: Is the issue a "result" (a death), a "conduct" (driving), or a "circumstance" (the age of a victim)?
Apply the Mental State: Use the MPC hierarchy. If the statute is silent, argue Recklessness (MPC § 2.02(3)).
Watch for "Ostriching": If a defendant says "I didn't know for sure, but I didn't ask questions," hit them with the Willful Blindness analysis from Global-Tech.
Moral Wrong Check: If the defendant made a mistake of fact, check if they were doing something "legally or morally grey." If so, the Prince "Moral Wrong" doctrine might block their defense.

1. The Rationale in Regina v. Prince
Case Context: Prince was charged with taking an unmarried girl under 16 from her father's possession. He reasonably believed she was 18.
The Principle: The court held that a mistake of fact is not a defense if the defendant’s intended conduct is still "wrong" (either morally or legally) even if the facts were as he believed them to be.
General Application: The court suggested this applies beyond sexual offenses to crimes like burglary or assaulting a police officer. If you intend to commit a wrongful act, you take the risk that the circumstances are more serious than you realize.
2. The Moral-Wrong Principle
The "Community Ethic": Proponents (like Peter Brett) argue that criminal law reflects community standards. A defendant only gets a "mistake" defense if they can prove they were acting in accordance with the community’s moral code.
The Critique: * Subjectivity: There is no single "community ethic"; values diverge.
Legality: It gives juries too much discretion to punish conduct that isn't explicitly criminal (e.g., punishing someone for the legal-but-immoral act of fornication by denying them a mistake defense on the age of their partner).
Bootstrapping: Critics argue a "moral wrong" should not be used to satisfy the mens rea for a "legal crime."
3. The "Acoustic Separation" Approach
Conduct Rules vs. Decision Rules: Professor Dan-Cohen suggests statutes speak to two audiences:
Conduct Rule (Public): "Don't take young girls from their parents." (A broad moral guide).
Decision Rule (Officials): "Only convict if the girl is under 16." (A precise line to limit judicial power).
The Tension: This approach challenges the legality principle (nulla poena). If the public only sees the "moral" rule, they aren't fairly warned about the specific "legal" lines that trigger high-level criminal liability.
4. The Lesser-Crime Principle
Risk Assumption: Unlike the Moral-Wrong principle (which triggers liability based on immorality), this principle triggers liability because the defendant intended a different, lesser crime.
The "Different Crime" Limitation: Courts (e.g., Cunningham, Faulkner) generally hold that if the intended act was a completely different type of crime (stealing a horse vs. kidnapping a girl), the lesser-crime principle does notapply.
Critiques: * Proportionality: It may be unfair to punish someone for a "Major Crime" when they only had the intent for a "Minor Crime."
Prosecutorial Power: It gives the state the discretion to seek much higher sentences for the same level of mental fault.
5. The Model Penal Code (MPC) Approach
Subjective Culpability: The MPC generally rejects the "moral-wrong" theory. It insists that a mistake is a defense if it negates the specific mens rea (purpose, knowledge, recklessness) required by the statute.
Grading by Intent (§2.04(2)): If a defendant is guilty of a lesser crime but made a mistake regarding an aggravating factor of a greater crime, the MPC typically reduces the grade of the offense.
Example: If you think you are breaking into a warehouse (lesser) but it’s actually a house (greater), you are punished at the level of the warehouse crime, not the house crime.
Requirement of Risk Awareness: Under §2.02(3), unless specified, the prosecution must usually prove the defendant was at least reckless regarding the facts that make the conduct a crime.
1. The Model Penal Code (MPC) Approach (§2.04)
Mistake as Evidence: The MPC treats mistake/ignorance not as a separate "excuse," but as evidence that negates the required mens rea. If a crime requires "knowledge" and the defendant was mistaken, they cannot be convicted because the mental state is missing.
Rejection of "Reasonableness": The MPC argues that if a crime requires purpose or knowledge, it is illogical to require a mistake to be "reasonable." Even an unreasonable (honest) mistake negates the intent.
The Grading Solution (§2.04(2)): * The Dilemma: What if a defendant makes a mistake but would still be committing a crime even if the facts were as they believed? (e.g., thinking they are stealing a $500 watch when it is actually a $5,000 watch).
The MPC "Legal Fiction": The defendant is denied the defense but is only punished at the level of the lesser offense they thought they were committing. Liability is measured by subjective culpability, not actual consequences.
2. Current Law: The "Lesser-Crime" Default
Modern Persistence: Many states still follow the Prince logic, especially in cases involving drugs, minors, or schools.
"Wrongful Conduct" Threshold: In Elonis, the Supreme Court suggested that courts only read in a mens rear equirement sufficient to separate "wrongful" conduct from "innocent" conduct. Once a person knows they are doing something "wrong," strict liability often applies to the specific details (e.g., being in a school zone).
Inconsistent Federal Rules:
Flores-Figueroa: Required proof that the defendant knew an ID belonged to a real person (rejecting strict liability for the aggravating factor).
Dean: Upheld strict liability for a firearm discharging during a crime, even if accidental (accepting strict liability for the aggravating factor).
3. Drug Crimes and Proportionality
The "Courier" Problem: In federal law, knowing you possess any drug is often enough to trigger massive mandatory minimums based on the actual drug and quantity, even if the defendant (like a courier) was mistaken about what was in the bag (United States v. Collazzo).
The 9th Circuit Split: * Majority View: Once you know you are trafficking a controlled substance, you take the "risk" of whatever the substance actually is (strict liability for quantity/type).
Dissenting View: Failing to require mens rea for the drug type violates the principle that punishment should fit the defendant's specific fault.
Prosecutorial Power: Critics argue that current "lesser-crime" doctrines give prosecutors too much power to dictate life sentences for defendants who may only have had the "fault" or intent of a much lower-level offender.
4. Policy Debate: Proportional Mens Rea
Judicial Role: Some scholars argue that courts should imply mens rea requirements for aggravating factors to ensure proportional punishment.
Counterweight to Prosecutors: While prosecutors might avoid charging "morally blameless" people, they rarely self-correct for "proportionality." Therefore, the law (via the courts) must ensure that the "punishment fits the crime" by linking the sentence to what the defendant actually knew.
People v. Olsen 
Supreme Court of California
The Facts
The defendants, Olsen (age 21) and Garcia (age 18), were charged under California Penal Code Section 288(a) for engaging in various sexual acts with a girl named Shawn. At the time of the incident, Shawn was 13 years and 10 months old. The defendants sought to present evidence that they reasonably and honestly believed the girl was over 14 years old, which would have moved the offense from a more serious felony to a potentially lesser charge (unlawful sexual intercourse). The trial court refused to allow this defense, and the defendants were convicted.
Issue: Does a defendant’s reasonable and honest mistake of fact regarding the age of a victim provide a defense to a charge of lewd and lascivious conduct with a child under the age of 14 (Penal Code § 288)?
Rule:
General Rule (People v. Hernandez): In many sex-related crimes (like statutory rape of a 16-year-old), a "reasonable mistake of age" is a valid defense because it negates the criminal intent (mens rea).
The "Lesser-Wrong" Principle: A mistake of fact is not a defense if the defendant’s intended conduct would still be illegal or "wrong" even if the facts were as believed.
Legislative Intent: Courts must look to whether the legislature intended to impose strict liability to protect a specific class of vulnerable victims (children of "tender years").
Application:
Protection of Young Children: The court distinguished this case from Hernandez. It argued that while a 17-year-old and an 18-year-old might be indistinguishable, the legislature intended to draw a "bright line" at age 14 to protect children of tender years from sexual influence.
The "Lesser-Crime" Context: The court noted that even if the defendants believed Shawn was 14 or 15, their conduct would still have been a crime (unlawful sexual intercourse with a minor). Therefore, they were not "innocent" actors making a mistake; they were people committing a crime who were mistaken only about the severity of the crime.
Public Policy: The court held that the risk of a mistake regarding a child's age should fall on the adult participant, not the child. Providing a defense would weaken the statutory protection intended by the legislature for children under 14.
Conclusion: No. The California Supreme Court held that a reasonable mistake of age is not a defense to a violation of Penal Code Section 288. The court affirmed the convictions, maintaining that the state's interest in protecting young children outweighs the traditional requirement of a subjective mens rea regarding the victim's exact age.
1. B (a Minor) v. Director of Public Prosecutions
House of Lords (2000)
The Facts
B, a 15-year-old boy, repeatedly asked a 13-year-old girl to perform oral sex. He was charged with inciting a child under 14 to commit an act of "gross indecency." B honestly believed the girl was over 14. The trial court ruled that his mistake was not a defense, leading B to plead guilty while reserving his right to appeal the legal ruling on mens rea.
Issue: Is there a presumption that mens rea (specifically regarding the age of the victim) is an essential element of a statutory sexual offense when the statute is silent on the required mental state?
Rule: 1. The Common Law Presumption: There is a baseline presumption that mens rea is required for every element of a crime unless Parliament expressly or by necessary implication excludes it. 2. Subjective Test: Modern English law prefers an "honest belief" standard over a "reasonable belief" standard. If a belief is genuinely held, its unreasonableness is irrelevant to the existence of mens rea. 3. Gravity of Offense: The more serious the offense (e.g., higher maximum penalties and greater social stigma), the stronger the presumption that mens rea is required.
Application: * Presumption of Intent: Lord Nicholls argued that if B genuinely believed the girl was over 14, he did not intend to commit indecency with a child under 14.
Statutory Construction: The Act did not explicitly state it was a strict liability offense. Because the penalty was severe (up to 10 years) and the stigma high, the court refused to imply strict liability.
Departure from Prince: The Court criticized the reasoning in Regina v. Prince, stating that defendants should be judged on the facts as they believed them to be.
Conclusion: Yes. The appeal was allowed. The prosecution must prove the absence of a genuine belief that the victim was 14 or older.
2. Garnett v. State
Maryland Court of Appeals (1993)
The Facts
Raymond Garnett, a 20-year-old man with an I.Q. of 52 (functioning at the level of an 11 or 12-year-old), engaged in consensual sexual intercourse with Erica, a 13-year-old girl. Erica and her friends had told Garnett she was 16. When Erica became pregnant and gave birth, Garnett was charged with second-degree rape. The trial court excluded evidence of Garnett’s honest and reasonable mistake of age, ruling the statute created a strict liability offense.
Issue: Does Maryland's second-degree rape statute allow for a "reasonable mistake of age" defense?
Rule: 1. Strict Liability in Statutory Rape: Traditionally, statutory rape is a strict liability crime intended to protect young persons regardless of the defendant's intent. 2. Legislative Deference: If the legislature does not include a mens rea requirement in the text of a statute, and the legislative history suggests a protective intent, courts generally will not "read in" a defense. 3. Rejection of Moral/Lesser Wrong: While some jurisdictions use the "moral wrong" theory, Maryland courts look primarily to statutory language and legislative history.
Application: * Statutory Language: The court found that the "plain language" of the Maryland code made no allowance for a mistake defense.
Policy Goals: The court prioritized the protection of minors from "physical injury, loss of chastity, and pregnancy" over the requirement of individual fault.
Judicial Restraint: The court argued that if a mens rea requirement is to be added to the rape statute, it must come from the Legislature, not the "judicial fiat" of the court.
Sentencing vs. Liability: The court suggested that "extraordinary cases" like Garnett's should be handled through the judge’s discretion during sentencing (e.g., probation) rather than by finding the defendant "not guilty."
Conclusion: No. The court affirmed the conviction, holding that second-degree rape of a child under 14 is a strict liability offense in Maryland, and a mistake of age—even if reasonable—is not a defense.

I. Statutory Rape and the Persistence of Strict Liability
While the Prince decision was repudiated in England (see B v. DPP), it remains highly influential in the United States.
Key Legal Trends
The Majority Rule: About two-thirds of states (like Maryland in Garnett) deny a mistake-of-age defense entirely, even if the mistake was reasonable.
The Minority Rule: Roughly one-third of states allow a mistake defense, but usually only if:
The victim is over 14.
The mistake was reasonable (objective standard).
Constitutional Status: Only Alaska (State v. Guest) has found strict liability in statutory rape unconstitutional. Most courts (MN, WI, MD) uphold it, citing a "state interest in protecting children" that outweighs individual liberty.
Policy and Social Critiques
The "Scarlet Letter" Effect: Conviction often triggers mandatory sex offender registration and community notification. This applies even to "morally innocent" defendants who made a reasonable mistake.
Gender Stereotypes: Many statutes are gender-neutral, but 16 states allow prosecution even when the "victim" and "defendant" are the same age. Critics like Professor Leslie Tenzer argue this allows prosecutors to use biased discretion based on gender stereotypes.
The MPC 2021 Revision: The American Law Institute (ALI) now proposes a subjective mens rea (recklessness) for statutory rape and opposes placing age-based offenders on registries unless the victim was under 12 or incest was involved.
II. Pure Strict Liability: Public Welfare Offenses
Unlike the "lesser-crime" cases, these cases involve defendants who had no reason to know they were doing anything wrong at all (no moral or legal fault).
1. United States v. Balint (1922)
Facts: Defendants sold prohibited drugs (derivatives of opium) without a specific tax form. They claimed they didn't know the drugs were prohibited.
Holding: Knowledge (scienter) is not required.
Rationale: The "social betterment" of protecting the public from dangerous drugs outweighs the "possible injustice" to an individual seller. The seller acts "at his peril."
2. United States v. Dotterweich (1943)
Facts: A pharma company president was convicted because his company shipped mislabeled drugs. He didn't know they were mislabeled; the manufacturer had made the error.
Holding: Strict liability applies to "responsible" corporate officers.
Rationale: In a modern industrial society, the public is "wholly helpless" to protect themselves against impure food and drugs. The burden is placed on the person in a "responsible relation" to the danger.

Morissette v. United States
Supreme Court of the United States
Morissette was a junk dealer who entered a wooded Air Force practice bombing range in Michigan. He found heaps of rusted, spent bomb casings that had been lying there for years. Believing the casings were abandoned, he collected them, crushed them with his truck, and sold them for a small profit ($84).
He was indicted under 18 U.S.C. § 641, which makes it a crime to "knowingly convert" government property. At trial, Morissette testified he had no intent to steal and honestly thought the property was unwanted junk. The trial judge, however, ruled that "intent" was merely the intent to physically take the casings—not the intent to violate a property right. The judge effectively treated the statute as a strict liability offense, instructing the jury that Morissette’s belief about abandonment was irrelevant.
Issue: Does a federal statute that "borrows" a traditional common-law crime (like embezzlement or stealing) but remains silent on the requirement of intent eliminate mens rea as an element of the offense?
Rule:
Presumption of Mens Rea: For traditional common-law crimes (mala in se), an "evil-meaning mind" must concur with an "evil-doing hand." Congressional silence does not eliminate this requirement.
Public Welfare Offense Exception: Strict liability is generally reserved for a "limited class" of regulatory offenses (mala prohibita) that:
Are relatively new/statutory (not from common law).
Are in the nature of "neglect" or "inaction."
Carry light penalties and do not result in grave damage to reputation (infamy).
Knowledge of Facts: To "knowingly convert" property, the defendant must know the facts that make the taking wrongful (i.e., that the property belongs to someone else and is not abandoned).
Application:
Common Law vs. Regulatory: Justice Jackson argued that stealing and conversion are ancient common-law "infamous" crimes. They are not like the "public welfare offenses" (such as mislabeling drugs in Balint or Dotterweich) created by the Industrial Revolution to regulate safety.
Severity of Stigma: A conviction for "conversion" under § 641 brands a person a "thief," which carries significant social stigma and "infamy." Such a brand should not be applied to someone who acted with an innocent mind.
Legislative Intent: When Congress uses a term like "conversion" or "stealing," it is assumed to be incorporating the "cluster of ideas" attached to those words at common law, including the requirement of a guilty intent.
Jury's Role: The trial court’s instruction essentially took the issue of intent away from the jury. Even if Morissette's story seemed unlikely, the jury—not the judge—must decide if his claim of "innocent intent" (belief in abandonment) was credible.
Conclusion: Reversed. The Court held that "knowingly converting" government property requires proof that the defendant had the specific intent to deprive the government of its property. An honest belief that the property was abandoned is a valid defense because it negates the necessary mens rea.
Staples v. United States 
Supreme Court of the United States
The Facts
Staples possessed an AR-15 rifle. Under the National Firearms Act, any weapon capable of firing automatically (more than one shot per trigger pull) is classified as a "firearm" and must be registered. Staples' rifle had been modified (a metal stop was filed down) so that it could fire automatically. Staples was charged with possessing an unregistered firearm.
At trial, Staples testified that he did not know the rifle was capable of automatic fire and that it had never malfunctioned or fired automatically while he used it. He requested a jury instruction requiring the government to prove he knew of the specific characteristics that made it a "firearm" under the Act. The trial court refused, the jury convicted him, and he was sentenced under a statute carrying a maximum of 10 years in prison.
Issue: Does the National Firearms Act require the government to prove that a defendant knew the specific physical characteristics of a weapon that brought it within the statutory definition of a "firearm," or is it a strict liability "public welfare offense"?
Rule:
Presumption of Mens Rea: Silence in a statute does not eliminate the requirement of mens rea. Courts presume Congress intended a "guilty mind" for felony offenses.
Public Welfare Offense Criteria: Strict liability is appropriate only for statutes that:
Regulate "dangerous or deleterious devices" where the defendant is in a "responsible relation to a public danger."
Alert the defendant to the "probability of strict regulation" (making the conduct not "apparently innocent").
Typically carry small penalties (misdemeanors/fines) rather than "infamous" punishment (felonies).
Dangerousness Exception: If the item is so inherently dangerous that possession is almost never "innocent" (e.g., hand grenades or narcotics), the court is more likely to infer strict liability (United States v. Freed).
Application:
Nature of the Item (Guns vs. Grenades): The Government argued that all guns are dangerous, putting owners on notice of regulation. Justice Thomas disagreed, noting a "long tradition of widespread lawful gun ownership" in the U.S. Unlike hand grenades (which are rarely possessed innocently), owning a rifle is a common, often "innocent" act that would not necessarily alert a citizen to the possibility of a 10-year prison sentence for a hidden mechanical defect.
Avoiding the Criminalization of "Innocent Conduct": If strict liability applied, a law-abiding citizen with a broken or unknowingly modified hunting rifle could become a felon. The Court is "reluctant to impute" a strict liability intent to Congress when it would trap "well-intentioned" citizens.
Penalty Severity: The 10-year maximum penalty is a "harsh penalty" incompatible with the history of public welfare offenses, which traditionally involved petty fines or short jail stays. A felony designation strongly suggests a requirement of mens rea.
Conclusion: Reversed. The Court held that the government must prove the defendant knew the weapon had the characteristics that brought it within the statutory definition (i.e., Staples had to know it was capable of automatic fire).
1. Distributing Child Pornography (United States v. X-Citement Video, Inc.)
The Legal Question
How far does the word "knowingly" travel in 18 U.S.C. § 2252? Does it only modify the verbs (transports, ships, receives) or does it extend to the age of the performers (the minority of the performers)?
The Statutory Conflict
The Textualist/Grammatical View (Justice Scalia): The most natural (and only) grammatical reading is that "knowingly" modifies the immediate verbs. Since the statute is not silent but explicitly places "knowingly" at the start, the omission of the word later in the sentence implies strict liability for the age element.
The Presumptive View (Chief Justice Rehnquist): Following Morissette and Staples, there is a legal presumption that mens rea applies to each element that criminalizes otherwise innocent conduct.
Analysis & Precedent
Is it a "Public Welfare" Offense? No. Under the Staples criteria, the distribution of visual depictions is not inherently "dangerous" in the way grenades or narcotics are, and the penalty (10 years) is too harsh for a strict liability regulatory offense.
The "Innocent Conduct" Rule: If strict liability applied, a person could be a felon for shipping a film they knew was a "visual depiction" but reasonably believed featured adults. To separate "innocent" (legal) conduct from "criminal" conduct, the court read the mens rea into the age element.
The "Ridiculous" Result: Justice Stevens argued that a purely grammatical reading would lead to the "ridiculous" result of convicting people who had no idea they were handling illegal material.
2. Producing Child Pornography (18 U.S.C. § 2251)
The Legal Question
Unlike the distribution statute, the production statute (§ 2251) contains no mens rea language at all. Should courts imply a knowledge requirement for the victim's age?
Arguments for Strict Liability (United States v. Ruggiero)
The 11th Circuit upheld strict liability based on three factors:
Legislative Intent: Congress included "knowingly" in the distribution statute (§ 2252) but left it out of the production statute (§ 2251), suggesting a deliberate choice.
Analogy to Statutory Rape: Historically, the common-law presumption of mens rea has not applied to the age element in sexual offenses involving minors (The Prince / Olsen / Garnett logic).
Opportunity to Verify: Producers deal with performers in person. Unlike a distributor who might receive a finished tape, a producer has a "fair opportunity" to check IDs and determine the minor's actual age.
Arguments for Mens Rea
The Staples/Morissette Presumption: The penalty is extremely high (15 to 30 years). Under Staples, such "harsh penalties" are usually incompatible with strict liability.
First Amendment Concerns: Producing sexually explicit material with adults is a legal, constitutionally protected activity. Mens rea is often required to ensure that individuals are not punished for what they reasonably believe is protected speech/conduct.

State v. Guminga 
Supreme Court of Minnesota
The Facts
During an undercover operation, a 17-year-old woman entered Lindee’s Restaurant with two investigators. A waitress served all three alcoholic beverages without asking for identification or checking the minor's age. The waitress was arrested for serving liquor to a minor.
The owner of the restaurant, George Guminga, was subsequently charged with a gross misdemeanor. The state admitted that Guminga was not present, was not aware of the waitress's actions, and did not ratify her conduct. He was charged solely under a state statute that imposed vicarious liability on employers for the liquor law violations of their employees. Guminga moved to dismiss, arguing the statute violated his Due Process rights.
Issue: Does a statute that imposes vicarious criminal liability (punishable by imprisonment and/or a fine) on an employer for an employee’s illegal act—committed without the employer's knowledge or consent—violate the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment and the state constitution?
Rule:
Due Process Balancing Test: Courts must balance the public interest (the goal of the law) against the intrusion on personal liberty, while considering if alternative means exist to achieve the same goal.
Vicarious Liability vs. Strict Liability: While strict liability punishes a person for their own acts regardless of intent, vicarious liability punishes a person for the acts of someone else.
Fundamental Principle of Culpability: Generally, criminal convictions require some level of personal fault (doing the act, knowing about it, or consenting to it).
Application:
Public Interest: The state argued the law provides a powerful deterrent, forcing owners to strictly supervise employees to prevent underage drinking.
Intrusion on Liberty: The court noted that a gross misdemeanor carries a penalty of up to one year in jail. Even if Guminga only received a fine, the conviction would damage his reputation and increase his "criminal history score," potentially leading to longer prison sentences for any future crimes.
Alternative Means: The court found the state's goal could be achieved through civil penalties (fines, license suspension, or revocation). These methods punish the business without the "legal and social ramifications" of a criminal record for an individual who did nothing wrong.
Lack of Fault: The court held it is fundamentally unfair to brand someone a criminal for an act they did not commit, authorize, or have the chance to prevent in the moment.
Conclusion: Yes. The court answered the certified question in the affirmative, holding the statute unconstitutional. In Minnesota, a person cannot be convicted of a crime punishable by imprisonment for an act they did not commit, have knowledge of, or consent to.
The Dissent's Theory
Justice Kelley argued that the state-granted "privilege" of a liquor license justifies higher standards. He believed that unless owners face personal jail time, they will treat civil fines merely as a "cost of doing business" rather than a serious incentive to prevent illegal sales to minors.
NOTES FROM CASE
1. Vicarious Liability: Individual Employers
While corporations are routinely held liable for employee crimes, holding an individual employer liable for an employee’s act is much more controversial.
The Fine vs. Imprisonment Distinction: * Fines: Courts generally uphold vicarious liability if the only penalty is a financial fine (regulatory/public welfare).
Imprisonment: There is a major split in authority. Guminga (MN) holds it unconstitutional if the statute allows for jail time, regardless of whether it is imposed. Conversely, State v. Beaudry (WI) upheld a conviction where a jail sentence was possible but only a fine was actually ordered.
The Problem of the "Rogue Employee": In Beaudry, the employer was convicted even though the employee stayed open past closing time specifically to entertain his own friends, directly against the owner’s instructions.
2. Vicarious Liability: Parents
Many jurisdictions have "Parental Responsibility" laws to address juvenile delinquency.
Constitutional Pushback: In State v. Akers (NH), the court struck down a statute punishing parents for their children’s illegal driving. The court held that punishing someone simply for their status as a parent offends Due Process.
Distinction from Employers: While employers choose to enter a business and assume regulatory risks, the "status" of being a parent is considered a more fundamental identity that should not automatically trigger criminal liability for another’s choices.
The Oregon Model: Some states allow an affirmative defense if the parent "took reasonable steps to control" the child, effectively shifting the standard from strict vicarious liability to a negligence-based standard.
Pragmatic vs. Moral Arguments:
Pragmatic: Laws provide an incentive for oversight.
Critics: These laws often target poor, single parents and lead to overly restrictive parenting (Professor Leslie Joan Harris).
Moral Emotional Reactions: Professor Amy Sepinwall argues some parents feel "blameworthy" for their children's acts due to their relationship, suggesting a "relationship-based" account of liability.
3. Vicarious Liability vs. Personal Omissions
To avoid the constitutional "vicarious liability" trap, many legislatures reframe these laws as Personal Omissions.
The "Failure to Supervise" Loophole: Instead of punishing the parent for the child's crime, the parent is punished for their own failure to act (not supervising).
The Reality: In practice, the child’s crime is often used as the sole evidence that the parent failed to supervise. Critics argue this is effectively vicarious liability under a different name.
4. Voluntary Acts and Strict Liability
Even in strict liability cases where mens rea (intent) is not required, the law generally still requires a voluntary act (actus reus).
The Involuntary Act Problem: If a driver swerves into oncoming traffic because a child grabbed their arm, they have no mens rea (faultless), but they also did not commit a voluntary act.
The Distinction: Most scholars agree that while you can dispense with mens rea for public welfare (Strict Liability), you cannot dispense with the requirement that the defendant actually performed a voluntary movement. If the act was involuntary, there is no "conduct" for the law to punish.

Regina v. City of Sault Ste. Marie (1978)
The Facts
The City of Sault Ste. Marie entered into an agreement with a private company to dispose of the city's refuse. The company's disposal methods resulted in pollution entering local waters. The city was charged with a public welfare offense (discharging/permitting the discharge of materials that impaired water quality). The central question was whether the city could be convicted even if it was not personally "at fault" or aware of the pollution.
Issue: Is there a middle category of criminal liability for public welfare offenses that avoids the harshness of absolute liability while still protecting the public interest?
Rule: The Court identified three categories of offenses:
Mens Rea Offenses: Prosecution must prove a positive state of mind (intent, knowledge, or recklessness).
Strict Liability Offenses: Prosecution proves the prohibited act (actus reus). The defendant is prima facie liable but can escape conviction by proving a due diligence defense (that they took all reasonable care).
Absolute Liability Offenses: Proof of the act leads to conviction; no defense of "lack of fault" is permitted.
Application:
The "Middle Position": Justice Dickson argued that absolute liability is efficient but unjust to the blameless. Conversely, requiring full mens rea for minor regulations makes enforcement nearly impossible.
Burden of Proof: In the new "Strict Liability" category, once the act is proven, the burden shifts to the defendant to prove on a "balance of probabilities" that they were not negligent. This is fair because the defendant has the most information about their own precautionary measures.
Conclusion: The Court ordered a new trial to determine if the City had exercised reasonable care to prevent the pollution.
II. Detailed Notes on the Academic & Legal Debate
The debate over strict and absolute liability revolves around a conflict between administrative efficiency and fundamental justice.
A. Arguments FOR Absolute Liability (The Pragmatic View)
Incentive for Extreme Care: Proponents argue that if defendants know "ignorance is no excuse," they will take precautions beyond what is "reasonable" to ensure zero violations.
Administrative Necessity: Proving a mental state for thousands of minor regulatory infractions (speeding, food safety, pollution) would overwhelm the court system and the prosecution's budget.
Minimal Stigma: Absolute liability is often defended on the grounds that these are "quasi-crimes" with small fines and little social "opprobrium."
B. Arguments AGAINST Absolute Liability (The Moral View)
Violation of Penal Principles: It is a core tenet of justice that the "blameless" should not be punished.
Inefficacy of Deterrence: If a person is already taking every reasonable precaution, a conviction does not "stimulate" more care—it only breeds cynicism and disrespect for the law.
The Reality of Stigma: Vera Bergelson and Justice Dickson point out that the "no stigma" argument is a myth. Convictions carry "collateral consequences": legal costs, loss of time, damaged reputations, and increased "criminal history scores" that affect future sentencing.
C. Perspectives from Key Academic Thinkers
Arthur Goodhart (The Utilitarian Defense): He asks: "Is it better that ten young persons should be tempted to become drug addicts than that one innocent man should be convicted?" He argues public harm from widespread acquittal outweighs the injury of one wrongful conviction in specific regulatory contexts.
Mark Kelman (The "Time Frame" Argument): Kelman argues the distinction between negligence and strict liability is an illusion created by using a "narrow time frame."
Example: A man who sleeps with a 13-year-old claiming she "looked 16" only looks non-negligent if you look at the moment of the act. If you expand the time frame, he was negligent for failing to check an ID days prior.
Phillip Johnson (The Business Context): He argues strict liability is only justifiable if the underlying conduct is "socially undesirable" (like robbery). Since managing a business or factory is productive activity the law wants to encourage, punishing those who take "all reasonable steps" is unjustifiable.
Stephen Schulhofer (The Counter-Intuitive Effect): He suggests strict liability might actually increase harm. Careful people might exit a highly regulated industry to avoid risk, leaving only the "careless" people who are willing to gamble on not getting caught.
The Model Penal Code (The "Frontal Attack"): The MPC § 2.05 refuses to allow imprisonment for any strict liability offense. If the prosecution doesn't prove fault, the offense is reduced to a "violation" (civil), not a "crime."

IV. Constitutional Status of Absolute Liability
Canada: Following Sault Ste. Marie, the Supreme Court of Canada ruled in the Motor Vehicle Act reference (1985) that absolute liability combined with the threat of imprisonment is unconstitutional under the Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

People v. Marrero
The Actor: The defendant, Marrero, was a federal corrections officer (a prison guard) employed at a federal facility in Danbury, Connecticut.
The Act: On a trip to Manhattan, Marrero went to a social club while carrying a loaded .38 caliber pistol. He did not have a New York permit for the weapon.
The Arrest: Police entered the club and arrested Marrero for unlicensed possession of a firearm under N.Y. Penal Law § 265.02.
The Justification: At the time of his arrest, Marrero didn't say, "I didn't know carrying a gun was illegal." Instead, he argued, "I am a peace officer, and the law says peace officers are exempt."
The Statutory Snag: * New York law exempted "peace officers" from the gun permit requirement.
The definition of "peace officer" included "correction officers of any state correction facility or of any penal correctional institution."
Marrero argued that "any" included federal prisons in Connecticut.
The Legal Twist: A New York court eventually ruled that "any" actually only meant facilities located within New York. Therefore, Marrero was technically not a peace officer under New York law and was breaking the law by carrying the gun.
The Defense's Stand: Marrero admitted he had the gun but argued he shouldn't be convicted because he honestly and reasonably believed the statute's wording (the "official statement of the law") applied to him.
Understanding the "Mistake" Hierarchy
To ace your 1L Criminal Law exam, you must distinguish between a mistake of fact and a mistake of law.
Mistake of Fact: "I took your umbrella because I thought it was mine." (Usually a defense if it negates the mens rea).
Mistake of Law: "I knew I was taking your umbrella, but I thought the law said all umbrellas in the lobby are public property." (Marrero Rule: Generally NOT a defense).
Professor's Sidebar: Why this Matters
This case is a favorite for professors because it highlights the strictness of the legal system. Marrero wasn't a "criminal" in the traditional sense; he was a law enforcement officer trying to follow the rules. However, the New York Court of Appeals decided that maintaining a stable, objective interpretation of the law is more important than being "fair" to one confused individual.
Issue
Does a defendant’s mistaken personal interpretation of a statute—even if that interpretation is reasonable—provide a valid "mistake of law" defense under New York Penal Law § 15.20(2)(a)?
Rule
A mistake of law is not a defense unless the mistaken belief is founded upon an official statement of the law contained in a statute, administrative order, or judicial decision. In New York, this exception applies only when the official statement is later found to be invalid or erroneous, not when a defendant simply misinterprets a valid statute.
Analysis
Defendant’s Argument: Marrero argued he was a "peace officer" under the statute because he was a federal corrections officer. He claimed his mistake was based on the "official statement" of the law (the statute itself).
The Court’s Literal vs. Policy View: While the New York statute didn't explicitly include the Model Penal Code’s "afterward determined to be invalid" language, the Court looked to legislative intent.
The "Floodgates" Concern: Justice Bellacosa argued that if every "reasonable" misinterpretation of a law were a defense, the "exception would swallow the rule." It would encourage people to form their own convenient interpretations of the law rather than obeying it.
The Difference between Mistake of Fact vs. Law: The court distinguished this from cases like People v. Weiss, where a mistake of law negated a specific intent element (like "without authority of law" in kidnapping). Here, the weapons charge was essentially a strict liability-style offense regarding the status of the person.
Conclusion
No. The conviction was affirmed. A personal misreading of a statute does not trigger the mistake of law defense. The "official statement" defense is a narrow "escape hatch" for those relying on laws that the government later admits were wrong, not for those who guess wrong about what a law means.

I. Rationale of Ignorantia Legis (Paragraphs 1-2)
1. Fact vs. Law Distinction
The "Gun" Hypo: In Marrero, if the defendant thought the gun was unloaded (a fact), he might still be guilty because weapons possession is often a strict liability offense (no mental state required).
Treating Law Differently: The Court in Marrero suggests that even if a mistake of fact could be a defense, a mistake of law about the term "peace officer" is strictly barred to prevent people from inventing their own legal meanings.
Policy Fear: Allowing a "personal interpretation" defense would encourage people to stay ignorant or find loopholes, effectively letting the "exception swallow the rule."
2. Utilitarianism and Incentives
Holmes’ View: We punish the ignorant to force people to "know and obey."
The Police Exception (Heien): The Supreme Court allows police officers a "reasonable mistake of law" defense for Fourth Amendment seizures.
Civilians vs. Police: A major debate arises—if a "reasonableness" requirement keeps police from being "sloppy" with the law, why wouldn't it provide the same incentive for civilians?
Morality as a Guide (Kahan): Professor Kahan argues that the law should be obscure so people follow social morality instead of looking for technical loopholes. He suggests Marrero lost because his "bad character" (reaching for his gun) signaled he wasn't a "virtuous" actor.
II. When Mistake of Law CAN be a Defense (Paragraphs 1-2)
1. Examples of Successful Defenses
Property Rights (Regina v. David Smith): A man who destroyed wall panels he installed thought they were his. Because the crime requires "damaging property belonging to another," his mistake about the legal status of the property negated his intent to damage someone else's stuff.
Larceny/Theft (State v. Varszegi): A landlord who took computers believing he had a "claim of right" under a lease lacked the felonious intent to steal.
The Rule: If you honestly believe the property is yours (even if your legal reasoning is wrong/unreasonable), you aren't a "thief" because you lack the intent to steal.
2. The MPC Reconciliation: The "Collateral Law" Rule
MPC § 2.04(1): Mistake of law is a defense if it negates the required mental state (mens rea) for an element.
MPC § 2.02(9): You generally don't need to know that your conduct is a crime (the "law defining the offense").
The "Collateral" Distinction:
Law Defining the Offense: "Is it illegal to carry this gun?" (No defense if you're wrong).
Collateral Law/Attendant Circumstances: "Does this property belong to me?" or "Is my visa status valid?" (Defense allowed because it negates the knowledge required for that specific element).
The Rehaif Case: A defendant must know his "legal status" (being in the U.S. unlawfully) to "knowingly" violate the federal firearm statute. It’s a "collateral" question of law, not a mistake about the crime itself.
III. The Perjury Problem (Paragraph 3)
The "Lineal Descendant" Case (Law v. State)
The Facts: A son omitted grandchildren from a guardianship petition because he misunderstood the legal term "lineal descendants."
The Court’s Holding: They called it a "mistake of law" and denied the defense.
Applying the MPC to this Problem:
Is it a defense? Yes. Perjury requires "knowingly" making a false statement.
Why? Whether someone is a "lineal descendant" is a collateral legal fact. If he honestly didn't think they qualified, he didn't "know" his statement was false.
Reasonable vs. Honest? Under the MPC/Theft rules, the belief usually only needs to be honest (subjective), even if it is unreasonable, because an unreasonable honest belief still negates "knowledge."

Cheek v. United States, 498 U.S. 192 (1991)
Facts
The Defendant: John Cheek, an American Airlines pilot.
The Conduct: Cheek stopped filing federal income tax returns and claimed zero allowances on his W-4 forms.
The Belief: Cheek attended seminars by a tax-protestor group. He became convinced of two things:
Statutory Interpretation: The tax laws (Internal Revenue Code) didn't actually treat wages as "income."
Constitutional Belief: The tax laws were unconstitutional and therefore void.
The Charges: He was charged with "willfully" attempting to evade income tax and failing to file returns (26 U.S.C. §§ 7201 & 7203).
Trial Ruling: The judge told the jury that an "honest but unreasonable" belief is not a defense. The judge essentially said Cheek's beliefs were so wacky (objectively unreasonable) that they couldn't count as a "good faith" misunderstanding.
Issue
Does a subjective, good-faith misunderstanding of the law negate the statutory "willfulness" requirement, even if that misunderstanding is objectively unreasonable?
Does a good-faith belief that the law is unconstitutional also negate willfulness?
Rule
General Rule: "Willfulness" in federal criminal tax statutes means the "voluntary, intentional violation of a known legal duty."
The Misunderstanding Rule: To prove willfulness, the government must prove the defendant knew of the duty. A subjective, good-faith misunderstanding of the law—even if unreasonable—negates this knowledge and provides a defense.
The Constitutional Rule: A belief that a law is unconstitutional is not a defense. This is treated as a "studied conclusion" that the law is invalid, which proves the defendant actually knew what the law required but chose to ignore it.
Application (The "A" in IRAC)
Complexity Exception: The Court notes that tax laws are so dense that Congress specifically added the "willfulness" requirement to protect citizens who get lost in the paperwork. Unlike the defendant in Marrero, a tax defendant cannot be convicted if they genuinely didn't know the law applied to them.
Subjective vs. Objective: The Court held the trial judge erred by requiring Cheek's belief to be "reasonable." If the jury believes Cheek truly thought wages weren't income, he hasn't "known" his legal duty, so he can't "willfully" violate it.
Strategic Note: The more "unreasonable" the belief is, the less likely a jury is to believe it’s actually "honest." (A jury might think, "He's a pilot; he's not that dumb.") But the legal standard remains: if it is honest, it is a defense.
Statutory vs. Constitutional: The Court drew a sharp line.
Statutory Misunderstanding: "I don't think the law says I owe money." (Defense allowed).
Constitutional Defiance: "I know the law says I owe money, but I think the law is unconstitutional." (Defense NOT allowed).
Why? In the latter, Cheek is admitting he knows what the statute says; he's just deciding he doesn't have to follow it. He takes the "risk of being wrong" on constitutional law.
Conclusion
Reversed and Remanded. The conviction was overturned because the jury was wrongly instructed to ignore "unreasonable" beliefs. Cheek was entitled to have the jury decide if his (weird) statutory beliefs were truly held in good faith. However, his constitutional arguments were legally irrelevant to his "willfulness."

I. The "Knowingly/Willfully" Spectrum (Paragraphs 1-3)
The Inconsistency: Courts don't agree on what "knowing" means. It can mean:
Awareness of the facts only.
General awareness that the act is unlawful.
Awareness of the specific statute violated.
The "Hazardous Materials" Rule (Int’l Minerals): For dangerous items (corrosive liquids), you only need to know the facts (that you are shipping acid). You don't need to know the ICC regulations. "Probability of regulation is so great that anyone who is aware [of the facts] must be presumed to be aware of the regulation."
The "Innocent Conduct" Rule (Liparota): For non-dangerous items (food stamps), "knowingly" does require knowing the law. Why? Because without it, someone who accidentally buys overpriced groceries could be a felon. We want to protect "apparently innocent conduct."
The "Drug" Exception (Ansaldi): Even if a drug is complex (GBL vs. GHB), courts often treat it like a strict liability matter—knowing you sold the chemical is enough, even if you didn't know the law labeled it a "controlled substance."
II. The Modern Critique: Too Much Law (Paragraph 4)
Larkin’s Argument: The ancient rule that "everyone knows the law" was fine when there were only 10 laws (don't kill, don't steal).
The 27,000-Page Problem: Today, with 27,000 pages of federal code, it's impossible for a normal human to know every malum prohibitum (regulatory) crime.
The Proposal: We should treat the "knowledge of law" rule as a rebuttable presumption, not an absolute fact.
III. PROBLEM: The "Gift" Gun (Karani) (Paragraph 5)
The Case: Karani sold a gun at a discount and called it a "gift" on a federal form. The court said he was guilty of "knowingly" making a false statement because "gift" legally means "no payment."
The Conflict: The court treated "gift" as a fact/dictionary definition, not a "complex legal duty" like the tax code in Cheek.
Professor's Critique: Is "gift" really that simple? In Varszegi and David Smith, property law mistakes weredefenses. This suggests the court was just being "tough on guns."
IV. The Mens Rea Reform Act (Paragraphs 6-7)
The Bill: If a federal law is silent on mens rea, the default should be "willfully" (meaning you knew it was unlawful).
The Debate:
Pro: Prevents "overcriminalization" of people who make honest mistakes.
Con: Lets corporate polluters off the hook by allowing them to claim "I didn't know the environmental regulation existed."
V. Official Reliance & "Entrapment by Estoppel" (Notes 1-4)
The Traditional (Harsh) View (Hopkins): Even if the State’s Attorney tells you a sign is legal, if he’s wrong, you go to jail. "Advice of counsel" isn't higher than the law.
The MPC (Sane) View: You have a defense if you reasonably rely on an official statement (statute, judicial decision, or administrative order) that is later found to be wrong.
Constitutional "Entrapment by Estoppel" (Raley): Due Process prevents the government from telling you "X is legal" and then prosecuting you for doing X.
The "Reasonableness" Trap:
Federalism (Cox): Cox relied on a Kansas law saying he didn't need a federal silencer permit. The court said his reliance was unreasonable because any "sophisticated lawyer" would know federal law beats state law (The Supremacy Clause).
Circuit Splits (Albertini/Muskett): You rely on a current court ruling in your favor. If that ruling is "shaky" or might be reversed by the Supreme Court, courts often say your reliance was unreasonable. You essentially need "uncanny legal foresight."

Lambert v. California, 355 U.S. 225 (1957)
Facts
The Ordinance: Los Angeles had a municipal code requiring any "convicted person" (a felon) to register with the police if they remained in the city for more than five days.
The Defendant: Virginia Lambert was a resident of Los Angeles for seven years. She had a prior felony conviction (for forgery), which made her subject to the registration law.
The Violation: Lambert was arrested on an unrelated suspicion. Police discovered she hadn't registered as a felon.
The "Passive" Nature: Lambert didn't "do" anything active—she simply existed in the city without filling out a form she didn't know existed.
The Knowledge Gap: Lambert had no actual knowledge of the requirement to register, and the law didn't require "willfulness" (knowledge of the law) for a conviction.
Issue
Does a municipal ordinance violate the Due Process Clause of the 14th Amendment when it punishes a person for a "wholly passive" failure to register, despite the person having no notice or knowledge of the duty to act?
Rule
While ignorance of the law is generally not a defense, Due Process requires "notice" when:
The conduct is wholly passive (an omission/failure to act);
The defendant is unaware of the duty to act; and
There are no circumstances that would move a reasonable person to inquire about the law (i.e., the act is not malum in se or inherently dangerous).
Application (The "A" in IRAC)
Active vs. Passive Conduct: Justice Douglas distinguishes this from cases like Balint (narcotics). In those cases, the defendant is doing something (selling drugs) that is inherently "alerting." Here, Lambert was just "being" in Los Angeles. The Court held that "mere presence" is not enough to put someone on notice that they might be breaking a law.
The "Probability of Knowledge": The Court looked at whether a person in Lambert's shoes would likely know about the law. Unlike a liquor or gun dealer who knows their business is heavily regulated, a person walking the streets of their own city has no reason to suspect there is a secret registration requirement.
The Fairness Principle: The Court compared this law to one "written in print too fine to read." To punish someone for an omission they didn't know was required, without giving them a chance to comply once they found out, is "engrained" as a violation of Due Process.
Dissent's Warning (The "Derelict"): Justice Frankfurter argued this was a dangerous "deviation" from precedent. He feared it would strike down thousands of registration laws and argued that there is no moral difference between being punished for a drug mistake (active) and a registration mistake (passive).
Conclusion
Reversed. The conviction cannot stand because the lack of notice violated Due Process. The government must prove either actual knowledge or the probability of knowledge before punishing a passive failure to register.

. Analyzing the Lambert Generalization (Note 1)
Frankfurter’s Fear: The dissent believed the "generalization" (that notice is required for Due Process) could potentially strike down all strict liability laws where a defendant is unaware of the law.
Limiting the Rule: Most scholars and courts argue the Lambert rule is narrow and only applies when:
The conduct is wholly passive (a failure to act).
The law is a regulatory/administrative measure (not inherently "evil").
The "Babysitter" Hypo: If an ordinance prohibited felons from being babysitters, Lambert might not apply. Why? Because "accepting employment" is an affirmative act, not a "wholly passive" state of being like just living in a city.
II. PROBLEM: Sex Offender Registration (State v. Bryant) (Note 2)
The Conflict: Bryant was told to register in South Carolina but moved to North Carolina and didn't register there. He claimed a Lambert "lack of notice" defense.
The "Circumstances" Test: The North Carolina Supreme Court rejected his defense using two distinctions:
Public Safety vs. Record-Keeping: Sex offender laws are "public safety measures," whereas the LA law in Lambert was just a "law enforcement technique" (compiling a list).
Duty to Inquire: Because nearly every state has sex offender registries now, the court held that a conviction for such a crime is an "alerting circumstance." Bryant should have asked if he needed to register.
The Takeaway: For Lambert to work, the situation must be "empty of circumstances" that would make a reasonable person ask, "Is there a law about this?"
III. "Entrapment by Omission" and Misleading Courts (Note 3)
The "Misleading Judge" (State v. Leavitt)
The Facts: A judge told a defendant he couldn't have guns during his one-year probation. The defendant thought that meant he could have them after the year. Actually, a different law banned them forever.
The Holding: The court reversed the conviction. Even though "knowledge of the law" wasn't an element, the government (via the judge) affirmatively misled the defendant. It’s unfair to punish someone for not "speculating" that the judge gave them incomplete information.
The "Obscure Statute" Trap (United States v. Wilson)
The Facts: A man under a domestic restraining order was arrested for owning a gun under a federal law. The state judge who issued the order never mentioned guns, and the man had no lawyer.
The Majority View: Conviction affirmed. They stuck to the "knowledge of facts" rule: He knew he had a gun and knew he had a restraining order. That’s enough.
Judge Posner’s Famous Dissent:
The "Johnny Horizon" Problem: Posner argues the federal code is full of "ridiculously obscure" laws no one could intuit.
Lulled into Safety: Because the restraining order didn't mention guns, it "lulled" Wilson into thinking he was fine as long as he stayed away from his wife.
Efficiency vs. Justice: Posner argues that keeping the law a "secret" serves no purpose. If the judge and the lawyer didn't know the law, how could a layperson? He argues Lambert should have saved Wilson.

B.STATUTORY FRAMEWORKS
1. The Historical Shift: From Blackstone to 2021
The law has moved through three distinct phases across these statutes:
Phase 1: Force & Resistance (Blackstone/1950 CA): The focus was on the defendant’s physical violence and the victim’s physical struggle.
Phase 2: The "Reasonable Woman" & Implied Threats (1962 MPC): The law began to recognize psychological "compulsion" but still graded the offense lower if the victim was a "voluntary social companion."
Phase 3: Affirmative Consent (Modern CA/WI/2021 MPC): The focus shifts from the defendant’s force to the victim’s lack of consent (defined as "positive cooperation").

Notice that the 1950 CA and 1962 MPC specifically say "not his wife."

Pay attention to what makes a crime "1st Degree" versus "3rd Degree":
NY: 1st Degree = Physical force or victim is "physically helpless."

Look at Section 261(a)(5) in CA 2021. It covers "Rape by Fraud"—specifically, pretending to be someone the victim knows (the "dark room" trick). Most older laws only recognized fraud as rape if the victim thought they were married to the perpetrator.
Professor's Perspective: The 2021 MPC Shift
The most significant change in the 2021 MPC Draft is the move toward a "Consent-Centric" model. It separates "Sexual Assault by Physical Force" (Section 213.2) from "Sexual Assault in the Absence of Consent" (Section 213.6). This allows the law to punish a broader range of behavior while still reserving the highest penalties for physical violence.

2. The Persistence of "Force"
The notes emphasize a point that surprises many 1L students: In just over half the states, "No" is not enough for a felony conviction. * The "Gap" in the Law: In "Force" states, if a victim says "no" but the defendant does not use physical violence or threats of "great bodily harm," the legal system may not recognize the act as a felony.

The notes end by pointing you back to State v. Rusk (the case we IRAC'd earlier). In 1981, Rusk was a "close case" because the "force" used (taking car keys, a "light" choking, a "heavy" look) was borderline psychological.
Professor's "Cold Call" Prep Questions:
Then vs. Now: Would Rusk be a "close case" today? In a "Non-consent" state, the answer is No—the lack of consent would be enough. In a "Force" state, it might still be close, depending on whether the court views taking car keys as "coercion."
The Reformers' View: Why is the glass "not yet half full"? Because reformers believe that the autonomy of the person is violated the moment consent is absent, and waiting for "force" to occur puts the victim in unnecessary physical danger.
4. Key Academic Concepts to Remember
Actus Reus: In "Force" states, the actus reus is the violence. In "Non-consent" states, the actus reus is the penetration without permission.
The "Statutory Rape" Exception: Notice that even "Force" states have always punished non-consensual sex without force in three specific categories: Age, Unconsciousness, or Mental Incompetence. The modern reform movement is essentially trying to add "Lack of Consent" as a fourth category to that list.
State v. Rusk
Court of Appeals of Maryland
424 A.2d 720 (1981)
The Facts
The complainant (Pat) and the defendant (Edward Rusk) met at a bar. Pat agreed to give Rusk a ride home. Once they arrived at his apartment in a "tough" neighborhood, Rusk took the car keys from the ignition and invited Pat up.
Pat testified that she followed him because she was afraid to be left alone in that neighborhood without her keys. Inside the apartment, Rusk spoke "lightly" but allegedly had a "look in his eyes." Pat testified that she was terrified, told him "no," and eventually cried. Rusk allegedly choked her lightly and then proceeded to have intercourse with her. There were no visible bruises or explicit threats of "I will kill you," which became the central point of contention for the defense.
Issue
Whether the evidence was sufficient to sustain a rape conviction when the victim’s fear was not preceded by a direct threat of force or immediate physical resistance, but was based on the defendant's conduct and the surrounding circumstances.
Rule
To sustain a conviction for second-degree rape (under the Maryland statute at the time), the state must prove the intercourse was committed by force or threat of force without the victim's consent. If the victim's fear is the reason for lack of resistance, that fear must be reasonably grounded in order to negate the element of consent.
Application
The Prosecution's View: The court looked at the "totality of the circumstances." Rusk took Pat's keys (effectively kidnapping her car), took her to a strange environment, and used a "heavy" look and a light choking gesture. The prosecution argued this created a climate of fear that made her submission involuntary.
The Defense's View: Rusk argued there was no "real" force. He didn't brandish a weapon or beat her. He argued that Pat's fear was subjective and unreasonable—essentially, that she gave up too easily without a "real" struggle.
The Court’s Conclusion: The Court of Appeals reinstated the conviction. They reasoned that the jury is the proper body to decide if a victim’s fear is reasonable. Taking the keys and the choking gesture, combined with the isolated location, were enough for a jury to find that Pat's will had been overcome by a reasonable fear of harm.
Conclusion
The court held that a victim does not need to be physically beaten for "force" to exist; if the defendant’s conduct creates a reasonable apprehension of fear in the victim, the element of force is satisfied.
Professor's Note: Why this matters
This case highlights the Objective vs. Subjective tension. The law generally doesn't care if you were personally scared; it cares if a Reasonable Person in your shoes would have been scared. Rusk pushed the boundaries of what counts as "reasonableness" by acknowledging that psychological coercion and "implied" threats can be just as potent as a physical punch.

United States v. Jackson
United States Court of Appeals, Second Circuit
560 F.2d 112 (1977)
The Facts: A Bad Day for "The Gang"
In 1977, Jackson and two associates decided to rob a Manufacturers Hanover Trust branch in Brooklyn. They didn't just talk about it; they took action:
The First Try: They drove to the bank with a "stick-up" note and weapons. However, they arrived too late—the bank was already closed.
The Second Try: They returned another day, again armed and ready. This time, they got spooked by the presence of a security guard and left.
The Arrest: Police pulled them over shortly after the second attempt and found the tools of the trade (guns, masks, and that incriminating note).
The defendants argued they hadn't actually done anything yet—they were just driving around. The court had to decide if these "dry runs" constituted a legal attempt at robbery.
Issue
Under the "substantial step" test, can defendants be convicted of attempted robbery if they never actually entered the bank or brandished a weapon, but reconnoitered the location while armed?
Rule
The Second Circuit adopted the Model Penal Code (MPC) § 5.01 definition of attempt. To be guilty of an attempt, a defendant must:
Act with the kind of culpability otherwise required for the commission of the crime (Intent).
Engage in conduct which constitutes a "substantial step" toward the commission of the crime.
The step must be strongly corroborative of the actor’s criminal purpose.
Application
The court rejected the old "proximity" test (which required being dangerously close to finishing the crime) in favor of the MPC's "substantial step" test.
Substantial Step: The court found that scouting the bank (reconnoitering) while in possession of robbery paraphernalia (guns, masks, and the note) was enough.
Corroboration: The fact that they did this twice and had a written demand note strongly corroborated that they weren't just out for a Sunday drive; they were there to rob the place.
Conclusion
Yes. The convictions were affirmed. The court held that their conduct crossed the line from mere preparation into a criminal attempt because their actions made their criminal intent clear.
1. The Policy Debate: Safety vs. Bias
The tension here is between Early Intervention (stopping a crime before it happens) and Due Process (ensuring we don't arrest people based on "vibes" or racial profiling).
The MPC's Goal: Relax the actus reus (the physical act) to catch criminals earlier.
The Bias Risk: Professor Chiesa argues that by focusing on "intent" rather than "proximity," we invite police and juries to rely on stereotypes. If the law doesn't require a "dangereously close" act, a person of color standing in a "suspicious" spot might be arrested for attempt, while a white person in the same spot is ignored.
The Counter-Intuitive Study: Professor Sood’s study suggests the "Proximity" test (Rizzo) might actually be morebiased because words like "dangerously close" act as emotional anchors, making jurors see guilt where it may not exist.
The "Wiz" Take: There is no "perfect" test. Every time you lower the bar for what counts as a "step," you increase the discretion of the police. In a system with embedded biases, more discretion often leads to more disparate outcomes.
2. Problem 1: United States v. Harper (The Bill Trap)
The Question: Would the MPC produce a different result than the 9th Circuit?
The 9th Circuit (Reversed): They used a "substantiality" test that looked a lot like proximity. They felt 90 minutes was too far away.
MPC Application: Under MPC § 5.01(2)(a), "lying in wait" or "searching for or following the contemplated victim" can be a substantial step. However, Harper wasn't following a victim yet; he was waiting for a hypotheticalone to show up in an hour.
Result: Likely the same. Even under the MPC, many courts find that "setting the stage" for a crime that is hours away is still "mere preparation."
3. Problem 2: United States v. Joyce (The Cocaine Stand-off)
The Question: Was the court right to reverse under the MPC?
The Analysis: The MPC requires a "substantial step" that is strongly corroborative of intent.
The Flaw: The court focused on the fact that Joyce abandoned the deal. However, under the MPC, if you have already taken a substantial step (flying to a city with $22,000 to buy drugs), the crime of attempt is already complete.
The Verdict: Many scholars argue this was a misapplication of the MPC. Once Joyce showed up with the cash, he had arguably taken a substantial step. Walking away later shouldn't negate the attempt that already happened (unless he meets the very strict requirements for the "Renunciation" defense).
4. Problem 3: United States v. Howard (The Digital Solicitation)
The Question: Correct application or "Thought Crime"?
(a) The MPC Application: The 5th Circuit says yes. The "step" was the specific instruction to the "mother" to prep the girls. The court felt this crossed the line from "talking" to "directing action."
(b) The Rizzo (Proximity) Test: Under Rizzo, Howard would likely be not guilty. He was in California; the "victims" were in Texas. He wasn't "dangerously near" the completion of the crime.
Comparison: The MPC approach is better for child protection (intervention), but the Proximity test is better for protecting free speech/thoughts.
5. Problem 4: Omissions as Attempt
The Question: Can not doing something be a "substantial step"?
Rule: Under MPC § 5.01(1)(c), an omission counts if it's a "substantial step."
The Nursing Home Example:
Duty: The attendant has a legal duty to provide meds.
Intent: They want the patient to die.
The Act: They skip the meds.
Conclusion: This is attempted murder. By the time the omission is discovered, the "ball is rolling" toward death. The "step" is the failure to act when the duty required it.
State v. Davis
Supreme Court of Missouri
319 Mo. 1222, 6 S.W.2d 609 (1928)
The Facts: A Hitman and a "Hustle"
The facts read like a grainy black-and-white noir film:
The Plot: The defendant, Davis, wanted to have his wife’s lover, Edmisten, killed. He also wanted to get his hands on Edmisten’s $2,000 life insurance policy.
The Plan: Davis hired a man named Lougan to find a "hitman." Davis agreed to pay $600 for the murder and provided Lougan with a map to Edmisten's house and a description of his habits.
The Twist: Lougan was actually an undercover police officer.
The Meeting: Davis met Lougan and a supposed "hitman" (another officer). Davis paid them a $200 down payment, gave them a photo of the target, and told them to go through with the killing that night.
The Arrest: As soon as Davis handed over the money and the photo, the police arrested him and charged him with attempted murder.
Issue
Does hiring a supposed hitman, providing a "hit kit" (photo, map, etc.), and paying a down payment constitute a criminal attempt at murder, or is it merely preparation?
Rule
Under the common law proximity test used in Missouri at the time:
Intent: The defendant must intend to commit the crime.
Overt Act: There must be an act moving toward the commission of the offense.
Proximity: The act must move beyond "mere preparation" and be dangerously close to completion. The court famously noted that "preparation" consists of devising the means or measures necessary for the commission of the offense, while "attempt" is the direct movement toward the commission after the preparations are made.
Application
The Missouri Supreme Court reversed Davis's conviction. Their reasoning was strict:
The Gap: Even though Davis had done everything he could do at that moment, the court held that the "hitman" had not yet moved toward the victim.
The Test: The court argued that for an attempt to occur, the defendant (or his agent) must perform an act that would naturally result in the crime unless interrupted by an outside force.
The Logic: Since the "hitman" was a cop who never intended to kill anyone, the "last act" necessary to commit the murder was never close to happening. Hiring a hitman was seen as mere preparation—setting the stage—rather than the "commencement of the consummation."
Conclusion
No. The conviction was reversed. The court held that Davis's actions did not reach the level of a "legal attempt" because no act was committed that was "dangerously near" the actual murder.
Why This Case Matters (The 1L Takeaway)
Compare this to the Jackson case. Under the modern MPC "Substantial Step" test, Davis would be guilty as charged because paying a hitman is "strongly corroborative" of criminal intent. But in 1928, the law required you to be much closer to the "finish line" of the crime.
1. The Punishment Paradox: Harm vs. Culpability
The Church note highlights a massive shift in how the law views "failure."
Traditional View (Result-Oriented): Punishment is tied to the harm caused. If the victim survives, the harm is lower; therefore, the sentence is lower. This leads to "factfinder hair-splitting" (trying to decide if a step was "substantial" or "proximate" just to justify a longer sentence).
Modern View (MPC/Federal Guidelines): Punishment is tied to the actor’s "badness" (culpability). If you intended to kill and did everything you thought was necessary, the law shouldn't give you a "discount" just because the police stopped you or the gun jammed.
The "Fortuity" Factor: The law is moving away from letting defendants benefit from the "good luck" of failing to complete the crime.
2. Solicitation: The "Hired Gun" Problem
Solicitation is asking, enticing, or commanding someone else to commit a crime.
A. Is Solicitation just a "Baby Attempt"?
The Problem: In Davis, the court said hiring a hitman wasn't an attempt. Why? Because the "independent will" of the hitman stood between the defendant and the crime.
The Split: * MPC § 5.01(2)(g): Can treat solicitation as an attempt if it’s a "substantial step."
Common Law/Some States: Solicitation cannot be an attempt because the solicitor doesn't intend to commit the crime personally. They want someone else to do it.
B. Solicitation as an Independent Crime
If you can't get them for attempt, can you get them for solicitation itself?
The Danger of Collaboration: The MPC argues that solicitation is more dangerous than attempt because it creates a "special hazard of cooperation." Two criminals are scarier than one.
The "Feigned" Accomplice: Note the technical trap—if the person you "solicit" is a cop (like in Davis), you can't be guilty of conspiracy (because you need two people to actually agree) but you can be guilty of solicitation(which only requires your unilateral request).
3. The First Amendment Buffer
This is where Criminal Law meets Constitutional Law. When does "hating" become "soliciting"?
The Rule: Speech that "advocates" or "incites" immediate violence is not protected.
The "Independent Moral Agent" Defense: Some argue solicitation isn't dangerous because the person being asked can say "no."
The Threshold: * Protected: "These politicians deserve to die!" (Political hyperbole/Abstract advocacy).
Unprotected: "I will pay you $5,000 to kill that politician tonight at 8 PM." (Specific solicitation of an illegal act).
The "How-To" Manuals: The Rice v. Paladin Enterprises case is famous because it held that a book (Hit Man) was so specific in its instructions that it crossed the line from "speech" to "providing the means" for a crime.

People v. Jaffe
New York Court of Appeals
The Facts: The Case of the "Honest" Stolen Goods
The Intent: Jaffe wanted to buy stolen goods. Specifically, he wanted to purchase some cloth from a group of thieves.
The Twist: The police caught the thieves first. The owner of the cloth got his property back.
The Trap: To catch Jaffe, the police and the owner conspired to have the thieves proceed with the sale anyway. They delivered the cloth to Jaffe as planned.
The Reality: At the moment Jaffe bought the cloth, it was no longer "stolen" property in the eyes of the law; it had been recovered by the owner and was technically "legal" property.
The Arrest: Jaffe was arrested and convicted of attempting to receive stolen property. He appealed, arguing that since the goods weren't actually stolen, he couldn't have committed the crime.
Issue
Can a defendant be convicted of an attempt to receive stolen property if the property, unknown to the defendant, has lost its status as "stolen" before the purchase?
Rule
The court applied the doctrine of Legal Impossibility:
An attempt requires that the act, if completed, would constitute the substantive crime.
If the intended act is not a crime in the eyes of the law, then the attempt to perform that act cannot be a crime.
Moral vs. Legal Intent: Having a "guilty mind" (mens rea) is not enough if the actus reus is legally impossible to complete.
Application
The New York Court of Appeals reversed the conviction. Their reasoning was strictly formalistic:
The statute defined the crime as receiving stolen property.
Since the property had been returned to its owner, it was no longer "stolen."
Therefore, even if Jaffe had completed the act (which he did), the result was not a crime because he was receiving legal property.
The "Pocket-Picking" Contrast: The court distinguished this from factual impossibility. If you reach into a pocket to steal a wallet and the pocket is empty, that is still an attempt (factual impossibility). But if you reach into a pocket to steal what you think is a wallet but is actually your own property, that is legal impossibility—and under Jaffe, not a crime.
Conclusion
No. The conviction was reversed. The court held that one cannot "attempt" to commit a crime that is legally impossible to commit, regardless of how much "larceny" is in their heart.
The Wiz Note: The Modern Rejection of Jaffe
Don't get too attached to this logic! The Model Penal Code § 5.01(1) was specifically written to "kill" the Jaffe rule.
Under the MPC, if you believe the circumstances are such that your conduct would be a crime (i.e., you believe the goods are stolen), you are guilty of attempt. Most modern jurisdictions have abandoned the Jaffe distinction because it allows "morally guilty" people to walk free on a technicality.
People v. Dlugash
New York Court of Appeals
41 N.Y.2d 725, 363 N.E.2d 1155 (1977)
The Facts: The Brooklyn Shooting
The First Act: On a December night in Brooklyn, a man named Bush was drinking with Geller (the victim) and Dlugash. Bush, in a fit of rage, shot Geller five times in the chest with a .38 caliber revolver. Geller fell over, bleeding profusely.
The Second Act: A few minutes later, Dlugash walked over to Geller’s body and fired five more shots—this time into Geller’s head and face.
The Legal Problem: The prosecution couldn't prove beyond a reasonable doubt that Geller was still alive when Dlugash fired the headshots. If Geller was already dead from Bush’s chest shots, Dlugash technically shot a corpse.
The Trial: Dlugash was convicted of murder. The lower appellate court reversed it, saying you can't kill a dead person (Legal Impossibility). The case then went to the New York Court of Appeals.
Issue
Can a defendant be convicted of attempted murder if they shoot a person with the intent to kill them, but it is factually impossible to do so because the victim is already dead?
Rule
The Court looked to the revised New York Penal Law § 110.10 (which was heavily influenced by the Model Penal Code):
Intent: The defendant must act with the specific intent to commit a crime.
The Impossibility Shift: If the defendant believes the circumstances are such that their conduct would be a crime, it is no defense that the crime was actually impossible to commit because of the "factual or legal" circumstances.
Application
The Court of Appeals performed a "judicial transplant":
Murder Conviction: They agreed that the murder conviction could not stand because there was no proof Geller was alive (you can't "kill" what is already dead).
The Attempt Pivot: However, they looked at Dlugash's subjective belief. Dlugash thought Geller was alive (or at least he intended to finish him off).
Death of Jaffe: The court explicitly moved away from the 1906 Jaffe logic. They held that the modern statute was designed to punish people who believe they are committing a crime, even if the world doesn't cooperate with their plan.
Conclusion
Yes. The court modified the judgment. While Dlugash was not guilty of completed murder, he was found guilty of attempted murder.

1. The Taxonomy of Impossibility
Your notes distinguish between three "flavors" of impossibility. Understanding these is the key to not getting tripped up by "mislabeled" terms.
Factual Impossibility (The "Empty Pocket"): You try to do something illegal, but a physical fact stops you (unloaded gun, empty safe). Never a defense.
True Legal Impossibility (The "Imaginary Law"): You think it's illegal to dance on a Tuesday. You dance on a Tuesday. Since no law prohibits this, you haven't committed a crime. Always a defense.
Hybrid (Mislabeled) Legal Impossibility (The "Jaffe/Dlugash" Zone): You want to do something that is a crime (receiving stolen goods), but a legal circumstance (the goods aren't actually stolen) makes it impossible. The "Great Debate" of Criminal Law.
2. The Three Competing Approaches
Your notes present three different ways courts handle these "Hybrid" cases:
A. The MPC/Modern Approach (Subjectivism)
The Logic: Focus entirely on the defendant's intent.
The Rule: If you believed you were committing a crime, you are guilty of attempt.
Example: In State v. Smith, the defendant bit a guard believing he could transmit HIV. Even though it was medically impossible, his intent to kill made him guilty.
Goal: Catch dangerous people regardless of whether their plan was scientifically sound.
B. The Berrigan Approach (Strict Legalism)
The Logic: You cannot "attempt" to do something that isn't a crime in that specific instance.
The Case: United States v. Berrigan (Smuggling letters).
The Rule: If the law says "it is a crime to do X without the Warden's consent," and the Warden does consent, then "X" is no longer a crime. If "X" isn't a crime, you can't be guilty of attempting "X."
The Result: It revives the Jaffe logic. It requires the "external, objective situation" to match the law's requirements.
C. The Oviedo Approach (The "Objective Acts" Test)
The Logic: We don't want to convict people based on "thought crimes" or undercover setups where the defendant's actions look innocent to an outsider.
The Case: United States v. Oviedo (Selling "fake" heroin).
The Rule: The defendant's physical acts, standing alone (without looking at their secret intent), must look criminal.
The Result: Since selling a legal substance (procaine) looks exactly like a legal business deal to a bystander, we won't convict for attempt. We need "objective acts" that "mark the conduct as criminal."
3. Policy Snapshot: Why does this matter?
The notes suggest two major "real world" pressures driving these rules:
Undercover Stings: If a "John" meets a "Prostitute" who is actually a cop, or a "Pedophile" meets a "Child" who is actually an adult detective, the MPC approach ensures a conviction. The Berrigan approach might let them walk because the "child" wasn't actually a child (Legal Impossibility).
Evidentiary Fear: The Oviedo court is terrified of "mistaken conclusions." They worry that if we don't require an objective criminal act, innocent people who are "talking tough" or "ripping off" drug dealers might be sent to prison for "intending" to sell real drugs.

1. Mens Rea for Group Criminality 
Hicks v. United States
Supreme Court of the United States
150 U.S. 442 (1893)
The Facts
The backdrop is a dramatic confrontation in the "Indian Territory" (modern-day Oklahoma). John Hicks, Andrew Colvard, and Stand Rowe (the shooter) were on horseback. Rowe and Colvard got into a heated dispute.
During the argument, Hicks allegedly said to Colvard: "Take off your hat and die like a man." Immediately after, Rowe shot and killed Colvard. Hicks and Rowe then rode off together. Hicks was charged and convicted of murder as an accomplice (aider and abettor), primarily based on those words and the fact that he fled the scene with the killer. Hicks appealed, claiming his words weren't intended to encourage the murder and that the jury instructions were flawed.
Issue
Can a defendant be convicted of aiding and abetting a murder based on words spoken at the scene if those words were not intended to encourage the perpetrator, or if the perpetrator was not actually influenced by them?
Rule
To be an accomplice by "words of encouragement," the defendant must:
Share the criminal intent of the principal (the shooter).
The words must be uttered with the purpose of inciting or encouraging the crime.
The words must actually induce the principal to commit the act (or at least be capable of doing so).
Analysis
The Supreme Court took issue with the trial judge’s instructions to the jury. The Court reasoned:
The Intent Gap: Hicks argued he told Colvard to "die like a man" to advise him to face his fate bravely, not to encourage Rowe to pull the trigger. Without proof that Hicks intended to cause the death, he cannot be a murderer.
The "Encouragement" Requirement: Even if the words sounded like encouragement to a bystander, if Rowe (the shooter) didn't hear them or wasn't influenced by them to act, the "aiding" element fails.
Mere Presence & Flight: The Court clarified that being present at a crime and even riding away with the criminal does not, by itself, make one an accomplice. Flight might suggest guilt, but it doesn't prove the specific intent to aid a murder at the moment it happened.
Conclusion
The Supreme Court reversed the conviction. The jury instructions were too broad because they allowed a conviction based on the effect of the words rather than the intent of the speaker.

State v. Gladstone
Supreme Court of Washington
The Facts:
An undercover police informant named Kent asked the defendant, Gladstone, where he could buy some marijuana. Gladstone replied that he didn't have any, but he knew a guy named Thompson who did. Gladstone then did three things:
He gave Kent Thompson’s address.
He drew a map to Thompson's house.
He told Kent that Thompson was "willing to sell."
Kent went to Thompson's, bought the drugs, and Gladstone was subsequently charged and convicted of aiding and abetting the sale. Gladstone appealed, arguing he wasn't "working with" Thompson in a legal sense.
Issue:
Whether a defendant be convicted of aiding and abetting a crime if there is no evidence of a "nexus" (connection), prior agreement, or "community of purpose" between the defendant and the principal perpetrator?
Rule:
To be guilty of aiding and abetting, a person must associate themselves with the venture, participate in it as something they wish to bring about, and seek by their action to make it succeed. Merely pointing someone toward a willing criminal is not enough.
Analysis:
The Court looked at the relationship (or lack thereof) between Gladstone and Thompson. They found:
No "Nexus": There was no evidence that Gladstone and Thompson had an agreement or were "in business" together.
Passive Assistance: While Gladstone’s map made the crime easier to commit, he didn't have a stake in the outcome. He wasn't getting a cut of the money, and he wasn't acting on Thompson's behalf.
Communication vs. Encouragement: To "abet" means to encourage or incite. Giving information to a buyer (the informant) is not the same as encouraging the seller (Thompson) to commit the crime.
Conclusion:
The Supreme Court of Washington reversed the conviction. Gladstone was not an accomplice because he lacked the specific intent to "further the enterprise" of Thompson.

This case draws a bright line: Knowledge + Assistance Accomplice Liability. You need Intent + Association. If you just give a guy directions to a bank he happens to rob later, you aren't his accomplice unless you intended for that robbery to succeed and were "in on it" with him.

1. Aiding and Abetting the Purchase vs. the Sale
In Gladstone, the defendant was charged with aiding the sale (helping the seller, Kent). The court found no "nexus" between Gladstone and the seller.
The "Buyer" Loophole: If Gladstone had been charged with aiding the purchase (helping the student, Thompson), the "nexus" would be obvious—he was literally talking to the buyer.
The Legal Snag: You generally cannot be an accomplice to a crime that didn't legally happen. If Thompson was a police agent, he wasn't "guilty" of a crime, which complicates Gladstone's liability.
The MPC Fix: Under MPC § 5.01(3), you can be guilty of attempted complicity even if the person you're trying to help doesn't actually commit the crime.
Key Takeaway: The "nexus" (the connection) is specific. You can have a nexus with a buyer but not the seller, even though they are in the same transaction.
2. When does "Knowledge" become "Intent"?
How do we distinguish Gladstone (no liability) from McKeown (liability)?
Physical Presence: In McKeown, the defendant was actually there. In Gladstone, he just drew a map.
"Touting" the Product: In State v. Wilson, the defendant encouraged the buyer by saying the weed was "good stuff."
The Gladstone Standard: The court requires more than just being a "human GPS." To cross the line, you must actively facilitate the deal (arranging the meeting, being present, or acting as a "salesman").

Minor Crimes (Prostitution/Gambling): We require Purpose. We don't want to over-regulate everyday transactions.
Major Crimes (Murder/Terrorism): Knowledge is enough. If you sell a gun to someone who says, "I'm going to kill my mother-in-law," you are an accomplice. The stakes are too high to let you hide behind "I just wanted to make a sale."

Pro-Posner: It's practical. It deters the most heinous crimes.
Anti-Posner: It makes the law unpredictable. It essentially lowers the burden of proof for the government in the most serious cases, where the defendant's liberty is most at risk.

Rosemond v. United States
Supreme Court of the United States
572 U.S. 65 (2014)
Rosemond v. United States is the modern-day blockbuster on accomplice liability. This case is crucial because it clarifies exactly when an accomplice must gain knowledge of the crime to be held responsible for the whole "bundle" of illegal acts.
The Facts
Justus Rosemond was part of a drug deal gone wrong. He and two associates met a buyer in a park to sell some marijuana. During the transaction, one of the sellers (it was disputed whether it was Rosemond or an associate) pulled out a gun and fired shots at the buyer, who had tried to drive away with the drugs without paying.
Rosemond was charged with two federal crimes:
Distributing marijuana.
Using a firearm during and in relation to a drug trafficking crime (under 18 U.S.C. § 924(c)).
Rosemond argued he didn't know his buddy brought a gun until the moment the shots were fired. The government argued that as long as he knew he was participating in a drug deal and a gun was used, he was guilty of the firearm charge as an accomplice.
Issue
To be convicted of aiding and abetting the "double-barreled" crime of using a firearm during a drug deal, must the defendant have advance knowledge that a confederate would be armed, or is knowledge acquired during the crime sufficient?
Rule
Double Intent: An accomplice must intend to facilitate the entire crime (both the drugs and the gun).
Advance Knowledge: The defendant must have "advance knowledge" of the firearm—meaning knowledge at a time when the defendant still has a realistic opportunity to withdraw from the crime.
Analysis
Justice Kagan, writing for the Court, used a great analogy (very "Law Wiz" style):
The "Bundle" Theory: Aiding and abetting is an "all-or-nothing" venture. If you choose to help with a "drug-and-gun" crime, you are liable for both.
The Timing Requirement: However, you can't "intend" to facilitate a gun crime if you don't know there’s a gun.
The "Point of No Return": If the defendant only finds out about the gun the second it's fired, they haven't made a conscious choice to participate in a gun crime—they were already "in too deep" to safely leave. Therefore, "advance knowledge" is required so the defendant can decide: "I'm okay with a drug deal, but I'm not okay with a shootout. I'm out."
Conclusion
The Supreme Court vacated Rosemond’s conviction. The jury instructions were flawed because they didn't require the jury to find that Rosemond had enough "lead time" to quit the enterprise once he learned of the gun.

1. The Rosemond Knowledge vs. Purpose Debate
The Court in Rosemond tried to simplify things, but may have made them murkier.
The Intent/Knowledge Paradox: The Court says you must intend to facilitate the crime, but then says that knowledge of the circumstances plus active participation equals intent.
The "Active Participation" Filter: The Court suggests that as long as you aren't just an "incidental" helper (like a guy who sells a car to a drug dealer), your knowledge of the gun makes you responsible for the gun.
Advance Knowledge (The Majority vs. Dissent):
Majority (Kagan): You must know about the gun early enough to withdraw. If you only find out when the bullets fly, you didn't "choose" to participate in a gun crime.
Dissent (Alito): If you know there's a gun and you don't leave immediately, you’ve accepted the risk.
11th Circuit (Steiner): Clarifies that the government must prove you had a "realistic opportunity" to walk away or change the plan.
2. Criminal Facilitation: The "Middle Ground"
Some states (like New York) created a separate, lesser crime for people who help criminals but don't necessarily share their "purpose."
N.Y. Penal Code § 115.05: Guilty if you provide "means or opportunity" believing it is "probable" the other person intends to commit a felony.
The Gladstone Question: Would Gladstone be guilty in NY?
Argument for Yes: He gave a map and address (means/opportunity) believing it was probable Kent would sell weed.
Argument for No: Did a map "in fact aid" the sale if Kent already knew Thompson was a dealer? (Facilitation usually requires the aid to actually contribute to the crime's success) 
3. Targeted Facilitation Statutes
When general accomplice law fails, legislatures write specific laws to close gaps.
A. Juvenile Gun Sales (The Columbine Example)
The Problem: Mark Manes sold a gun to minors. He didn't intend a massacre, so he wasn't a murder accomplice.
The Fix: Colo. Rev. Stat. § 18-12-108.7. It makes the act of providing the gun the felony itself.
Mens Rea: Only requires recklessness as to the age of the recipient. It ignores the intent regarding what the juvenile does with the gun.
B. Material Support for Terrorism (18 U.S.C. § 2339A/B)
The Standard: "Knowing" provision of support.
The Trade-off: The law "hardens" the actus reus (defining exactly what support is, like training or expert advice) so it can "dilute" the mens rea (just knowledge, not purpose).
The Humanitarian Law Project Ruling: SCOTUS ruled that even "benign" help (teaching a group how to use international law for peace) is illegal if that group is a designated Terrorist Organization.
Critique: This creates a "chilling effect" on speech and charity, as organizations fear any interaction with certain groups could lead to 15 years in prison.

Money laundering is the most common "facilitation" charge because it targets the transaction rather than the crime.
§ 1956: Knowledge that the deal is designed to conceal dirty money.
§ 1957: Knowledge that the money is illegal (no concealment intent required) for deals over $10,000.

State v. McVay
Supreme Court of Rhode Island
132 A. 436 (R.I. 1926)
The Facts
This case involves a tragic steam boiler explosion on the steamer Larchmont in Rhode Island.
The Principal (The Captain/Engineer): They operated a boiler they knew was unsafe, which eventually exploded, killing several people. They were charged with manslaughter based on "criminal negligence."
The Accomplice (McVay): McVay didn't touch the boiler. However, he was the one who directed and encouraged the captain and engineer to keep the steamer running with that faulty boiler to maintain their schedule.
McVay argued that he couldn't be an "accessory before the fact" (an accomplice) to manslaughter. His logic: Manslaughter by negligence is an unintentional crime. You can’t "counsel and command" someone to do something unintentionally.
Issue
Can a person be held liable as an accessory (accomplice) to the crime of involuntary manslaughter if they intentionally encouraged the negligent conduct that led to the death?
Rule
Yes. An accessory may be convicted of manslaughter if they intentionally incited or encouraged the principal to engage in the dangerous and negligent conduct that resulted in the fatality.
Analysis
The Court rejected McVay's "logical impossibility" argument. Here is how they reconciled the two:
The "Act" vs. the "Result": While the death was unintentional (negligent), the conduct (running a broken boiler) was intentional.
The Encouragement: McVay intentionally encouraged the captain to be negligent. He "counseled and commanded" the dangerous behavior.
Foreseeability: If you push someone to act with "gross negligence," and that negligence kills someone, you are just as responsible as the person who pulled the lever. You shared the   of "disregard for human life."
Conclusion
The Supreme Court of Rhode Island ruled against McVay. They held that a defendant can be an accessory to involuntary manslaughter if they encourage the underlying negligent act.

Commonwealth v. Roebuck
Supreme Court of Pennsylvania
32 A.3d 613 (Pa. 2011)
Case Overview: Commonwealth v. Roebuck (2011)
The Facts
The defendant, Roebuck, was part of a group involved in a drug-related dispute. Roebuck lured the victim, a rival drug dealer, to an apartment complex under the guise of a meeting. When the victim arrived, Roebuck’s associates opened fire and killed him.
Roebuck did not fire the shots, but he set the trap. The shooters were convicted of Third-Degree Murder (a killing with "malice" or "extreme recklessness" regarding human life). Roebuck was also convicted as an accomplice to third-degree murder.
Roebuck appealed, arguing a logical impossibility: Accomplice liability requires the "intent to promote or facilitate" a crime. Third-degree murder, by definition, is a crime where the killer did not intend to kill. Therefore, Roebuck argued, he couldn't "intend" for an "unintentional" killing to happen.
IRAC Analysis
Issue
Can a defendant be convicted as an accomplice to third-degree murder, which requires a   of malice but not a specific intent to kill, given that accomplice liability requires the "intent to promote or facilitate" the crime?
Rule
Yes. To be an accomplice to a crime involving a specific result (like death), the defendant must:
Intend to promote or facilitate the conduct (setting the trap/confrontation).
Act with the same mental state (recklessness/malice) toward the result required of the principal.
Analysis
The Court adopted the logic of the Model Penal Code § 2.06(4). They broke the crime into two parts:
The Conduct: Roebuck intentionally facilitated the confrontation (luring the victim). He had the "intent to promote" the dangerous situation.
The Result (Death): For crimes where the result is unintended (manslaughter or third-degree murder), the accomplice doesn't have to "intend" the death. They just have to share the principal's reckless disregard for the risk of death.
Logical Consistency: The Court held it is not a "logical impossibility" to intentionally help someone do something incredibly dangerous while being reckless about whether someone might die.
Conclusion
The Supreme Court of Pennsylvania affirmed the conviction. Roebuck was a legal accomplice because he intentionally helped start a violent chain of events while possessing the "malice" (reckless heart) that someone might get killed.
1L Exam Summary: "The Result vs. The Conduct"
This is the key distinction you must make on your final:
Specific Intent Crimes (1st Degree Murder): The accomplice must want the victim to die.
Recklessness/Malice Crimes (3rd Degree/Manslaughter): The accomplice only needs to want to help the dangerous conduct and not care if the victim dies.
Wiz Note: Think of Roebuck as the "Party to a Riot" rule. If you hand a brick to a friend during a riot, you aren't "intending" for them to accidentally hit a bystander, but you are intentionally helping the dangerous act and you clearly don't care about the risk. That makes you an accomplice.
NOTES FROM CASE 
1. Results vs. Conduct: The Double Standard
The law treats the "act" and the "outcome" differently.
The Rule: To be an accomplice, you must have the specific intent to help the conduct (e.g., the driving, the shooting, the operating of the boiler). However, regarding the result (the death), you only need the mens rearequired by the underlying statute (usually recklessness or negligence).
The Logic: If you intentionally help someone do something incredibly dangerous, you have "adopted" their risk-taking. You don't have to want the victim to die; you just have to not care enough to stop the dangerous act you are fueling.
2. Solving the Problems: The "Taxi" and the "Roommate"
These are classic exam hypotheticals testing the McVay and Roebuck principles.
(a) The Hurried Passenger:
Conduct: Passenger intentionally encouraged the "speeding and aggressive driving."
Result: Passenger may not have wanted a death, but by urging "whatever it takes," they likely shared the driver's reckless disregard for safety.
Verdict: Passenger is likely an accomplice to negligent homicide/manslaughter.
(b) The Drunk Roommate (The Childress issue):
Conduct: Drew intentionally gave the keys (the "aid").
Result: Did Drew have a "reckless" mental state regarding the death? Most courts say yes—giving keys to a drunk person is inherently reckless.
The "Pretzel" Twist: If Drew asked Jesse to "pick up pretzels," it strengthens the case for accomplice liability because it shows Drew affirmatively promoted the drunk driving trip, rather than just passively allowing it.
3. Attendant Circumstances: The "Blind Spot"
"Attendant Circumstances" are facts that must exist for an act to be a crime (e.g., the victim's age in statutory rape, or a person's status as a "felon").

People v. Russell
Court of Appeals of New York
693 N.E.2d 193 (N.Y. 1998)
The Facts
The setting was a housing project in Brooklyn. Three defendants—Russell, Bekka, and Sanchez—engaged in a "triangular" gunfight.
They were all firing at each other across a crowded courtyard.
During the exchange, a bystander (a father looking for his children) was struck and killed by a single stray bullet.
The Problem: Ballistics could not prove which defendant fired the fatal shot.
The prosecution charged all three with Depraved Indifference Murder (a New York variation of second-degree murder involving extremely reckless conduct) under an accomplice theory. The defendants argued they couldn't be accomplices because they were trying to kill each other, not work together.
Issue
Can participants in a mutual gunfight be held liable as accomplices for the death of a bystander, even if it cannot be proven who fired the fatal shot, and even though the defendants were adversaries rather than "partners"?
Rule
Yes. Accomplice liability is satisfied when defendants intentionally aid each other in engaging in the conduct that causes the death, while possessing the mental state (depraved indifference/extreme recklessness) required for the result.
Analysis
The Court of Appeals of New York used a "Mutual Encouragement" theory:
Shared Conduct: Even though they were shooting at each other, they were all "willing participants" in the gunfight itself. By choosing to engage in a shootout in a crowded area, each defendant encouraged the others to continue firing.
The "Nexus": The "community of purpose" wasn't to kill the bystander; it was to have an illegal, high-stakes gunfight. Because they all agreed (implicitly or explicitly) to settle their dispute with a shootout, they were "working together" to create the zone of danger.
State of Mind: All three acted with "depraved indifference" to human life. Since they all intentionally participated in the reckless conduct, they are all liable for the result, regardless of whose gun the bullet came from.
Conclusion
The Court affirmed the convictions. When you engage in mutual combat that endangers the public, you have effectively "deputized" your enemies as your accomplices in recklessness

Wiz Note:
On an exam, look for "drag racing" or "mutual combat" scenarios. If two people race cars on a highway and one of them hits a pedestrian, Russell says the other driver is also guilty of the homicide because they "aided" the race by participating in it.
Notes from the reading
1. The Paradox of "Accomplice in the Conduct"
The cases of Abbott (drag racing) and Russell (gunfights) create a legal fiction: that enemies or competitors are actually "helping" each other.
The "Willing Participant" Theory: In Abbott, the court argues that by racing, Moon gave Abbott the "incitement and encouragement" to drive dangerously. Even if Moon didn't want Abbott to crash, he wanted the race to happen.
The Russell Riddle: Did Russell want the rival gang to shoot at him?
Literally: No, he wanted to live.
Legally: Yes. By pulling his own gun and firing, he "accepted the terms" of a mutual shootout. He aided the existence of the gunfight.
Key Distinction: You don't have to intend the specific movement (the swerve of the car or the trajectory of the rival's bullet); you only have to intend to participate in the high-risk collective activity that made those movements possible.
2. Causation vs. Complicity: Which Tool to Use?
When a bystander dies in a "group mess," prosecutors have two ways to pin it on a defendant. Your exam should ideally discuss both.
Approach
Legal Question
Case Example
Causation
Did the defendant's act "set in motion" the chain of events? Was it a "but-for" cause and "proximate" cause?
Commonwealth v. Root (Strict proximate cause—no liability if the other driver was the direct cause).
Complicity
Did the defendant "encourage or aid" the person who fired the fatal shot/hit the victim?
People v. Russell (Broad liability—if you're part of the dangerous conduct, you're an accomplice to the result).
Why Complicity is often "stronger" for the Prosecution: Causation can be broken by "intervening acts" (like the rival shooter's independent decision to fire). Complicity bypasses this by saying, "It doesn't matter who did the final act; you were both in it together."
3. Intro to: The Natural and Probable Consequences (NPC) Theory
This is where the   requirements for accomplice liability get "relaxed"—or, as some defense attorneys say, "destroyed."
The Traditional Rule (The Ceiling)
Normally, an accomplice is only liable for the specific crime they intended to aid (e.g., "I helped you rob the bank, so I'm liable for robbery").
The NPC Rule (The Floor)
Under this doctrine, an accomplice is liable for any other crime committed by the principal, provided that crime was a "natural and probable consequence" of the intended crime.
The "Target" Crime: The crime you meant to help with (e.g., Burglary).
The "Non-Target" Crime: The crime your partner committed that you didn't intend (e.g., Murdering the homeowner).
The Test: Was it foreseeable that a burglary might turn into a murder? if "Yes," you are a murderer, even if you were the lookout and specifically told your partner "no guns."
Wiz Note on the "NPC" Controversy:
Many scholars hate this doctrine because it effectively allows a defendant to be convicted of a specific intent crime (like murder) based on a negligence standard (foreseeability).
The MPC rejects this: The Model Penal Code generally requires purpose or knowledge for the specific offense charged.
States are split: Some states are currently abolishing this (like California's recent reforms to the felony murder and accomplice rules).
1L Exam Strategy Tip:
If your hypothetical involves a "Plan A" that turns into a "Plan B" (e.g., "We planned to shoplift, but my friend punched the guard"), you must analyze:
Accomplice Liability: Did the defendant intend to aid the punch? (Probably not).
NPC Doctrine: Was the punch a "natural and probable consequence" of the shoplifting? (Argue both sides!).
People v. Luparello
California Court of Appeal, Fourth District
231 Cal. Rptr. 832 (1987)
The Facts
Luparello was involved in a messy domestic situation. He was looking for his former lover, who had gone into hiding with her new husband, Mark Van Kleef. Luparello was desperate to find them, so he recruited several "thugs" (his friends) to help him get information.
Luparello’s instructions to his friends were: "Do whatever is necessary" to get the information from a man named Martin (a friend of the husband). He specifically told his recruiters that he wanted the couple found "at any cost."
While Luparello was not present, his friends went to Martin’s house. Instead of just "questioning" him, they lured Martin outside and shot him to death. Luparello was charged with first-degree murder as an accomplice. He argued that he only intended for his friends to threaten or interrogate Martin, not to kill him.
Issue
Can an accomplice be held liable for a specific-intent crime (murder) committed by the principal, even if the accomplice did not intend for that specific crime to occur, if that crime was a natural and probable consequence of the intended target crime?
Rule
Yes. Under the NPC doctrine, an accomplice is vicariously liable for any reasonably foreseeable offense committed by the principal that is in furtherance of the originally contemplated conspiracy or help.
Analysis
The Court applied a purely objective test rather than a subjective one:
The Target Crime: Luparello intended and aided a "confrontation" involving threats and potential assault/battery to get information.
The Foreseeability: The Court reasoned that when you send violent "thugs" to "do whatever is necessary" to get information in a high-tension situation, it is objectively foreseeable that someone might get killed.
Irrelevant Intent: It did not matter that Luparello personally didn't want Martin dead. What mattered was that a "reasonable person" in Luparello's position would have recognized that murder was a likely (natural and probable) result of the initial plan to use force.
Conclusion
The Court affirmed the first-degree murder conviction. Luparello was legally responsible for the murder because it was a foreseeable consequence of the assault he set in motion.

This case represents the Broadest version of accomplice liability. In an exam, use Luparello when you want to argue for the prosecution in a "Plan A went wrong" scenario.

Wiz Note (The Modern Twist):
It is important to note that California has since significantly limited this. In 2018, the CA legislature passed SB 1437, which effectively abolished the Natural and Probable Consequences doctrine for Murder. Today, in California, you generally cannot be convicted of murder unless you actually had the intent to kill or were a "major participant" acting with "reckless indifference."
However, Luparello remains a titan in textbooks because it illustrates the common law/traditional NPC theory that many other states still follow.
Roy v. United States (1995)
The Facts
The Plan: Roy helped arrange a illegal handgun sale. He hooked up a buyer (who was actually an informant) with a seller named Ross.
The Deviance: Instead of completing the sale, Ross changed his mind, pulled the gun on the informant, and robbed him of $600.
The Charge: Roy was convicted of armed robbery as an accomplice. The prosecution’s theory was that when you set up an illegal gun deal, it is "natural and probable" that one criminal might just rob the other.
Issue
Is the "natural and probable consequence" doctrine broad enough to hold an accomplice liable for a "qualitatively different" crime (robbery) when their original intent was only to facilitate a different, lesser crime (an illegal sale)?
Rule
A "natural and probable" consequence is one that occurs in the "ordinary course of things." It must be a reasonably predictable outcome, not merely something that is "conceivable" or a "possibility."
Analysis
The court reigned in the "foreseeability" test with three key arguments:
Qualitative Difference: A sale (where both parties get what they want) is fundamentally different from a robbery (where one party is victimized). Roy contemplated a transaction, not a transition into violence.
The "Illegal Market" Logic: The government argued that because the victim was a criminal, he was a "logical target" for robbery (since he wouldn't call the police). The court rejected this, noting it would create a "no limiting principle" rule where any crime involving criminals would make everyone liable for every other crime committed.
Predictability vs. Conceivability: While it was conceivable Ross might rob the buyer, it wasn't a "reasonably predictable" part of a standard gun sale.
Conclusion
The Court reversed the robbery conviction. Roy’s misdemeanor intent could not be "stretched" to cover Ross’s independent decision to commit a violent felony.
Notes and Questions: The "Deep Dive"
1. The Judicial "Carve-Outs" (The Chiu Limitation)
Even states that like the Luparello rule are getting cold feet.
People v. Chiu (CA): The California Supreme Court ruled that you cannot use the NPC doctrine for First-Degree Premeditated Murder.
The Logic: Premeditation is "uniquely subjective." You can't "foresee" someone else's careful, deliberate weighing of a choice to kill. It’s too personal to be transferred to an accomplice who wasn't there.
2. The Pinkerton Doctrine vs. NPC
You will see both on exams. They are cousins, but not twins.
NPC Doctrine: Applies to Accomplices.
Pinkerton Doctrine: Applies to Conspirators. It requires an agreement and that the "extra" crime was committed to further the conspiracy's goals.
The Luparello Distinction: Could Luparello be liable under Pinkerton? Likely No. Pinkerton requires the murder to be in "furtherance" of the goal. If the goal was just to get information, killing the source of that information actually thwarts the goal. NPC liability is broader because it doesn't care if the crime helped the goal—only if it was foreseeable.
3. The Model Penal Code (MPC) Critique
The MPC (and many scholars) views the NPC doctrine as a "negligence trap."
The MPC View: Accomplice liability should not be extended beyond the shared purposes of the actors.
The "Negligence" Problem: If the crime requires "Purpose" (like Murder or Robbery), but the NPC doctrine allows a conviction based on "Foreseeability" (Negligence), you are essentially convicting someone of a high-intent crime using a low-intent standard.
Fairly Envisaged: The MPC says you are liable for the means used (e.g., if you help rob a bank and your partner uses a different gun than planned), but not for a "wholly different crime."
