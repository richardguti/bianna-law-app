PART I: JUSTICIABILITY & JUDICIAL REVIEW

1. Judicial Review — Marbury v. Madison (1803)

Rule: The Supreme Court has the authority to declare acts of Congress unconstitutional. "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is."

Fisher Alert: This is the structural bedrock. Every ConLaw answer begins with the assumption that the Court can review the law.

2. Standing (Article III, § 2)

The Lujan Three-Prong Test

Prong Requirement Key Case
Injury in Fact Concrete, particularized, actual/imminent Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife
Causation Fairly traceable to defendant's conduct Allen v. Wright
Redressability Favorable decision would likely remedy injury Simon v. Eastern KY Welfare Rights

Decision Tree: Standing

Does P have INJURY IN FACT?
├─ NO → No Standing. Case dismissed.
└─ YES → Is injury TRACEABLE to D's conduct?
    ├─ NO → No Standing.
    └─ YES → Would a favorable ruling REDRESS the injury?
        ├─ NO → No Standing.
        └─ YES → P HAS STANDING. Proceed to merits.

Special Standing Rules

Doctrine Rule Key Case
Taxpayer Standing Generally NO standing to challenge federal spending. Exception: Establishment Clause challenges to congressional exercises of taxing/spending power Flast v. Cohen
Third-Party Standing Generally NO. Exception: close relationship + hindrance to third party asserting own rights Craig v. Boren (beer vendor for young men)
Organizational Standing Org can sue on behalf of members if: (1) members have standing; (2) interests germane to purpose; (3) individual participation not required Hunt v. Washington Apple

3. Mootness & Ripeness

Doctrine Rule Exception
Mootness Case must present a live controversy at all stages "Capable of repetition yet evading review" (Roe v. Wade)
Ripeness Case must not be premature; actual harm must be imminent Pre-enforcement challenges allowed if threat of prosecution is credible

4. Political Question Doctrine — Baker v. Carr (1962)

The Baker Factors (any one = political question): 1. Textually demonstrable constitutional commitment to another branch 2. Lack of judicially discoverable/manageable standards 3. Impossibility of deciding without an initial policy determination 4. Impossibility of resolving without disrespecting another branch 5. Unusual need for unquestioning adherence to a political decision 6. Potential for embarrassment from conflicting pronouncements

Fisher Tip: Gerrymandering (Rucho v. Common Cause) = political question. Malapportionment (Baker v. Carr) = justiciable.

PART II: FEDERAL LEGISLATIVE POWER

1. The Commerce Clause (Art. I, § 8, cl. 3)

The Lopez Three Categories

Category Definition Example
Channels Highways, waterways, internet, airways Heart of Atlanta Motel
Instrumentalities Persons/things in interstate commerce (trucks, planes, people) Shreveport Rate Cases
Substantial Effects Activities with a substantial effect on interstate commerce Wickard v. Filburn, Gonzales v. Raich

Decision Tree: Commerce Clause

Is the regulated activity in a CHANNEL of interstate commerce?
├─ YES → Valid under Commerce Power.
└─ NO → Is it an INSTRUMENTALITY or person/thing IN commerce?
    ├─ YES → Valid.
    └─ NO → Does it have a SUBSTANTIAL EFFECT on interstate commerce?
        ├─ Is it ECONOMIC activity? (*Raich*)
        │   ├─ YES → Aggregate effects. Rational basis. Congress wins.
        │   └─ NO → (*Lopez*/*Morrison*) Apply 4 factors:
        │       1. Is it economic? (NO = bad for govt)
        │       2. Jurisdictional element? (hook to specific interstate nexus)
        │       3. Congressional findings? (helpful but not dispositive)
        │       4. Attenuation? (too many causal steps = fails)
        └─ NO → Beyond Commerce Power.

⚠️ The NFIB v. Sebelius Anti-Commandeering Limit

Rule: Congress can regulate existing activity but cannot compel individuals to enter commerce. The individual mandate was NOT valid under Commerce Clause because "inactivity" ≠ "activity."

Fisher Trap: The mandate was upheld under the Taxing Power — the "penalty" functioned as a "tax" (produces revenue, paid to IRS, no scienter requirement).

2. The Necessary & Proper Clause (Art. I, § 8, cl. 18)

Rule (McCulloch v. Maryland): "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consist with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."

Modern Limit (Comstock): The N&P Clause can support federal civil commitment of sexually dangerous prisoners — 5-factor test: 1. Broad, not narrow, construction of N&P 2. Long history of federal involvement 3. Sound reasons for the statute 4. The statute accommodates state interests 5. The statute is narrow in scope

3. Taxing & Spending Power (Art. I, § 8, cl. 1)

Taxing Power

  • Congress can tax virtually anything as long as it produces some revenue
  • NFIB: "Shared responsibility payment" = tax (not a penalty) because it was paid to IRS, produced revenue, and had no scienter requirement

Spending Power — South Dakota v. Dole (1987)

Factor Requirement
General Welfare Spending must serve general welfare (broad deference)
Unambiguous Conditions States must know what they're agreeing to
Germaneness Conditions must relate to the federal interest in the program
No Independent Bar Cannot violate other constitutional provisions
No Coercion Cannot be so coercive it's a "gun to the head" (NFIB)

Fisher Alert: NFIB Medicaid expansion was unconstitutional coercion — threatening to revoke ALL existing Medicaid funding (10%+ of state budgets) was "economic dragooning."

4. The Tenth Amendment & Anti-Commandeering

Principle Rule Key Case
Anti-Commandeering (Legislative) Congress cannot compel state legislatures to enact/enforce federal programs New York v. United States
Anti-Commandeering (Executive) Congress cannot compel state executive officers to administer federal programs Printz v. United States
General Applicability OK Laws that apply to states AND private parties are fine Garcia v. San Antonio

PART III: DORMANT COMMERCE CLAUSE (DCC)

Decision Tree: DCC Analysis

Does a STATE law burden interstate commerce?
├─ Is there a FEDERAL LAW on point?
│   ├─ YES → Preemption analysis (see Part IV). DCC doesn't apply.
│   └─ NO → Continue DCC.
├─ Does the law DISCRIMINATE against out-of-state commerce?
│   ├─ ON ITS FACE → Virtually Per Se Invalid. Strict Scrutiny.
│   │   └─ State must prove: (1) Compelling interest AND (2) No non-discriminatory alternatives
│   │       Key cases: *Philadelphia v. NJ* (waste); *Dean Milk* (milk)
│   ├─ IN EFFECT → Same Strict Scrutiny analysis
│   │   └─ *Hunt v. Washington Apple* (labeling law = facial neutrality, discriminatory effect)
│   └─ NO (Truly Neutral) → Apply the PIKE BALANCING TEST
│       └─ Does the burden on interstate commerce CLEARLY EXCEED
│           the putative local benefits?
│           ├─ YES → Unconstitutional
│           └─ NO → Law is upheld
│               Key case: *Pike v. Bruce Church*
└─ EXCEPTION: Market Participant Doctrine
    └─ State acting as BUYER/SELLER (not regulator) → DCC doesn't apply
        *Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap*; *Reeves v. Stake*

Key Distinctions Table

If the Law... Standard Govt Wins?
Facially discriminates Virtual per se invalidity Almost never
Discriminates in effect Strict Scrutiny Rarely
Is neutral but burdensome Pike Balancing Usually
State is market participant No DCC review Always

DCC vs. Privileges & Immunities (Art. IV, § 2)

Feature DCC P&I Clause
Protects Interstate commerce flow Individual "fundamental" rights (livelihood)
Who can sue? Anyone (including corps) Only natural persons (NO corporations)
Market Participant? Exception applies (state immune) NO exception — Camden
Standard Pike or Strict Scrutiny United Building v. Camden: (1) Does law burden fundamental right? (2) Is there substantial reason? (3) Is it closely tailored?

Fisher Exam Tip: If the plaintiff is a corporation → DCC only. If the plaintiff is a person and the state is a market participant → P&I is the only option (Camden).

PART IV: PREEMPTION (Supremacy Clause, Art. VI)

Decision Tree: Preemption

Is there a FEDERAL LAW and a STATE LAW on the same topic?
├─ EXPRESS PREEMPTION: Does federal law explicitly say "states may not..."?
│   ├─ YES → State law preempted. End.
│   └─ NO → Continue.
├─ FIELD PREEMPTION: Does federal regulation so thoroughly occupy the field
│   that it leaves no room for state law?
│   ├─ YES → State law preempted. (*Arizona v. US* — immigration)
│   └─ NO → Continue.
├─ CONFLICT PREEMPTION:
│   ├─ IMPOSSIBILITY: Is it physically impossible to comply with both?
│   │   ├─ YES → State law preempted.
│   │   └─ NO → Continue.
│   └─ OBSTACLE: Does state law stand as an obstacle to federal objectives?
│       ├─ YES → State law preempted. (*Hines v. Davidowitz*)
│       └─ NO → State law SURVIVES. Both enforced.
└─ PRESUMPTION: Against preemption in areas of traditional state regulation
    (*Medtronic v. Lohr*)

PART V: SEPARATION OF POWERS

1. Executive Power Framework — Youngstown (Jackson Concurrence)

Zone Description Standard
Zone 1 President acts WITH congressional authorization Maximum authority. Presumption of validity.
Zone 2 President acts in congressional SILENCE "Zone of twilight." Concurrent authority. Case-by-case.
Zone 3 President acts AGAINST congressional will "Lowest ebb." Power at minimum. Can only prevail if Congress lacks authority over the matter.

2. Executive Privilege & Immunity

Doctrine Rule Key Case
Executive Privilege Presumptive privilege for presidential communications; yields to specific need in criminal proceedings United States v. Nixon
Civil Immunity Absolute immunity for official acts; NO immunity for unofficial/private acts Clinton v. Jones; Nixon v. Fitzgerald
Criminal Immunity Absolute for core Art. II functions; Presumptive for official acts; None for unofficial acts Trump v. United States (2024)

Trump v. United States — Tripartite Immunity Test

Was the President's act...
├─ A CORE constitutional function (e.g., pardon, commander-in-chief)?
│   └─ ABSOLUTE IMMUNITY. Cannot be prosecuted. Period.
├─ An OFFICIAL ACT (within outer perimeter of duties)?
│   └─ PRESUMPTIVE IMMUNITY. Prosecution must prove no intrusion on executive function.
└─ An UNOFFICIAL/PRIVATE act?
    └─ NO IMMUNITY. Fully subject to prosecution.

3. Non-Delegation Doctrine

Rule: Congress cannot delegate legislative power without an "intelligible principle" to guide the executive. (Whitman v. American Trucking)

Reality: The Court has only struck down delegations twice (both 1935). Modern delegations are almost always upheld.

4. Appointment & Removal

Power Rule
Appointments (Art. II, § 2) Principal officers: President appoints, Senate confirms. Inferior officers: Congress may vest in President, courts, or department heads.
Removal President has implied removal power over executive officers (Myers). Congress can limit removal of quasi-judicial/quasi-legislative officers to "good cause" (Humphrey's Executor).

PART VI: INCORPORATION

The Doctrine

Rule: The 14th Amendment's Due Process Clause selectively incorporates most Bill of Rights protections against the states.

Approach Description Status
Total Incorporation All of the Bill of Rights apply to states Rejected (Black's view)
Selective Incorporation Only "fundamental" rights apply Current Law (McDonald v. Chicago)
Total Inc. + Extras All BoR + other unenumerated rights Rejected (but SDP does add extras)

Test: Is the right "fundamental to our scheme of ordered liberty" or "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition"?

Not Incorporated: Grand Jury indictment (5th), Excessive fines (partially), Civil jury trial (7th — but see recent developments).

PART VII: SUBSTANTIVE DUE PROCESS (SDP)

The Two Competing Methodologies

Method Approach Champion Modern Status
Glucksberg ("Narrow") Right must be "deeply rooted in history and tradition" + "carefully described" Rehnquist, Alito DOMINANT after Dobbs
Lawrence/Obergefell ("Broad") Tradition is a "living thing"; look at emerging awareness of injustice Kennedy, Souter Weakened but not dead

The Text, History & Tradition (THT) Framework (Bruen/Dobbs/Heller)

Is a FUNDAMENTAL RIGHT claimed?
├─ Step 1: Is the right DEEPLY ROOTED in this Nation's history and tradition?
│   ├─ YES → Fundamental right. Apply Strict Scrutiny.
│   └─ NO → NOT a fundamental right. Apply Rational Basis.
├─ Step 2: CAREFULLY DESCRIBE the right (narrow framing per Glucksberg)
│   └─ "Right to physician-assisted suicide" NOT "right to autonomy"
└─ Step 3: HISTORICAL ANALOGUES — is there a historical tradition of
    regulating this conduct? (*Bruen* for 2A; *Dobbs* for abortion)

Fisher Exam Strategy: - Want law upheld? → Cite Glucksberg THT. Frame right narrowly. Show no historical protection. - Want law struck down? → Cite Lawrence/Obergefell dignity framework. Frame right broadly.

The SDP Evolution: Key Cases

A. Economic SDP (Dead)

Era Rule Key Case
Lochner Era (1905–1937) "Liberty of contract" is fundamental; courts will second-guess economic regulation Lochner v. New York
Post-New Deal (1937+) Economic regulation gets Rational Basis only; courts defer completely West Coast Hotel v. Parrish; Williamson v. Lee Optical

Fisher Tip: Lochner is the "ghost" — any time a Justice accuses the majority of "judicial activism," they invoke Lochner. The fear of being a "Lochner court" constrains SDP expansion.

B. Privacy & Contraception

Case Rule
Griswold v. CT (1965) Right to marital privacy → "penumbras" of BoR → struck down contraception ban for married couples
Eisenstadt v. Baird (1972) Extended to unmarried individuals — "If the right means anything, it is the right of the individual"

C. Abortion — The Doctrinal Arc

Case Standard Status
Roe v. Wade (1973) Trimester framework; right to abortion is fundamental OVERRULED
Planned Parenthood v. Casey (1992) Undue Burden test replaces trimesters; viability line retained OVERRULED
Dobbs v. Jackson (2022) No constitutional right to abortion. Returned to states. Rational Basis. CURRENT LAW

Dobbs — The Controlling Framework

Holding: The Constitution does not confer a right to abortion. Roe and Casey are overruled.

The Test Applied: 1. Is abortion "deeply rooted in history and tradition"? → NO (criminalized in 3/4 of states at ratification of 14th Amendment) 2. Is it "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty"? → NO 3. Therefore: Rational Basis Review → Mississippi's 15-week ban is rationally related to legitimate interests (potential life, maternal health, medical ethics)

Fisher Traps: - Dobbs does NOT say states must ban abortion — only that states MAY - The Dobbs majority insists its logic does NOT threaten Griswold, Lawrence, or Obergefell (but Concurrence Thomas disagrees) - Exam move: Always mention the Thomas concurrence suggesting ALL SDP rights should be reconsidered

D. Marriage as a Fundamental Right

Case Rule Scrutiny
Loving v. Virginia (1967) Marriage is a "basic civil right of man"; anti-miscegenation laws fail Strict Scrutiny on EP AND DP grounds Strict
Zablocki v. Redhail (1978) Cannot condition marriage on payment of child support Strict
Turner v. Safley (1987) Even prisoners retain fundamental right to marry Reasonableness (prison context)
Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) Same-sex couples have fundamental right to marry under DP + EP "synergy" Effectively Strict (Kennedy never says it)

Obergefell — The "Double Helix"

Kennedy's Four Principles: 1. Individual autonomy in marriage choice 2. Marriage supports a unique two-person union 3. Marriage safeguards children and families 4. Marriage is a "keystone of social order"

The Synergy: DP protects the liberty to marry; EP ensures the state can't grant it to some but deny it to others. Together they create a "double helix" — the state cannot "level down" by abolishing marriage entirely.

Fisher Exam Strategy: If a fact pattern involves a "dignity" interest: 1. Argue Strict Scrutiny (fundamental right under Loving/Skinner) 2. Alternative: "Rational Basis with Bite" — moral disapproval/animus is never legitimate (Romer/Lawrence)

E. The "Right to Die" Spectrum

Category Legal Status Key Case
Refusing treatment (competent patient) Protected liberty interest Cruzan (assumed)
Refusing treatment (incompetent) State may require "clear and convincing evidence" Cruzan
Physician-assisted suicide NOT a fundamental right Glucksberg
Active euthanasia No protection; state can criminalize No case on point

Glucksberg — The "History and Tradition" Fortress

Holding: No fundamental right to physician-assisted suicide. Rational Basis.

State Interests (all legitimate): 1. Preserving human life 2. Preventing suicide 3. Protecting vulnerable populations (elderly/disabled) 4. Protecting medical profession integrity 5. Preventing slippery slope to euthanasia

Fisher Tip — Distinguish the Methods: - Passive (removing a tube) = Cruzan = more protected - Active (providing a pill) = Glucksberg = not protected - Palliative care (pain meds that hasten death) = "Double Effect" loophole — intent is pain relief, not death

F. Sexual Intimacy

Case Rule
Bowers v. Hardwick (1986) No fundamental right to sodomy (defined narrowly) OVERRULED
Lawrence v. Texas (2003) Adults have liberty interest in consensual intimate conduct. Anti-sodomy laws violate SDP. Kennedy avoids naming scrutiny level.

Lawrence's Lasting Impact: - "Moral disapproval" alone is NOT a legitimate state interest - Paved the way for Windsor → Obergefell - Uses "Rational Basis with Bite" (or dignity-based review)

PART VIII: PROCEDURAL DUE PROCESS (PDP)

Step 1: Is There a Protected Interest?

Type Source Key Case
Life Self-evident —
Liberty Freedom from physical restraint + other fundamental interests Board of Regents v. Roth
Property NOT just real property — includes entitlements, licenses, welfare benefits, continued employment (if "for cause" only) Goldberg v. Kelly; Roth

The "New Property" (Goldberg v. Kelly): Government benefits (welfare, licenses, permits) = property interests if there's a legitimate claim of entitlement under state law.

Fisher Tip: "Rights vs. Privileges" distinction is DEAD. If the government creates an entitlement, they must provide process before taking it away.

Step 2: What Process Is Due?

The Mathews v. Eldridge Balancing Test (1976)

Three Factors to Balance:
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ (1) Private Interest: How important is the      │
│     interest to the individual?                  │
│                                                  │
│ (2) Risk of Error: What's the risk of erroneous │
│     deprivation under current procedures? Would  │
│     additional safeguards reduce that risk?       │
│                                                  │
│ (3) Government Interest: What's the fiscal and  │
│     administrative burden of additional process? │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
Situation Process Required Case
Welfare termination Pre-termination hearing (oral) Goldberg v. Kelly
Disability benefits Post-termination paper review sufficient Mathews v. Eldridge
Public employment (for cause) Pre-termination notice + opportunity to respond Cleveland Bd. v. Loudermill
Reputation alone NO due process (stigma alone ≠ liberty) unless coupled with loss of tangible interest ("stigma-plus") Paul v. Davis

Fisher Alert — "Good Name" Interest: You have a property interest in your reputation only if the government's defamatory statement is accompanied by a concrete loss (firing, license revocation). Stigma alone = no process due.

PART IX: EQUAL PROTECTION (14th Amend. § 1)

"No State shall... deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws."

Structural Note: EP applies to states via the 14th Amendment. It applies to the federal government via the 5th Amendment Due Process Clause ("Reverse Incorporation" — Bolling v. Sharpe).

THE MASTER SCRUTINY TABLE

Level Classification Test Burden Gov't Wins?
Strict Scrutiny Race, National Origin, Alienage (state law) Compelling interest + Narrowly tailored Government Rarely
Intermediate Scrutiny Sex/Gender Important interest + Substantially related + "Exceedingly persuasive justification" Government Sometimes
Rational Basis Age, Disability, Poverty, Economic regulation Legitimate interest + Rationally related Challenger Almost never (gov't wins)
Rational Basis "with Bite" Animus-based laws targeting unpopular groups Legitimate interest + Rationally related (but Court actually looks closely) Unclear (Court says challenger) Gov't loses when animus found

DECISION TREE: Equal Protection Analysis

Does a LAW treat people DIFFERENTLY?
├─ YES → What is the BASIS of the classification?
│   ├─ RACE or NATIONAL ORIGIN → STRICT SCRUTINY
│   │   ├─ Is the law FACIALLY discriminatory?
│   │   │   ├─ YES → Strict Scrutiny automatically
│   │   │   └─ NO (Facially Neutral) → Must prove DISCRIMINATORY INTENT
│   │   │       └─ Impact alone NOT enough (*Washington v. Davis*)
│   │   │       └─ Use *Arlington Heights* factors to find intent
│   │   └─ Apply: Compelling Interest + Narrow Tailoring
│   │
│   ├─ SEX/GENDER → INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY
│   │   ├─ Facially discriminatory? → Intermediate automatically
│   │   ├─ Facially neutral? → Must prove intent (*Feeney* "because of" test)
│   │   └─ Apply: Important Interest + Substantially Related
│   │       + "Exceedingly Persuasive Justification" (*VMI*)
│   │
│   ├─ ALIENAGE →
│   │   ├─ STATE law → Strict Scrutiny (general rule)
│   │   │   └─ EXCEPTION: "Political Function" jobs → Rational Basis
│   │   │       (cops, teachers = OK to exclude; notaries = NOT OK)
│   │   └─ FEDERAL law → Rational Basis (broad deference to immigration power)
│   │
│   └─ AGE / DISABILITY / WEALTH → RATIONAL BASIS
│       └─ BUT check for ANIMUS → *Cleburne*/*Moreno*/*Romer*
│           └─ "Bare desire to harm" is NEVER legitimate
│
└─ NO differential treatment → No EP claim.

SECTION 1: RATIONAL BASIS REVIEW

The Standard

Rule: A classification is constitutional if it is rationally related to a legitimate state interest. The court hypothesizes reasons — the government doesn't need to articulate its actual purpose.

Principle Rule Case
Hypothesized Reasons OK Court can invent a reason the legislature never stated Railway Express v. NY; Fritz
One Step at a Time Legislature can attack a problem piecemeal Williamson v. Lee Optical
Underinclusivity OK Law need not address all evils at once Railway Express
Overinclusivity OK Law can be imperfect in its reach Lee Optical

Rational Basis "with Bite" — The Animus Exception

Case What Happened Why It Failed
USDA v. Moreno (1973) Food stamp denial to "hippie" households "Bare desire to harm a politically unpopular group" = never legitimate
City of Cleburne (1985) Zoning denied for group home for disabled Frat houses/nursing homes allowed in same zone → irrational prejudice, not safety
Romer v. Evans (1996) CO amendment stripped all LGBT protections "Animus" — law so broad it could only be explained by hostility
Village of Willowbrook v. Olech (2000) 33-ft easement demanded vs. 15-ft for neighbors "Class of one" — irrational and wholly arbitrary treatment of individual

Fisher Tip: Even when alleging animus, if the government can articulate a good economic rational basis, the law will probably be upheld (Beazer). Merely alluding to animus isn't enough.

SECTION 2: RACE DISCRIMINATION

A. The Historical Arc

Era Doctrine Key Case
1880 Political rights protected Strauder v. WV (jury exclusion struck down)
1896 "Separate but Equal" Plessy v. Ferguson
1938–50 Attacking "Equal" in graduate schools Sweatt v. Painter (intangibles)
1954 "Separate is inherently unequal" Brown v. Board
1955 Remedy: "All deliberate speed" Brown II
1967 Interracial marriage protected Loving v. Virginia
1968 Affirmative duty to integrate "root and branch" Green v. County School Bd.

B. Proving Discrimination — Facial vs. Neutral Laws

Is the law FACIALLY discriminatory (mentions race)?
├─ YES → STRICT SCRUTINY automatically
│   └─ Even "equal application" is no defense (*Loving*)
│   └─ Even in PRISON → Strict Scrutiny (*Johnson v. California*)
└─ NO (Facially Neutral) →
    Must prove DISCRIMINATORY PURPOSE/INTENT
    └─ Impact alone is NOT enough (*Washington v. Davis*)
    └─ Use the *ARLINGTON HEIGHTS* factors:
        1. Disproportionate impact (starting point, not enough alone)
        2. Historical background of discrimination
        3. Sequence of events (sudden rule changes)
        4. Procedural departures (skipped meetings/notice)
        5. Substantive departures (ignored usual criteria)
        6. Legislative history (floor statements, minutes)
    └─ If plaintiff proves race was a "MOTIVATING FACTOR":
        → BURDEN SHIFTS to government (*Mt. Healthy*)
        → Gov't can survive if it proves SAME DECISION
          would have been made anyway ("but-for" defense)

C. Korematsu and Facial Discrimination Against Minorities

Birth of Strict Scrutiny: "All legal restrictions which curtail the civil rights of a single racial group are immediately suspect... courts must subject them to the most rigid scrutiny." — Korematsu v. United States (1944)

Overruled: Trump v. Hawaii (2018): "Korematsu was gravely wrong the day it was decided."

Fisher Exam Move: If government claims "national security" for a racial classification: 1. State Strict Scrutiny applies (Korematsu/Loving) 2. Argue Korematsu is "gravely wrong" per Trump v. Hawaii 3. Apply Narrow Tailoring: Could non-racial means achieve safety? (Usually yes)

D. Affirmative Action — The Rise and Fall

Case Holding Status
Bakke (1978) Quotas = unconstitutional. "Plus factor" for diversity = OK. Powell's "Harvard Plan." Superseded
Grutter v. Bollinger (2003) Law school holistic review OK — diversity is compelling OVERRULED
Gratz v. Bollinger (2003) Mechanical point system = unconstitutional (not individualized) Still good law
Fisher I (2013) Courts must apply Strict Scrutiny rigorously, not defer to university Still good law
SFFA v. Harvard (2023) Race-conscious admissions unconstitutional. No measurable goals, relies on stereotypes, no logical endpoint. CURRENT LAW

Current Rule: ALL racial classifications get Strict Scrutiny regardless of "benign" or "hostile" motive.

Permissible Justifications: - ✅ Remedying specific, proven past discrimination by that entity - ❌ Remedying "societal discrimination" (too amorphous) - ❌ Diversity in higher education (expired per SFFA)

Fisher Tip: Never say "reverse discrimination." The Court treats ALL race-based government action the same way.

SECTION 3: SEX/GENDER DISCRIMINATION

The Standard: Intermediate Scrutiny (Craig v. Boren, 1976)

Test: Classification must serve important governmental objectives and be substantially related to achievement of those objectives.

Enhanced: Must provide an "exceedingly persuasive justification" (VMI/Hogan)

Key Cases

Case Holding Principle
Reed v. Reed (1971) Struck down preference for males as estate administrators First sex-discrimination win
Craig v. Boren (1976) Struck down OK beer age law (21 for men, 18 for women) Established Intermediate Scrutiny
Mississippi Univ. v. Hogan (1982) All-female nursing school must admit men "Compensatory" argument fails if it reinforces stereotypes
US v. Virginia (VMI) (1996) VMI must admit women; VWIL is "pale shadow" Cannot exclude based on generalizations about "most women"
Sessions v. Morales-Santana (2017) Struck down citizenship residency disparity "Leveling down" remedy: took benefit from mothers rather than extending to fathers

Sex-Based Purpose Requirement — Feeney (1979)

Rule: Facially neutral law with disproportionate sex-based impact is NOT unconstitutional without proof of discriminatory purpose.

The "Because Of" Test: Legislature must have acted "because of, not merely in spite of," the adverse effects on women.

Fisher Trap: Foreseeability ≠ Intent. Even if the legislature knew a law would disadvantage women, that's insufficient. They must have wanted it.

"Benign" Sex Preferences — The "Lady vs. Woman" Test

Type Outcome Example
"Lady" Preferences (based on stereotype of female dependency) STRUCK DOWN Orr v. Orr (alimony); Wiesenfeld (Social Security)
"Woman" Preferences (based on documented economic disadvantage) UPHELD Webster (Social Security formula); Ballard (Navy promotion)

Pregnancy

Rule (Geduldig v. Aiello): Excluding pregnancy from disability insurance is NOT sex discrimination (divides "pregnant persons" vs. "non-pregnant persons").

Override: Congress enacted the Pregnancy Discrimination Act for employment. But Geduldig remains the constitutional rule.

Age Rational Basis Murgia We all age → not "discrete and insular"
Disability Rational Basis Cleburne But see "RB with Bite" when animus present
Poverty Rational Basis Rodriguez School funding disparities upheld

Fisher Tip — Fundamental Rights Bypass: Even if a group isn't suspect (disability), if the law affects a fundamental right (voting, courts, travel), Strict Scrutiny may still apply regardless of the classification.

PART X: THE STATE ACTION DOCTRINE

The 14th Amendment applies ONLY to STATE action. Private conduct — no matter how discriminatory — is not covered. Civil Rights Cases (1883).

Decision Tree: Is There State Action?

Is the DEFENDANT a government entity or official?
├─ YES → STATE ACTION exists. Apply 14th Amendment.
└─ NO (Private Actor) → Run through the EXCEPTIONS:
    │
    ├─ 1. PUBLIC FUNCTION TEST
    │   └─ Does the private entity exercise powers "traditionally
    │      and EXCLUSIVELY reserved to the State"?
    │       ├─ Running a town → YES (*Marsh v. Alabama*)
    │       ├─ Running an election → YES (*Smith v. Allwright*; *Terry v. Adams*)
    │       ├─ Running a park → YES (*Evans v. Newton*)
    │       ├─ Shopping center → NO (*Hudgens v. NLRB*)
    │       ├─ Utility company → NO (*Jackson v. Metro Edison*)
    │       └─ Warehouse lien sale → NO (*Flagg Bros. v. Brooks*)
    │
    ├─ 2. JUDICIAL ENFORCEMENT (*Shelley v. Kraemer*)
    │   └─ Is a STATE COURT enforcing a discriminatory private agreement?
    │       ├─ YES → State Action (court is the engine of discrimination)
    │       └─ NO (enforcing neutral law like trespass) → No State Action
    │       └─ Extends to money damages for breach (*Barrows v. Jackson*)
    │
    ├─ 3. SYMBIOTIC RELATIONSHIP (*Burton v. Wilmington Parking Auth.*)
    │   └─ Are the state and private actor so INTERTWINED that the state
    │      profits from the discrimination?
    │       ├─ Lease in public building + mutual benefit → YES (*Burton*)
    │       ├─ Mere state licensing → NO (*Moose Lodge v. Irvis*)
    │       └─ Mere regulation → NO (*Jackson*)
    │
    ├─ 4. STATE ENCOURAGEMENT (*Reitman v. Mulkey*)
    │   └─ Did the state AFFIRMATIVELY authorize/encourage discrimination
    │      (e.g., amending constitution to protect right to discriminate)?
    │       ├─ YES → State Action
    │       └─ Mere repeal of anti-discrimination law → Arguable (*Harlan dissent*)
    │
    ├─ 5. JOINT PARTICIPATION (*Lugar v. Edmondson Oil*)
    │   └─ Did the private actor use STATE OFFICIALS (sheriff, clerk, judge)
    │      to carry out the act?
    │       ├─ YES → State Action
    │       └─ *Edmonson v. Leesville*: peremptory challenges in court = State Action
    │
    ├─ 6. ENTWINEMENT (*Brentwood Academy v. TN Secondary School*)
    │   └─ Is the private org so permeated by public officials (84% public
    │      school members, state retirement system) that it IS the state?
    │       ├─ YES → State Action
    │       └─ Must be "pervasive" entwinement, not just occasional overlap
    │
    └─ 7. NEGATIVE LIBERTY (*DeShaney v. Winnebago County*)
        └─ Did the state FAIL to protect someone from a private actor?
            ├─ Generally NO State Action — DP is a shield, not a sword
            └─ EXCEPTION: State has CUSTODY of the person (prison, hospital)
                → Affirmative duty to protect arises

The 13th Amendment — The "Green Light" for Private Conduct

Key Distinction: Unlike the 14th Amendment, the 13th Amendment is NOT limited to state action. It bans slavery AND empowers Congress to abolish the "badges and incidents of slavery."

Case Holding Reach
Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer (1968) § 1982 bars private racial discrimination in property sales Congress defines "badges of slavery"
Sullivan v. Little Hunting Park (1969) Extended to community facility membership Lease-related discrimination
Runyon v. McCrary (1976) § 1981 bars private schools from race-based admissions Commercial operations can't exclude by race
Griffin v. Breckenridge (1971) § 1985(3) covers purely private racial conspiracies Requires "class-based invidiously discriminatory animus"

Limit: The 13th Amendment power is generally limited to racial discrimination. It does not reach gender (Bray) or economic (Carpenters v. Scott) animus.

The 14th Amendment, § 5 — "Appropriate Legislation"

Two Theories of Congressional Power

Theory Definition Champion
Remedial Congress can only provide mechanisms to fix violations the Court has already found Boerne (dominant)
Prophylactic Congress can proactively prevent future violations, even those the Court hasn't yet declared Katzenbach v. Morgan (limited)

The Katzenbach v. Morgan "Ratchet"

Holding: Congress can use § 5 to ban literacy tests for Puerto Rican voters, even though the Court in Lassiter said literacy tests were constitutional.

The "One-Way Ratchet" (Footnote 10): Congress can expand rights beyond the Court's floor. Congress can NOT restrict or dilute rights below that floor.

The City of Boerne "Congruence & Proportionality" Test

Holding: RFRA is unconstitutional as applied to states. Congress cannot use § 5 to redefine constitutional rights.

The Test:

Is the federal law a valid exercise of § 5?
├─ (1) CONGRUENCE: Is the remedy closely related to an identified
│      constitutional injury (a pattern of STATE violations)?
│   ├─ YES → Continue
│   └─ NO → Unconstitutional (law is "substantive" not "remedial")
│
├─ (2) PROPORTIONALITY: Is the remedy proportional to the harm?
│   ├─ "Surgical" fix for documented violations → VALID
│   └─ "Sledgehammer" nationwide ban without record → INVALID
│
└─ KEY: Who is the DEFENDANT?
    ├─ State Actor → Potentially valid under § 5
    └─ Private Actor → Almost certainly FAILS (*Morrison*)

§ 5 Scorecard: What Passed and What Failed

Law Passed? Why? Case
Voting Rights Act (§ 5 preclearance) ✅ Massive record of state voter discrimination South Carolina v. Katzenbach
VRA literacy test ban (nationwide) ✅ Unanimous — prejudice was "prevalent throughout the Nation" Oregon v. Mitchell
FMLA (family care leave) ✅ Gender = heightened scrutiny → easier to show pattern Nevada v. Hibbs
ADA Title II (access to courts) ✅ Protects fundamental right of court access Tennessee v. Lane

ADA Title I (employment) ❌ Disability = rational basis → anecdotal record only Board of Trustees v. Garrett
VAWA (civil remedy) ❌ Targets private actors, not states; § 5 can't reach private conduct United States v. Morrison
VRA § 4(b) coverage formula ❌ Data 50 years old; "equal sovereignty" violated Shelby County v. Holder

Shelby County — The VRA Gutted

Holding: § 4(b) coverage formula is unconstitutional because it's based on 40-year-old data that no longer reflects current conditions.

Equal Sovereignty Principle: Federal government must justify treating states differently based on current needs, not historical ones.

Ginsburg's Dissent (the "Umbrella"): "Throwing out preclearance when it has worked... is like throwing away your umbrella in a rainstorm because you are not getting wet."

Post-Shelby Reality: - § 5 preclearance is "inoperable" without a coverage formula - § 2 (results test, nationwide) is now the primary tool - Brnovich v. DNC (2021) further limited § 2 with restrictive "guideposts"

PART XIII: FREEDOM OF SPEECH

Framework Summary

Restriction Type Scrutiny Key Test
Content-Based Strict Scrutiny (compelling interest, narrowly tailored) Reed v. Town of Gilbert
Content-Neutral (time, place, manner) Intermediate Scrutiny (important interest, narrowly tailored, ample alternative channels) Ward v. Rock Against Racism
Expressive Conduct O'Brien Test (see below) United States v. O'Brien
Commercial Speech Central Hudson Intermediate Scrutiny (see below) Central Hudson Gas v. Public Serv. Comm'n
Prior Restraint Heavy presumption of invalidity Near v. Minnesota; Pentagon Papers
Overbroad / Vague Statutory challenge; can chill protected speech Broadrick v. Oklahoma; Grayned v. Rockford

Fisher Tip: The first move in any speech question is classification. Content-based vs. content-neutral determines everything — scrutiny level, burden, and outcome. Get this wrong and the entire analysis collapses.

Decision Tree: Content-Based vs. Content-Neutral

Does the law restrict SPEECH?
├─ Is it a PRIOR RESTRAINT?
│   └─ Presumptively unconstitutional. Government must show:
│       1. Extreme harm
│       2. Narrowly drawn
│       (Near v. Minnesota; N.Y. Times v. U.S.)
├─ Is the regulation CONTENT-BASED?
│   ├─ Does it target specific subject matter or viewpoint?
│   │   └─ YES → STRICT SCRUTINY. (Reed v. Town of Gilbert)
│   │       └─ Compelling interest + least restrictive means
│   └─ NO (Content-Neutral) → INTERMEDIATE SCRUTINY
│       └─ Time, place, manner or expressive conduct
│       └─ Important gov't interest, narrowly tailored,
│           ample alternative channels of communication
└─ If EXPRESSIVE CONDUCT → O'BRIEN TEST:
     1. Government interest unrelated to suppression of expression
     2. Interest is important / substantial
     3. Restriction is no greater than essential
     (United States v. O'Brien)

Fisher Alert: Reed v. Town of Gilbert (2015) was a game-changer. A law that draws distinctions based on the message conveyed is content-based — even if the government claims a neutral purpose. If the regulation requires you to read the speech to know if it applies, it is content-based. Period.

Major Speech Doctrine Tables

A. Incitement / Advocacy of Illegal Action

Case Rule
Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969) Speech advocating force/lawlessness is protected UNLESS it is (1) directed to inciting imminent lawless action AND (2) likely to produce such action. (Overrules clear-and-present-danger)
Hess v. Indiana (1973) Mere "might cause" future harm insufficient; immediacy required

Fisher Tip: Brandenburg is the single most important speech case on the exam. The two-prong test (imminent + likely) is conjunctive — both must be met. A fiery speech at a protest is protected unless violence is about to happen right now.

Fisher Trap: Do NOT confuse Brandenburg with the old "clear and present danger" test (Schenck). That standard is dead. If you cite Schenck as controlling law, you've lost the point.

B. Fighting Words & Hostile Speech

Doctrine Rule
Chaplinsky v. NH (1942) "Fighting words" — personal insults that provoke immediate breach of peace — are unprotected.
Modern limitation Fighting words doctrine is still nominally valid but rarely upheld; statutes are often struck for overbreadth / content discrimination. (R.A.V. v. St. Paul)
True Threats Unprotected if a reasonable person would view the statement as a serious expression of intent to harm. (Virginia v. Black)

Fisher Alert: R.A.V. v. St. Paul (1992) is the "trap within a trap." Even within the unprotected category of fighting words, the government cannot discriminate based on viewpoint. The St. Paul ordinance banned only fighting words based on race, color, creed — that's content discrimination within an unprotected category. Scalia's logic: the government can ban all fighting words, but it cannot selectively ban only certain fighting words based on their message.

C. Defamation & First Amendment

Case Rule
N.Y. Times v. Sullivan (1964) Public officials must prove "actual malice" (knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for truth).
Gertz v. Welch (1974) Private figures: no need for actual malice; states can set their own liability standard but must show fault.

Fisher Tip — The "Who Is the Plaintiff?" Shortcut: - Public Official/Figure → Actual malice required (Sullivan/Curtis Publishing) - Private Figure + Public Concern → Fault standard set by state, but no presumed/punitive damages without actual malice (Gertz) - Private Figure + Private Concern → State common law controls (Dun & Bradstreet)

D. Obscenity — The Miller Test

Miller v. California (1973): Material is obscene (and unprotected) if a trier of fact, applying contemporary community standards, finds:

  1. The work, taken as a whole, appeals to the prurient interest in sex.
  2. It depicts/describes sexual conduct in a patently offensive way (specifically defined by state law).
  3. The work, taken as a whole, lacks serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value (SLAPS test — national standard).

Fisher Trap: Prong 3 (SLAPS) uses a national standard, not a community standard. This is the key distinction students miss. A work can appeal to prurient interest in a conservative community (prongs 1-2) but still be protected if it has serious artistic value nationally (prong 3).

Child Pornography: Separate category, no Miller test required; totally unprotected (New York v. Ferber). Even private possession is not protected (Osborne v. Ohio). Compare: private possession of obscenity by adults IS protected (Stanley v. Georgia).

E. Commercial Speech — The Central Hudson Test

Central Hudson Gas v. Public Serv. Comm'n (1980) Four-part intermediate scrutiny for regulations of commercial speech:

  1. Is the speech lawful and not misleading? (If misleading/unlawful → no protection)
  2. Is the government's interest substantial?
  3. Does the regulation directly advance that interest?
  4. Is the regulation no more extensive than necessary? (Reasonable fit, not least restrictive means)

Fisher Alert: Step 4 does NOT require "least restrictive means" — only a "reasonable fit." This is a common exam trap. Commercial speech gets intermediate, not strict, scrutiny. The government has more room here than with political speech.

Post-Sorrell v. IMS Health (2011): The Court sometimes applies "heightened" rather than relaxed intermediate scrutiny when law targets specific content/viewpoint within commercial speech — blurring the line. Flag this on an exam as an evolving area.

F. Speech Forums Analysis

Forum Government Power Key Case
Traditional Public Forum (streets, parks) Strict scrutiny for content-based restrictions; content-neutral time, place, manner allowed. Hague v. CIO
Designated Public Forum (opened for speech by choice) Same as traditional public forum while open. Government can close it, but while open, cannot discriminate. Widmar v. Vincent
Nonpublic Forum (military base, jail, gov't offices) Reasonable, viewpoint-neutral regulations OK. Perry Education Ass'n
Government Speech Government has its own speech rights; no First Amendment obligation to be viewpoint-neutral. Pleasant Grove v. Summum

Fisher Tip: The forum analysis is really about who owns the space. Traditional public forums have been "dedicated to assembly and debate" since "time immemorial" (Perry). The government cannot convert a traditional public forum into a nonpublic forum just by declaring it so. But it CAN close a designated public forum entirely — it just can't selectively exclude viewpoints while it's open.

Fisher Trap: Government speech is the "escape hatch." If the government is the speaker (monuments, license plate designs, public school curriculum), the First Amendment does not apply. Summum and Walker v. Texas Division confirm this. The trap: students argue viewpoint discrimination when the government is simply choosing its own message.

G. Prior Restraint

Rule: The government cannot censor speech before it occurs, except in the most extraordinary circumstances. (Near v. Minnesota; New York Times v. United States (Pentagon Papers))

Pentagon Papers test: Prior restraint requires proof of direct, immediate, irreparable harm → "grave and certain danger."

Fisher Tip: Prior restraint is the nuclear option — almost never valid. The only established exceptions are (1) troop movements in wartime, (2) obscenity, and (3) certain national security secrets where disclosure would cause "grave and irreparable" harm. Even in the Pentagon Papers case, the government lost — and those were classified defense documents.

Fisher Exam Strategy: If you see a prior restraint on an exam, the government almost certainly loses. Write the analysis but reach the conclusion quickly. The more interesting issue is usually subsequent punishment — which is where the real balancing occurs.

PART XIV: FREEDOM OF RELIGION

A. Free Exercise Clause

The Modern Framework

Does the law burden a sincerely held religious belief?
├─ Is the law NEUTRAL and of GENERAL APPLICABILITY?
│   ├─ YES → RATIONAL BASIS (Smith v. Employment Division, 1990)
│   └─ NO (law targets religion or is not generally applicable)
│       → STRICT SCRUTINY (Lukumi Babalu Aye v. Hialeah)
└─ RFRA (Religious Freedom Restoration Act):
      Applies to FEDERAL actions only.
      Government must show:
        (1) Compelling interest AND
        (2) Least restrictive means.
      (Does NOT apply to states after City of Boerne.)

Fisher Alert — The Smith Revolution: Before Smith (1990), the Court applied strict scrutiny to ALL laws that substantially burdened religion (Sherbert v. Verner). Smith gutted that — now neutral, generally applicable laws get only rational basis. This is why Congress passed RFRA to "restore" strict scrutiny. But Boerne held RFRA exceeds Congress's § 5 power as applied to states. This directly connects to your Section 5 analysis in Part XI.

Key Cases

Case Rule
Employment Division v. Smith (1990) Neutral, generally applicable law does not trigger strict scrutiny, even if it incidentally burdens religion.
Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v. Hialeah (1993) Law that targets a specific religious practice (animal sacrifice) is not neutral; strict scrutiny applies.
Fulton v. Philadelphia (2021) If a law provides for individualized exemptions, it is not "generally applicable"; strict scrutiny applies.
Hosanna-Tabor v. EEOC (2012) Ministerial exception: First Amendment bars employment suits by ministers against their religious employers.

Fisher Tip — "General Applicability" Is the Key Battleground: After Fulton, the practical question is whether the law has any mechanism for individualized exceptions (secular exemptions, waiver provisions, discretionary enforcement). If it does, it's not "generally applicable" and strict scrutiny kicks in — even under Smith. This is how litigants functionally get around Smith without overruling it.

State RFRAs: Many states have their own versions of RFRA; but the federal RFRA cannot be enforced against states (Boerne). On an exam, always check: is the actor federal (RFRA applies) or state (only Smith/Lukumi framework applies, unless state has its own RFRA)?

B. Establishment Clause

Modern trend: The Court has moved away from the Lemon test toward a "historical practices and understandings" approach, often referencing coercion and historical tradition.

Lemon v. Kurtzman (1971) — The Old (But Not Dead) Test

  1. Statute must have a secular legislative purpose.
  2. Principal or primary effect must neither advance nor inhibit religion.
  3. Must not foster excessive government entanglement with religion.

The New Direction (Post-Kennedy v. Bremerton, 2022)

  • The Establishment Clause must be interpreted by reference to "historical practices and understandings."
  • Government coercion of religious exercise is a core evil.
  • Purely symbolic or historical displays (Ten Commandments monument, legislative prayer) are often upheld if they have a long-standing tradition (Town of Greece v. Galloway).
  • School prayer cases remain strict: government-led prayer in schools is unconstitutional, but private, voluntary prayer by students is protected.

Fisher Trap — Do NOT Automatically Recite Lemon: After Kennedy v. Bremerton (2022), the Lemon test is effectively abandoned. The Court now uses a historical practices and coercion framework. If you write out the three-prong Lemon test as your primary analysis, you are applying dead law. Mention Lemon as historical context, then pivot to the Kennedy/Town of Greece framework: (1) Is there a historical tradition for this practice? (2) Does the government coerce religious participation?

Schemes of Aid to Religious Schools

  • Direct aid for religious instruction → unconstitutional (old Lemon line).
  • Neutral, generally available aid (vouchers, tax credits) → constitutional if parents have genuine private choice (Zelman v. Simmons-Harris; Espinoza v. Montana).
  • Cannot exclude religious schools from generally available public benefits solely because they are religious (Trinity Lutheran; Carson v. Makin).

Fisher Alert: The modern trend is anti-exclusion: the government violates the Free Exercise Clause if it excludes religious entities from generally available secular benefits. This flips the old Establishment Clause concern on its head — now the greater constitutional risk is excluding religion, not including it.

Key Cases Table

Case Holding
Everson v. Board of Ed. (1947) Incorporation; "wall of separation" language used.
School Dist. of Abington v. Schempp (1963) Bible reading / Lord's Prayer in public school unconstitutional.
Lee v. Weisman (1992) Clergy-led prayer at public school graduation unconstitutional (coercion).
Town of Greece v. Galloway (2014) Legislative prayer upheld; historical tradition.
American Legion v. American Humanist Assoc. (2019) Bladensburg Cross (war memorial) upheld; historical significance.
Kennedy v. Bremerton (2022) Coach's post-game midfield prayer protected; Lemon abandoned; analysis based on historical practices.
Carson v. Makin (2022) State cannot exclude religious schools from tuition assistance available to all private schools.

C. Intersection of Speech & Religion

  • Government generally cannot ban blasphemy or "hate speech" based solely on content; such laws are generally content-based and struck under strict scrutiny (Matal v. Tam, Iancu v. Brunetti).
  • Exceptions: True threats, incitement, harassment (as defined by law) remain unprotected.

Fisher Tip — The "Viewpoint Discrimination" Bridge: When government restricts religious speech in a public forum to avoid "establishing" religion, it commits viewpoint discrimination — which is the most egregious First Amendment violation. (Rosenberger v. UVA; Good News Club v. Milford). The Establishment Clause does not justify viewpoint discrimination. This is where the two Religion Clauses and the Speech Clause intersect.

FISHER EXAM STRATEGY FOR FIRST AMENDMENT

  1. Always classify the speech restriction first (content-based vs. neutral). That determines the whole analysis.
  2. For religious claims, run both Free Exercise AND Establishment Clause arguments separately; do not conflate them.
  3. Watch the intersection: When government tries to avoid "establishing" by restricting religious speech — usually fails strict scrutiny (Rosenberger; Good News Club).
  4. Connect to § 5: If RFRA or a state RFRA is at issue, connect to your Boerne analysis — RFRA is the poster child for the "congruence and proportionality" test.
  5. The "Both Sides" Move: Fisher wants expository writing. For speech cases, argue the government's interest seriously before concluding the restriction fails. For religion cases, acknowledge both the Free Exercise and Establishment Clause tensions.
