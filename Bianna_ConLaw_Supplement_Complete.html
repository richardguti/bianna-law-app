CHAPTER 1: THE STRUCTURAL CONSTITUTION — FEDERALISM

I. The Foundation: McCulloch v. Maryland (1819)

McCulloch v. Maryland

17 U.S. (4 Wheat.) 316 (1819)

FACTS: Congress chartered the Second Bank of the United States. Maryland imposed a tax on all banks not chartered by the state — effectively targeting the federal bank. James McCulloch, the cashier of the Baltimore branch, refused to pay. Maryland sued.

ISSUE: 1. Does Congress have the power to incorporate a bank? 2. May a state tax a federal instrumentality?

RULE: - Necessary and Proper Clause (Art. I, § 8, cl. 18): Congress may enact laws that are a "necessary and proper" means to execute its enumerated powers. - Supremacy Clause (Art. VI): Federal law is supreme; states cannot impede valid federal action.

APPLICATION:

Issue 1 — The Bank: Chief Justice Marshall rejected Maryland's argument that "necessary" means "absolutely indispensable." He read it broadly:

  • "Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the Constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consistent with the letter and spirit of the Constitution, are constitutional."
  • Congress has enumerated powers over taxation, borrowing, commerce, and war. A national bank is a convenient, useful, and appropriate means to execute those powers.
  • The Necessary and Proper Clause is listed among Congress's powers, not its limitations.

Issue 2 — The Tax: - "The power to tax involves the power to destroy." If Maryland can tax the bank, it can tax it out of existence — giving one state a veto over federal policy. - The people of Maryland did not elect the federal government; the people of the whole nation did. Maryland cannot use its taxing power to control an entity answerable to a different sovereign.

CONCLUSION: Congress has implied powers beyond those enumerated. The bank is constitutional. Maryland's tax is unconstitutional under the Supremacy Clause.

🏛️ Fisher Trap: The "Necessary" Spectrum

Interpretation Meaning Proponent
Strict/Narrow "Absolutely indispensable" Maryland / Jefferson / Strict Constructionists
Broad/Liberal "Convenient, useful, conducive to" Marshall / Hamilton / Implied Powers

Fisher Alert: On an exam, if a professor asks "Does Congress have the power to do X?", always start with McCulloch. Congress doesn't need an enumerated power for every specific action — it needs an enumerated end and a rational means.

The "Pretext" Limitation

Marshall added a crucial limit: Congress cannot use the Necessary and Proper Clause as a pretext to achieve ends not entrusted to it.

  • The Test: "Should Congress, under the pretext of executing its powers, pass laws for the accomplishment of objects not entrusted to the government, it would become the painful duty of this tribunal... to say that such an act was not the law of the land."
  • Modern Application: This "pretext" limit was revived in NFIB v. Sebelius (2012) when the Court rejected the individual mandate as a valid exercise of the Commerce + N&P power.

II. Modern Necessary & Proper: United States v. Comstock (2010)

United States v. Comstock

560 U.S. 126 (2010)

FACTS: A federal statute (18 U.S.C. § 4248) allowed the government to civilly commit "sexually dangerous" federal prisoners beyond the end of their prison sentence. Graydon Comstock, a federal prisoner whose sentence was about to expire, challenged the law, arguing Congress had no enumerated power to civilly commit people.

ISSUE: Does the Necessary and Proper Clause give Congress the authority to enact a civil commitment statute for sexually dangerous federal prisoners?

RULE: The Necessary and Proper Clause grants Congress broad authority to enact laws that are "rationally related" to the implementation of a constitutionally enumerated power, provided the law does not violate an independent constitutional prohibition.

APPLICATION:

Justice Breyer, writing for the 7-2 majority, identified five considerations:

  1. Breadth of the N&P Clause: McCulloch established that "necessary" does not mean "indispensable." Congress has wide latitude.
  2. Long History: The federal government has been responsible for the mental health of federal prisoners for over a century (since 1855).
  3. Sound Reasoning: Congress was acting as a "custodian" — it put these people in prison, it knows they are dangerous, and releasing them without treatment would endanger the public.
  4. Accommodation of State Interests: The statute requires the government to notify the state where the prisoner is domiciled and give the state the first shot at commitment.
  5. Narrow Scope: The statute is limited to federal prisoners already in federal custody.

CONCLUSION: The statute is constitutional. It is a "necessary and proper" means to effectuate the federal criminal justice system.

The Dissents

  • Justice Thomas (joined by Scalia): Argued the majority conflated "convenient" with "necessary." He demanded a tighter link: Congress must show the law is "carrying into execution" a specific enumerated power, not just a general "custodial" responsibility.
  • Justice Kennedy (Concurrence): Agreed with the result but wanted stricter scrutiny — the law must not be a "substantial expansion" of federal authority.
  • Justice Alito (Concurrence): Focused on the "accommodation" prong — the law was acceptable because it deferred to states.

III. Term Limits & the Structural Constitution

U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton (1995)

514 U.S. 779 (1995)

FACTS: Arkansas amended its state constitution to prohibit anyone who had already served three terms in the U.S. House or two terms in the U.S. Senate from appearing on the ballot for re-election.

ISSUE: May a state impose term limits (or additional qualifications) on its members of Congress beyond those listed in the Constitution?

RULE: The Qualifications Clauses of Articles I, §§ 2 and 3 set forth the exclusive qualifications for membership in Congress: Age, Citizenship, and Residency. States may not add to, subtract from, or alter these qualifications.

APPLICATION:

Justice Stevens, writing for the 5-4 majority:

  • Textual Argument: The Constitution lists three qualifications. If the Framers intended states to add more, they would have said so (expressio unius).
  • Historical Argument: At the time of the Framing, the dominant view was that the people should choose their representatives freely, without state-imposed barriers.
  • Structural Argument — "The People of the Nation": Members of Congress represent the national interest, not just their state. Allowing states to add qualifications would create a "patchwork" of different eligibility rules, undermining national unity.
  • The Powell Precedent: In Powell v. McCormack (1969), the Court held that even Congress itself could not add qualifications beyond those listed. If Congress can't do it, surely a state can't.

CONCLUSION: The Arkansas amendment is unconstitutional. Only a constitutional amendment (like the 22nd Amendment for the President) can impose term limits on Congress.

Justice Thomas's Dissent (Critical for Fisher)

Thomas argued for a "Reserved Powers" view: - 10th Amendment: Powers not delegated to the federal government are reserved to the states or the people. - Original Understanding: The states existed before the Constitution and retained the power to set qualifications for their own representatives unless the Constitution explicitly prohibited it. - "The People of the States": Thomas rejected the "national constituency" idea. He argued that each state's people elect their own representatives, so each state should have the power to set qualifications.

🏛️ Fisher Alert: The "Source of Power" Debate

View Who Holds Sovereignty? Proponent
National Sovereignty "We the People" of the whole nation Stevens (Majority)
State Sovereignty "We the People" of each individual state Thomas (Dissent)

Exam Tip: If you see a question about state power vs. federal structure, Thornton is your cite for the principle that the federal Constitution sets the floor AND the ceiling for federal qualifications. States cannot freelance.

CHAPTER 2: THE COMMERCE POWER — FULL CASE BRIEFS

I. The Modern Commerce Power Framework

After Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) established Congress's broad power over interstate commerce, the modern era recognizes three categories of regulable activity under the Commerce Clause:

  1. Channels of interstate commerce (highways, waterways, internet)
  2. Instrumentalities of interstate commerce (trucks, trains, planes, people/things moving in commerce)
  3. Activities with a substantial effect on interstate commerce

Fisher's Golden Rule: Categories 1 and 2 are "easy wins" for Congress. Category 3 is where all the fights happen.

II. The Civil Rights Era Commerce Cases

Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States (1964)

379 U.S. 241 (1964)

FACTS: The Heart of Atlanta Motel, located near interstate highways in downtown Atlanta, had 216 rooms and refused to rent to Black guests. It solicited patronage from outside Georgia through national advertising and approximately 75% of its guests were from out of state. The motel challenged Title II of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which prohibited racial discrimination in "public accommodations" that affected interstate commerce.

ISSUE: Does Congress have the power under the Commerce Clause to prohibit racial discrimination in a private motel that serves interstate travelers?

RULE: Congress may regulate local activities that have a "substantial and harmful effect" on interstate commerce, even if the specific business is "local" in nature.

APPLICATION:

Justice Clark, writing for a unanimous Court:

  • The Record: Congress compiled an extensive record showing that discrimination in hotels and motels deterred Black citizens from traveling interstate, which suppressed commerce.
  • Rational Basis: The Court applied a highly deferential standard: if Congress has a rational basis for finding that a regulated activity affects interstate commerce, the Court will not substitute its judgment.
  • The "Moral vs. Commercial" Debate: The motel argued this was really a "moral" regulation, not a "commercial" one. The Court responded that Congress's motive is irrelevant — as long as the means (regulating commerce) are within Congress's power, the ends (eliminating discrimination) are permissible.

CONCLUSION: Title II is a valid exercise of the Commerce Power. The motel must serve guests of all races.

Katzenbach v. McClung (1964)

379 U.S. 294 (1964)

FACTS: Ollie's Barbecue was a family-owned restaurant in Birmingham, Alabama. It was located 11 blocks from an interstate highway and had no out-of-state customers. However, approximately 46% of its food was purchased from a supplier who procured it from out of state. Ollie's refused to serve Black customers in its dining room.

ISSUE: Can Congress regulate a purely local restaurant under the Commerce Clause simply because it purchases food that has moved in interstate commerce?

RULE: Same as Heart of Atlanta — Congress may regulate local activities if it has a rational basis for concluding they substantially affect interstate commerce.

APPLICATION:

  • The "Aggregate" Theory: Even though Ollie's was one small restaurant, the Court looked at the aggregate effect of all restaurants that discriminate. Together, they suppress demand for food shipped interstate, deter interstate travel, and reduce the tax base.
  • The Supply-Side Link: Ollie's purchased meat from a supplier who got it from out of state. This "supply chain" link was enough to establish a connection to interstate commerce.

CONCLUSION: Ollie's Barbecue must comply with Title II. Congress has the rational basis to conclude that discrimination in restaurants collectively affects interstate commerce.

🏛️ Fisher Tip: The Commerce Clause "Side-Door"

Remember the Civil Rights Cases (1883)? The Court said Congress could not use the 14th Amendment to regulate private discrimination. So how did Congress finally do it?

  • The Side-Door: Congress used the Commerce Clause (Article I, § 8) instead of the 14th Amendment (§ 5).
  • The Result: The same goal (ending private discrimination) was achieved through a different constitutional power.
  • The Lesson: If one power source fails, look for another. This is classic "Fisher-level" structural analysis.

III. The Rehnquist Revolution: Limiting the Commerce Power

United States v. Lopez (1995)

514 U.S. 549 (1995)

FACTS: Alfonso Lopez, a 12th-grade student in San Antonio, Texas, carried a concealed .38 caliber handgun to school. He was charged under the federal Gun-Free School Zones Act of 1990 (GFSZA), which made it a federal crime to possess a firearm within 1,000 feet of a school.

ISSUE: Does the Gun-Free School Zones Act exceed Congress's power under the Commerce Clause?

RULE: Under the Commerce Clause, Congress may regulate: 1. The channels of interstate commerce; 2. The instrumentalities of, or persons/things in, interstate commerce; and 3. Activities that substantially affect interstate commerce.

For Category 3, the activity must have a substantial effect on interstate commerce, not merely an attenuated or theoretical one.

APPLICATION:

Chief Justice Rehnquist, writing for the 5-4 majority:

  • Not Commercial Activity: Gun possession in a school zone is not an "economic" or "commercial" activity. It has nothing to do with buying, selling, or producing goods.
  • No Jurisdictional Element: The statute contained no requirement that the firearm had "moved in interstate commerce."
  • No Congressional Findings: Congress made no formal findings explaining the link between guns near schools and interstate commerce.
  • The "Infinity" Problem: The government argued: Guns → Violence → Bad Education → Less Productive Workers → Lower Commerce. Rehnquist rejected this "costs of crime" reasoning because under that logic, Congress could regulate anything (marriage, divorce, child custody) — obliterating the line between federal and state power.

CONCLUSION: The GFSZA is unconstitutional. It exceeded Congress's Commerce Clause power because gun possession near schools is not economic activity and has no substantial effect on interstate commerce.

The Critical Concurrences & Dissents

Justice Kennedy (Concurrence): Emphasized that the case was about federalism — preserving the states' traditional role in education and criminal law. He warned that if the federal government can regulate local school safety, there is nothing left for states to do.

Justice Thomas (Concurrence): Went further. He argued the "substantial effects" test itself should be abandoned because it has no limiting principle. He would return to the original meaning: Congress can only regulate the actual movement of goods across state lines.

Justice Breyer (Dissent): Argued the majority was ignoring "empirical reality." He cited studies showing that gun violence costs the economy billions and that education quality directly impacts the national workforce. He would use a much more deferential rational basis test.

United States v. Morrison (2000) — Commerce Clause Prong

529 U.S. 598 (2000)

Note: Your V2 covers Morrison's § 5 analysis in detail (lines 4070-4097). This supplement covers the Commerce Clause prong that V2 does not brief.

FACTS: Same as V2 — Christy Brzonkala sued under VAWA. Congress also relied on the Commerce Clause as a power source, arguing gender-motivated violence costs the economy $5-10 billion annually.

ISSUE (Commerce Prong): Does gender-motivated violence "substantially affect" interstate commerce?

APPLICATION:

Chief Justice Rehnquist applied the Lopez framework:

  • Not Economic Activity: Gender-motivated crimes of violence are not "economic activity" in any meaningful sense. They are violent crimes — traditionally the domain of state criminal law.
  • The "Attenuated" Chain: The government's argument (violence → medical costs → lost productivity → less commerce) was the same "costs of crime" reasoning rejected in Lopez.
  • The "Aggregate" Limit: Rehnquist held that the aggregation principle (Wickard) only applies to economic activity. You cannot aggregate non-economic conduct.
  • Congressional Findings Don't Save It: Even though Congress compiled a massive record of gender violence's economic impact, the Court held that "simply because Congress may conclude that a particular activity substantially affects interstate commerce does not necessarily make it so."

CONCLUSION: VAWA's civil remedy is also unconstitutional under the Commerce Clause.

Gonzales v. Raich (2005)

545 U.S. 1 (2005)

FACTS: Angel Raich and Diane Monson grew marijuana at home for personal medical use, as permitted under California's Compassionate Use Act. Federal agents seized and destroyed Monson's plants under the Controlled Substances Act (CSA). The plaintiffs argued that purely local, non-commercial cultivation of marijuana for personal medical use was beyond Congress's Commerce Clause power (citing Lopez).

ISSUE: Can Congress regulate the purely local cultivation and possession of marijuana for personal medical use under the Commerce Clause?

RULE: Congress may regulate purely local activity if it is part of a broader "comprehensive regulatory scheme" and failure to regulate the local activity would undercut the larger scheme.

APPLICATION:

Justice Stevens, writing for the 6-3 majority:

  • The Wickard Principle: Just as Filburn's homegrown wheat affected the national wheat market, homegrown marijuana affects the national drug market. If people grow their own, they won't buy from the interstate (illegal) market, undercutting Congress's ability to regulate that market.
  • Economic Activity: Unlike the guns in Lopez or the violence in Morrison, marijuana cultivation is economic in nature — it involves the production of a commodity (even for personal consumption).
  • The "Comprehensive Scheme" Rationale: The CSA is a comprehensive scheme to regulate all controlled substances. If California can carve out an exception, other states will too, creating a "Swiss cheese" effect that destroys the federal regulatory structure.
  • Rational Basis: Congress need only have a rational basis for believing that homegrown marijuana, in the aggregate, substantially affects the interstate drug market.

CONCLUSION: The CSA is a valid exercise of Commerce Power as applied to local medical marijuana. Lopez and Morrison are distinguished because those cases involved non-economic activity.

🏛️ Fisher Trap: The Lopez/Raich Distinction

Factor Lopez (Invalid) Raich (Valid)
Nature of Activity Gun possession (NOT economic) Marijuana cultivation (IS economic — production of a commodity)
Regulatory Scheme Stand-alone statute Part of comprehensive CSA
Aggregation Cannot aggregate non-economic conduct CAN aggregate economic conduct
State Role Education = traditional state function Drug enforcement = shared federal/state

Exam Tip: If the activity is economic (production, distribution, consumption of a commodity), Congress can aggregate under Raich and will almost always win. If it is non-economic (violence, gun possession, family law), Congress loses under Lopez/Morrison.

IV. The ACA and the "Outer Limit": NFIB v. Sebelius (2012)

National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius

567 U.S. 519 (2012)

FACTS: The Affordable Care Act (ACA) included an "individual mandate" requiring most Americans to purchase health insurance or pay a "shared responsibility payment." Congress argued this was valid under the Commerce Clause (regulating the massive health insurance market), the Necessary and Proper Clause, and the Taxing Power.

ISSUE: Does the Commerce Clause or the Necessary and Proper Clause authorize Congress to compel individuals to purchase health insurance?

APPLICATION (Commerce Clause — REJECTED 5-4):

Chief Justice Roberts:

  • Activity vs. Inactivity: The Commerce Clause regulates existing activity. It does not compel people to enter commerce. Not buying insurance is inactivity — the absence of commerce.
  • "Everyone is in the market": The government argued everyone will eventually need health care. Roberts responded: "The Commerce Clause is not a general license to regulate an individual from cradle to grave."
  • The "Broccoli" Hypothetical: If Congress can force you to buy insurance because it's good for you, can they force you to buy broccoli? The limiting principle disappears.

APPLICATION (Necessary & Proper — REJECTED):

  • Roberts invoked McCulloch's "pretext" limitation. If the underlying Commerce power doesn't authorize the mandate, the N&P Clause cannot save it. The N&P Clause is a supplement, not an independent font of power.
  • A law that "creates the necessary predicate to the exercise of an enumerated power" is not a proper exercise of that power.

APPLICATION (Taxing Power — UPHELD):

  • The "shared responsibility payment" functions like a tax: it is collected by the IRS, produces revenue, and applies to taxpayers who file returns.
  • Under the Taxing Power, Congress need not rely on the Commerce Clause. A tax is valid if it is not so punitive as to be a "penalty."

CONCLUSION: The individual mandate cannot be sustained under the Commerce Clause or the Necessary and Proper Clause. However, it is valid as a tax under the Taxing Power.

The Medicaid Expansion (7-2 Rejection)

The ACA also expanded Medicaid, threatening to withdraw all existing Medicaid funding from states that refused to expand coverage.

  • The "Gun to the Head": Roberts held this was unconstitutional coercion — not a mere "inducement" like South Dakota v. Dole. Threatening to withdraw an existing, massive program (10% of state budgets) left states with "no real choice."
  • The Dole Line: Congress can attach conditions to new funding. It cannot threaten to take away existing funding as punishment for not accepting new conditions.

🏛️ Fisher Flowchart: Taxing & Spending Power

TAXING POWER:
  Is the "penalty" collected by the IRS? → YES
  Does it produce revenue? → YES
  Is it imposed on taxpayers filing returns? → YES
  Is it so punitive it functions as a criminal sanction? → NO
  → VALID TAX (NFIB)

SPENDING POWER (South Dakota v. Dole):
  (1) Is spending for the "general welfare"? → YES
  (2) Are conditions stated "unambiguously"? → YES
  (3) Are conditions related to the federal interest? → YES
  (4) Is the spending independently constitutional? → YES
  (5) Is the financial pressure so great it's COERCION? → IF YES → INVALID (NFIB)

CHAPTER 3: THE DORMANT COMMERCE CLAUSE (DCC)

I. The Concept

The Commerce Clause is not just a grant of power to Congress — it is also an implicit limitation on the states. Even when Congress has not acted, states cannot pass laws that unduly burden or discriminate against interstate commerce.

Fisher's One-Liner: "The Commerce Clause speaks not only to what Congress can do, but to what the States cannot do even in Congress's silence."

II. The Two-Track Framework

Track 1: Facial Discrimination Against Interstate Commerce → Per Se Invalid

If a state law facially discriminates against out-of-state commerce (treating in-state goods/services better than out-of-state), it is virtually per se invalid.

The state must prove: 1. A legitimate local purpose exists; 2. No nondiscriminatory alternative is available.

This is essentially strict scrutiny — and the state almost never wins.

Track 2: Evenhandedly Burdensome Laws → Pike Balancing

If a state law is facially neutral (applies equally to in-state and out-of-state) but still burdens interstate commerce, apply the Pike balancing test:

"Where the statute regulates even-handedly to effectuate a legitimate local public interest, and its effects on interstate commerce are only incidental, it will be upheld unless the burden imposed on such commerce is clearly excessive in relation to the putative local benefits." — Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc. (1970)

III. Key DCC Cases

Philadelphia v. New Jersey (1978)

437 U.S. 617 (1978)

FACTS: New Jersey passed a law prohibiting the importation of most solid or liquid waste from other states into New Jersey landfills. New Jersey claimed this was an environmental/health measure.

ISSUE: Does a state law banning the importation of out-of-state waste violate the Dormant Commerce Clause?

RULE: A state cannot isolate itself from a national problem by discriminating against out-of-state commerce. Waste is an "article of commerce," even if unwanted.

APPLICATION:

Justice Stewart, writing for the 7-2 majority: - Facial Discrimination: The law explicitly treated waste differently based on its origin (in-state vs. out-of-state). This is textbook discrimination. - Protectionism: The "evil" of protectionism lies in "differential treatment of in-state and out-of-state economic interests that benefits the former and burdens the latter." - Health Pretext: New Jersey claimed health concerns. The Court responded: waste from NJ is just as dangerous as waste from NY. If the concern is volume, New Jersey can cap the total waste going into landfills — but it cannot single out out-of-state waste.

CONCLUSION: The law is unconstitutional. It facially discriminates against interstate commerce with no legitimate, non-discriminatory justification.

Dean Milk Co. v. City of Madison (1951)

340 U.S. 349 (1951)

FACTS: Madison, Wisconsin required all milk sold in the city to be pasteurized within 5 miles of the city center. This effectively excluded all out-of-state milk producers.

HOLDING: Invalid. The city had reasonable nondiscriminatory alternatives — it could have required inspection of out-of-state pasteurization plants or accepted certifications from other states' inspectors.

Fisher Tip: Dean Milk establishes the "least restrictive means" requirement for discriminatory DCC laws. Always ask: "Could the state achieve its health/safety goal without discriminating against out-of-state commerce?"

Pike v. Bruce Church, Inc. (1970)

397 U.S. 137 (1970)

FACTS: Arizona required that all cantaloupes grown in Arizona be packed in Arizona before shipment to other states. Bruce Church, Inc. grew cantaloupes in Arizona but packed them at a nearby California facility (just across the border).

RULE: The Pike Balancing Test (see above). Even a facially neutral law fails if the burden on interstate commerce clearly outweighs the local benefit.

APPLICATION: The Court found that Arizona's real purpose was to protect its reputation by having Arizona cantaloupes packed under the Arizona label. This economic protectionism was not a sufficient local benefit to justify the burden of forcing a company to build an entirely new packing facility in Arizona.

CONCLUSION: The law is unconstitutional.

  → YES → Apply STRICT SCRUTINY (Per Se Invalid)
    → Does the state have a LEGITIMATE LOCAL PURPOSE?
      → YES → Is there a NONDISCRIMINATORY ALTERNATIVE?

        → NO → Law *might* survive (very rare)
      → NO → LAW FAILS (Philadelphia v. NJ)

  → Apply PIKE BALANCING:
    → Does the BURDEN on interstate commerce CLEARLY EXCEED

      → YES → LAW FAILS (Pike)
      → NO → LAW SURVIVES

IV. The Market Participant Exception

The Rule: When a state acts as a market participant (buyer or seller) rather than a market regulator, the DCC does not apply.

  • Hughes v. Alexandria Scrap Corp. (1976): Maryland offered bounties for scrapping old cars but made it easier for in-state processors to qualify. Valid — Maryland was participating in the scrap market, not regulating it.
  • Reeves, Inc. v. Stake (1980): South Dakota owned a cement plant and gave preference to in-state buyers during shortages. Valid — the state was a seller.
  • South-Central Timber Dev. v. Wunnicke (1984): Alaska required that timber from state lands be processed in Alaska before export. Invalid — this was a "downstream" restriction that went beyond the state's role as a seller.

Fisher Trap: The Market Participant Exception has a limit — the state can only impose conditions on the immediate transaction, not on downstream commerce.

V. Privileges & Immunities Clause (Article IV, § 2)

The Text: "The Citizens of each State shall be entitled to all Privileges and Immunities of Citizens in the several States."

The Function: Prevents states from discriminating against out-of-state citizens (not corporations) regarding "fundamental" rights (employment, practice of profession, access to courts).

The Two-Part Test (United Bldg. Trades v. Camden, 1984):

  1. Does the law burden a fundamental privilege or immunity?
  2. If yes, is there a substantial reason for the discrimination, and is the discrimination closely related to that reason?

Key Distinctions from DCC:

Feature Dormant Commerce Clause Privileges & Immunities
Protects Interstate commerce (goods & services) Out-of-state citizens (natural persons)
Corporations? Yes No (only natural persons)
Market Participant Exception? Yes No
Congressional Override? Yes (Congress can authorize discrimination) No

Fisher Exam Strategy: If a state discriminates against out-of-state people (not just goods), argue both the DCC and Art. IV P&I. P&I is especially useful when the Market Participant Exception would block the DCC claim.

CHAPTER 4: PREEMPTION

I. The Supremacy Clause Framework

Art. VI, cl. 2: Federal law is the "supreme Law of the Land." When federal and state law conflict, federal law wins.

Three Types of Preemption:

Type Definition Example
Express Federal statute explicitly says "states may not regulate in this area" ERISA preempts state regulation of employee benefit plans
Field Federal regulation is so pervasive it "occupies the field," leaving no room for states Immigration (Arizona v. US)
Conflict State law directly conflicts with federal law (compliance with both is impossible, OR state law stands as an obstacle to federal objectives) State law requiring more than federal minimum

Arizona v. United States (2012)

567 U.S. 387 (2012)

FACTS: Arizona passed SB 1070 in response to perceived federal inaction on immigration. Key provisions: - Made it a state misdemeanor to fail to carry alien registration documents (§ 3); - Made it a state crime for unauthorized aliens to seek employment (§ 5(C)); - Authorized warrantless arrest of anyone an officer had probable cause to believe had committed a deportable offense (§ 6); - Required officers to verify immigration status during lawful stops (§ 2(B) — the "show me your papers" provision).

ISSUE: Are these provisions preempted by federal immigration law?

APPLICATION:

Justice Kennedy, writing for the 5-3 majority:

  • § 3 (Registration): Preempted by field preemption. Congress created a comprehensive federal alien registration scheme. Arizona cannot create a parallel state registration crime.
  • § 5(C) (Employment): Preempted by conflict preemption. Congress specifically chose to punish employers who hire unauthorized workers (IRCA), not the workers themselves. Arizona's law stands as an "obstacle" to that congressional choice.
  • § 6 (Warrantless Arrest): Preempted. It gave state officers authority that even federal officers didn't have and could lead to the prolonged detention of lawful aliens.
  • § 2(B) (Status Check): Not preempted (at this stage). The Court found it was not facially invalid because it merely required officers to check status during otherwise lawful stops. However, the Court warned it could be challenged "as applied" if it led to racial profiling or prolonged detention.

CONCLUSION: Three of four provisions are preempted. Federal immigration law occupies the field.

🏛️ Fisher Preemption Checklist

When you see a state law touching a federal area:

1. EXPRESS PREEMPTION: Does the federal statute say "states cannot..."?
   → If YES → State law is preempted. Done.

2. FIELD PREEMPTION: Is federal regulation so PERVASIVE that it
   "occupies the field"?
   → Look for: comprehensive federal scheme, federal agency involvement,
     dominant federal interest (immigration, foreign affairs, nuclear energy)
   → If YES → State law is preempted.

3. CONFLICT PREEMPTION:
   (a) IMPOSSIBILITY: Can someone comply with BOTH laws simultaneously?
       → If NO → State law is preempted.
   (b) OBSTACLE: Does the state law stand as an OBSTACLE to the full
       purposes and objectives of Congress?
       → If YES → State law is preempted.

4. PRESUMPTION AGAINST PREEMPTION: In areas of TRADITIONAL STATE
   REGULATION (health, safety, family law), courts presume Congress
   did NOT intend to preempt unless clearly stated.

CHAPTER 5: SEPARATION OF POWERS — THE EXECUTIVE

I. Executive Power: The Youngstown Framework

Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952)

343 U.S. 579 (1952) — "The Steel Seizure Case"

FACTS: During the Korean War, steelworkers threatened to strike. President Truman, fearing a steel shortage would jeopardize the war effort, issued an executive order directing the Secretary of Commerce to seize and operate the nation's steel mills. Truman did not invoke the Taft-Hartley Act (which Congress had passed and which gave the President the power to seek an injunction — a tool Truman deliberately rejected). The steel companies sued.

ISSUE: Does the President have inherent constitutional authority to seize private property during a national emergency without congressional authorization?

RULE (Justice Black — Majority): The President's power to issue orders must stem from an act of Congress or the Constitution itself. There is no "inherent" executive power to seize private property. The President is a law-executor, not a law-maker.

RULE (Justice Jackson — Concurrence — THE GOLD STANDARD):

Jackson's famous Three-Zone Framework:

Zone Scenario Presidential Power
Zone 1 President acts with express or implied congressional authorization Power at its MAXIMUM. President has his own power + Congress's delegated power. Presumptively valid.
Zone 2 President acts in the absence of congressional grant or denial ("Zone of Twilight") Power is UNCERTAIN. Depends on the "imperatives of events." Congressional inertia may enable executive action.
Zone 3 President acts contrary to the express or implied will of Congress Power at its LOWEST EBB. President can only rely on his own constitutional powers minus any Congress has over the matter. Court will scrutinize carefully.

APPLICATION:

  • Truman's seizure was in Zone 3 — Congress had considered and rejected giving the President seizure power when it passed the Taft-Hartley Act. By choosing a method Congress had specifically denied, Truman was acting against the expressed will of Congress.
  • In Zone 3, the President can only prevail if Congress lacks any power over the subject. Here, Congress clearly had power over property seizure and labor disputes.

CONCLUSION: The seizure is unconstitutional. The President exceeded his authority.

🏛️ Fisher Alert: The Youngstown Checklist

On any Separation of Powers question involving executive action:

  1. Identify the Zone. Did Congress authorize, stay silent, or prohibit the action?
  2. Zone 1: Almost always valid. The government will win.
  3. Zone 2: Gray area. Analyze the "imperatives of events" and historical practice.
  4. Zone 3: Almost always invalid. The President is fighting uphill.

II. Appointment & Removal Powers

The Appointments Clause (Art. II, § 2, cl. 2)

  • Principal Officers: Must be appointed by the President with Senate confirmation.
  • Inferior Officers: Congress may vest appointment in the President alone, the Courts, or Heads of Departments.

Key Case — Morrison v. Olson (1988): - Upheld the Independent Counsel Act. The independent counsel was an "inferior officer" because (1) she could be removed by the AG, (2) her duties were limited, and (3) her jurisdiction was narrow. - Scalia's Lone Dissent: Argued that all executive prosecution power belongs to the President. An independent counsel who can't be fired at will violates Article II. This dissent has become increasingly influential.

The Removal Power

Type Who Can Remove? Standard Key Case
Pure Executive Officers President alone At will Myers v. United States (1926)
Officers with Quasi-Judicial/Legislative Functions Congress can limit removal to "good cause" "For cause" protection Humphrey's Executor v. United States (1935)
Inferior Officers Congress can limit removal Reasonable restrictions OK Morrison v. Olson (1988)
Double Layer of Protection ❌ UNCONSTITUTIONAL Congress cannot insulate officers behind two layers of for-cause protection Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB (2010)

Fisher Trap: If a question asks about a federal agency head who "can only be removed for good cause," check: Is this a single layer of protection (valid per Humphrey's) or a double layer (invalid per Free Enterprise Fund)?

III. Executive Privilege & Immunity

United States v. Nixon (1974)

418 U.S. 683 (1974)

  • Holding: The President has a qualified executive privilege to protect confidential communications with advisors. However, this privilege is not absolute — it must yield to the demonstrated needs of the criminal justice system (here, the Watergate special prosecutor's subpoena for tape recordings).
  • The Balance: The need for evidence in a criminal trial outweighs the generalized claim of confidentiality.

Trump v. United States (2024)

600 U.S. __ (2024)

  • Holding: A former President has absolute immunity from criminal prosecution for acts within the "conclusive and preclusive" core constitutional powers of the presidency (e.g., pardons, commanding the military).
  • Presumptive Immunity: For all other "official acts," there is a presumption of immunity that the prosecution must overcome.
  • No Immunity: For unofficial/private acts, there is no immunity.

Fisher Alert: Trump v. United States is the most significant Separation of Powers decision since Youngstown. The key exam issue is drawing the line between "official" and "unofficial" presidential conduct.

IV. The Non-Delegation Doctrine

The Principle: Congress cannot delegate its legislative power to the Executive Branch. Any delegation must include an "intelligible principle" to guide the executive's discretion.

The Test (J.W. Hampton Jr. & Co. v. United States, 1928): A delegation is valid if Congress provides an "intelligible principle" to which the executive must conform.

The Two Historical Failures: - Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan (1935): Struck down a provision giving the President unlimited power to ban oil shipments. No standards. - A.L.A. Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States (1935): Struck down the NIRA's delegation of power to set "fair competition" codes. No limits on what the codes could contain.

Modern Reality: Since 1935, the Court has never struck down a delegation. Standards like "public interest," "just and reasonable," and "necessary and appropriate" have all been upheld.

Fisher Prediction: Justice Gorsuch has signaled interest in reviving the non-delegation doctrine. Watch for cases involving broad EPA or agency authority.

CHAPTER 6: PROCEDURAL DUE PROCESS

I. The Two-Step Framework

Step 1: Is There a Protected Interest?

The Due Process Clause protects life, liberty, and property. But "property" doesn't just mean land — it includes government entitlements.

The "New Property" Doctrine (Goldberg v. Kelly, 1970): - Welfare benefits are a property interest if the recipient has a legitimate claim of entitlement under existing rules. - The government cannot terminate them without Due Process.

Board of Regents v. Roth (1972): - A non-tenured professor had no property interest in being rehired because his contract gave him no entitlement to renewal. Property interests must be created by an independent source of law (statute, contract, regulation).

Perry v. Sindermann (1972): - Even without a formal contract, an implied promise of continued employment (like an unwritten tenure system) can create a property interest.

Step 2: What Process is Due?

Mathews v. Eldridge (1976)

424 U.S. 319 (1976)

FACTS: George Eldridge's Social Security disability benefits were terminated after an internal review. He was not given an evidentiary hearing before termination — only after. He sued, arguing Goldberg v. Kelly required a pre-termination hearing.

ISSUE: Does the Due Process Clause require an evidentiary hearing before (rather than after) the termination of Social Security disability benefits?

RULE — The Mathews Balancing Test:

The Court weighs three factors:

  1. The Private Interest: How important is the benefit to the individual? How severe is the deprivation?
  2. The Risk of Error: How likely is the current procedure to produce a wrong result? Would additional safeguards reduce that risk?
  3. The Government Interest: How much would additional procedures cost? What is the administrative burden?

APPLICATION:

Justice Powell distinguished Goldberg: - Welfare (Goldberg): Recipients are typically on the "brutal edge of subsistence." A wrongful termination could mean starvation. Pre-termination hearing required. - Disability (Eldridge): Recipients can turn to other sources (welfare, savings). The determination is based on medical records (objective), not credibility (subjective). A pre-termination hearing would add enormous cost for marginal accuracy improvement.

CONCLUSION: No pre-termination hearing required. The existing procedures (written questionnaire + post-termination hearing) provide adequate due process.

🏛️ Fisher Flowchart: Procedural Due Process

STEP 1: Is there a PROTECTED INTEREST?
  → LIFE: Always protected.
  → LIBERTY: Includes physical freedom, reputation (if combined with
     loss of a tangible interest — "stigma plus"), parental rights.
  → PROPERTY: Must have a "legitimate claim of entitlement" created
     by statute, contract, or regulation (Roth).
  → If NO protected interest → NO DUE PROCESS REQUIRED. Stop.

STEP 2: What PROCESS is DUE?
  → Apply MATHEWS v. ELDRIDGE Balancing:
    (1) PRIVATE INTEREST: How important? How severe?
    (2) RISK OF ERROR: Is the current process reliable?
        Would more process help?
    (3) GOVERNMENT INTEREST: Cost and administrative burden.

  → RESULT: The more important the private interest AND the higher
    the risk of error AND the lower the government's cost,
    the MORE process is due (approaching Goldberg's
    full pre-deprivation hearing).

The "Stigma-Plus" Doctrine

A government statement that damages your reputation alone is NOT a deprivation of liberty. But if the stigma is combined with a tangible loss (loss of employment, inability to get another job), then Due Process applies.

  • Paul v. Davis (1976): Police posted a "shoplifters" flyer with Davis's photo. No tangible loss beyond stigma → No Due Process claim.
  • Wisconsin v. Constantineau (1971): Being publicly posted as a "drunkard" and banned from buying alcohol → Stigma PLUS loss of a legal right → Due Process applies.

Fisher Trap: Students often think any government attack on reputation triggers Due Process. It doesn't. You need "stigma plus" a concrete deprivation.

Case Interest Process Required?

Roth (1972) Non-tenured job (no entitlement) NO — No property interest

CHAPTER 7: PRIVILEGES & IMMUNITIES (14th AMENDMENT) & THE RIGHT TO TRAVEL

I. The Slaughter-House Cases (1873)

The Holding: The 14th Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause protects only a narrow set of federal citizenship rights (access to federal offices, protection on the high seas, right to travel to Washington D.C.). It does NOT incorporate the Bill of Rights or protect general civil liberties.

The Effect: This decision effectively gutted the Privileges or Immunities Clause for over 100 years. That's why the Court later used the Due Process Clause (not Privileges or Immunities) to incorporate the Bill of Rights.

Fisher Note: Justice Thomas has repeatedly argued that Slaughter-House was wrong and that the Privileges or Immunities Clause should be revived as the proper vehicle for incorporation (McDonald v. Chicago concurrence).

II. Saenz v. Roe (1999)

526 U.S. 489 (1999)

FACTS: California limited new residents' welfare benefits to the amount they would have received in their prior state of residence for their first year. A family moving from a state with low benefits to California would get less than longtime California residents.

ISSUE: Does this violate the right to travel?

RULE: The right to travel has three components: 1. The right to enter and leave another state (Art. IV); 2. The right to be treated as a welcome visitor (Art. IV P&I); 3. The right of new citizens to be treated the same as established citizens (14th Amendment Privileges or Immunities Clause).

HOLDING: California's law violates component 3. Once you become a citizen of a state, you are entitled to the same benefits as every other citizen. A state cannot create a two-tier system based on how long you've lived there.

Fisher Alert: Saenz is the only modern case to successfully rely on the 14th Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause. It remains an extremely narrow doctrine.

"Impact = Intent" "The law hurts minorities, so it's unconstitutional" Impact alone is NOT enough. Must prove discriminatory purpose (Washington v. Davis)
"The 14th Amend. applies to everyone" "The private company violated my equal protection" 14th Amend. only applies to STATE actors (Civil Rights Cases)
"Congress can do anything under Commerce" "This affects the economy, so Congress can regulate" Activity must be economic in nature. Non-economic = Lopez/Morrison fail
"Rational Basis always saves the law" "Any hypothetical reason works" Not if the law is based on animus (Moreno, Romer, Cleburne, Windsor)
"Separate can be equal" "They gave women their own program" Separate is almost NEVER equal when intangibles differ (Sweatt, VMI)
"History and tradition = unchangeable" "It wasn't a right in 1868, so it can't be one now" Obergefell said tradition "guides but does not set outer boundaries" — but Dobbs reinstalled Glucksberg. Argue BOTH sides.
"Congressional findings save everything" "Congress held hearings, so the law is valid" Findings don't matter if the target is wrong (Morrison: targeted private actors) or the fit is wrong (Boerne: sledgehammer)

SECTION E: THE FIVE MOST TESTABLE TENSIONS

For your exam essay, these are the five doctrinal tensions Fisher will test. You MUST argue both sides.

1. Formalism vs. Functionalism (Separation of Powers)

  • Formalist: The Constitution draws bright lines. If it says "legislative power" in Art. I, only Congress can make laws. Period. (Black in Youngstown)
  • Functionalist: The Constitution creates a flexible system. As long as one branch doesn't aggrandize power or encroach too far, overlapping authority is fine. (Jackson in Youngstown)

2. Color-Blind vs. Anti-Subordination (Equal Protection)

  • Color-Blind: The Constitution forbids ALL racial classifications, even "benign" ones. (Roberts: "The way to stop discriminating on the basis of race is to stop discriminating on the basis of race.")
  • Anti-Subordination: The 14th Amendment targets the caste system. It was written to help Black Americans, so helping them isn't the same as oppressing them. (Marshall in Bakke)

3. Glucksberg vs. Obergefell (Substantive Due Process)

  • Glucksberg (Narrow): Only rights "deeply rooted in history and tradition" qualify as fundamental. (Dobbs majority)
  • Obergefell (Broad): History "guides but does not set outer boundaries." Rights can evolve as our understanding of injustice deepens. (Kennedy)

4. Judicial Supremacy vs. Legislative Centrality (§ 5)

  • Judicial Supremacy (Boerne): The Court defines the right. Congress can only remedy violations the Court has recognized.
  • Legislative Centrality (Morgan): Congress can independently determine what violates Equal Protection using its superior fact-finding power.

5. Federalism vs. National Power (Commerce/Spending)

  • Federalism: States are sovereign. The 10th Amendment reserves vast areas (education, criminal law, family law) to the states. (Lopez, NFIB Medicaid)
  • National Power: "We the People" created a national government with implied powers broad enough to address national problems. (McCulloch, Raich)

Core Rule: Government needs a warrant based on probable cause to search, UNLESS an exception applies.

Key Exceptions:

Exclusionary Rule (Mapp v. Ohio): Evidence obtained in violation of 4th Amend. is inadmissible ("fruit of the poisonous tree"). Exception: Good faith reliance on warrant (Leon).

5th Amendment — Self-Incrimination & Miranda

Miranda v. Arizona (1966): Before custodial interrogation, officers must warn: 1. Right to remain silent 2. Anything said can be used against you 3. Right to an attorney 4. If you can't afford one, one will be appointed

Triggers: Both (1) custody AND (2) interrogation must be present. Public encounters or voluntary conversations don't require Miranda.

6th Amendment — Right to Counsel

  • Attaches at the initiation of formal adversarial proceedings (indictment, arraignment).
  • Gideon v. Wainwright: Right to appointed counsel for indigent defendants in ALL felony cases.
  • Strickland v. Washington: Ineffective assistance = (1) deficient performance + (2) prejudice (reasonable probability result would have been different).

8th Amendment — Cruel & Unusual Punishment

"Evolving Standards of Decency" Test (Trop v. Dulles): - Death penalty: Constitutional for murder (Gregg), unconstitutional for rape of adult (Coker), unconstitutional for non-homicide crimes (Kennedy v. Louisiana) - Juveniles: No death penalty (Roper); no mandatory LWOP (Miller v. Alabama) - Timbs v. Indiana (2019): Excessive Fines Clause incorporated against states

SECTION D: THE 10th AND 11th AMENDMENTS — DEEPER DIVE

I. The Anti-Commandeering Doctrine (10th Amendment)

The Rule: Congress cannot commandeer state governments — it cannot force state legislatures to pass laws or force state executive officials to enforce federal programs.

Case What Congress Tried Holding
New York v. United States (1992) Forced states to either regulate radioactive waste or "take title" to it INVALID — commandeers state legislatures
Printz v. United States (1997) Required local sheriffs to conduct background checks under the Brady Act INVALID — commandeers state executive officials
Murphy v. NCAA (2018) Federal law prohibited states from authorizing sports betting INVALID — commandeers by telling states what laws they cannot pass

Fisher Distinction: Congress CAN: - Attach conditions to federal funding (Spending Power — Dole) - Preempt state law entirely (Supremacy Clause) - Regulate individuals directly (Commerce Clause)

Congress CANNOT: - Order state officials to implement federal programs (Printz) - Order state legislatures to legislate (New York) - Prohibit states from changing their own laws (Murphy)

II. State Sovereign Immunity (11th Amendment)

The Rule: States cannot be sued in federal court by their own citizens or citizens of other states, unless:

  1. The state consents to the suit;
  2. Congress abrogates sovereign immunity using a valid power source; OR
  3. The suit is against a state officer for prospective injunctive relief (Ex parte Young fiction).

The Abrogation Question:

Power Source Can Congress Abrogate? Key Case
Commerce Clause NO Seminole Tribe v. Florida (1996)
Art. I powers generally NO Seminole Tribe
§ 5 of the 14th Amendment YES — if congruent & proportional to documented violations Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer (1976)
§ 2 of the 13th Amendment YES (probably) Not yet definitively resolved

Fisher Connection: This links directly to Boerne. Congress must demonstrate a pattern of unconstitutional state behavior to abrogate immunity under § 5. A prophylactic law that sweeps too broadly fails Boerne's "congruence and proportionality" test.
