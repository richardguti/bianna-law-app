**Question 1**

Dwayne and Marcus agree to rob an armored car. Dwayne brings a gun. Marcus brings a crowbar. Elena, a security guard, sees them approach. She draws her weapon and orders them to stop. Dwayne shoots Elena in the leg. Marcus hits her with the crowbar. Elena dies. The jurisdiction follows the common law felony murder rule, with the "independent felony" limitation and the "agency" theory of causation. Which of the following is the most accurate statement regarding Dwayne's liability for Elena's death?

(A) Dwayne is guilty of felony murder because Elena's death occurred during the commission of a felony.
(B) Dwayne is guilty of felony murder only if the jury finds that he intended to kill Elena.
(C) Dwayne is not guilty of felony murder because the killing was not "in furtherance" of the felony.
(D) Dwayne is not guilty of felony murder because the killing was committed by a co-felon, not by a third party.
(E) Dwayne is guilty of felony murder, but only if Marcus is acquitted of the underlying felony.

**Answer: (A)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Correct.** Under the common law felony murder rule, any killing (even accidental) caused during the commission of an inherently dangerous felony is murder. Robbery with a deadly weapon is an inherently dangerous felony. Dwayne shot Elena during the robbery. The "independent felony" limitation is satisfied because the robbery is separate from the killing. The "agency" theory holds co-felons liable for killings they or their accomplices commit. Dwayne personally caused the death. No additional mens rea regarding the death is required.

- **(B) Incorrect.** Felony murder does not require intent to kill. It imputes malice from the intent to commit the underlying felony. This is the defining feature of the rule.

- **(C) Incorrect.** The killing *was* in furtherance of the felony. Dwayne shot Elena to overcome resistance and complete the robbery. This is the classic scenario for the rule's application.

- **(D) Incorrect.** The "agency" theory holds co-felons liable for killings they personally commit or that are committed by their accomplices. Dwayne is liable for his own act. The rule also covers killings by co-felons.

- **(E) Incorrect.** Felony murder liability is independent for each participant. Dwayne's liability does not depend on Marcus's conviction or acquittal.

**Question 2**

Assume the same facts as Question 1. The jurisdiction also follows the common law "year and a day" rule. Elena dies 13 months after the shooting. Which of the following is the most accurate statement?

(A) Dwayne cannot be convicted of murder because the death occurred outside the year-and-a-day period.
(B) Dwayne can be convicted of murder because the year-and-a-day rule has been abolished in all jurisdictions.
(C) Dwayne can be convicted of murder because the rule only applies to homicide, not to felony murder.
(D) Dwayne can be convicted of attempted murder and robbery, but not murder.
(E) Dwayne cannot be convicted of any crime because the death was too remote.

**Answer: (A)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Correct.** The common law year-and-a-day rule creates a conclusive presumption that if death occurs more than one year and one day after the act, causation cannot be established. Since Elena died at 13 months, the rule bars a murder conviction.

- **(B) Incorrect.** Many jurisdictions have abolished the rule, but the question specifies this jurisdiction "follows the common law" rule. We must apply it.

- **(C) Incorrect.** The rule applies to all homicide offenses, including felony murder.

- **(D) Correct in part, but (A) is more directly responsive.** Dwayne could be convicted of attempted murder and robbery. However, the question asks about liability for Elena's *death*. The best answer is (A) because it directly addresses the murder charge.

- **(E) Incorrect.** Dwayne can be convicted of crimes that do not require the death, such as attempted murder and robbery.

**Question 3**

Marcus, believing the robbery is a bad idea, tells Dwayne he is "out." Dwayne says, "Fine, I'll do it myself." Marcus leaves. Dwayne then commits the robbery alone. Under the common law, what is Marcus's liability?

(A) Marcus is guilty of robbery because he originally agreed to participate.
(B) Marcus is guilty of conspiracy to commit robbery, but not the robbery itself.
(C) Marcus is not guilty of any crime because he effectively withdrew.
(D) Marcus is guilty of robbery only if he communicated his withdrawal to the police.
(E) Marcus is guilty of attempted robbery.

**Answer: (C)**

**Analysis:**

- **(C) Correct.** Under the common law, a defendant can withdraw from a conspiracy by communicating their abandonment to all co-conspirators in time for them to abandon the plan. Marcus told Dwayne he was out, and Dwayne acknowledged it. Marcus then left before any crime was committed. He took no affirmative steps to further the plan after withdrawal. He is not liable for the subsequent robbery.

- **(A) Incorrect.** Withdrawal, if effective, negates liability for future acts of co-conspirators.

- **(B) Incorrect.** Conspiracy is an inchoate crime. If Marcus effectively withdrew before any overt act in furtherance of the conspiracy, he may not even be liable for conspiracy. More importantly, the question asks about liability for the robbery itself. Withdrawal cuts off liability for the substantive offense.

- **(D) Incorrect.** Communication to police is one way to withdraw, but it is not the only way. Communicating abandonment to co-conspirators is sufficient under the common law.

- **(E) Incorrect.** Marcus abandoned the plan before taking a substantial step toward completion. He is not liable for attempt.

**Question 4**

Elena, the security guard, survives the attack. She later dies from an infection caused by the crowbar wound. Marcus is charged with murder. The jurisdiction follows the common law "transferred intent" doctrine. Which of the following is the best argument for the defense?

(A) Marcus did not intend to kill anyone.
(B) The infection was an unforeseeable intervening cause.
(C) Marcus intended to rob, not to kill.
(D) Elena was a security guard, not an innocent bystander.
(E) The crowbar was not a deadly weapon.

**Answer: (B)**

**Analysis:**

- **(B) Correct.** The best defense argument is that the infection was a superseding, intervening cause that breaks the chain of causation. While defendants take their victims as they find them (eggshell skull rule), an infection from a wound is generally foreseeable. However, if the infection was highly unusual or resulted from grossly negligent medical treatment, it could be argued as a superseding cause. This is the most viable defense.

- **(A) Incorrect.** Intent to kill is not required for felony murder. Malice is implied from the intent to commit the felony.

- **(C) Incorrect.** Same as (A). The intent to rob supplies the malice.

- **(D) Incorrect.** The transferred intent doctrine applies when a defendant intends to harm one person but harms another. Here, Marcus intended to harm the security guard. The doctrine is not needed. The issue is causation, not intent.

- **(E) Incorrect.** A crowbar used as a bludgeon is clearly a deadly weapon. This is a factual impossibility.

**Question 5**

Dwayne is charged with first-degree murder. The prosecutor argues it was premeditated. Which of the following facts, if true, would be *most* helpful to the defense in negating premeditation?

(A) Dwayne and Marcus planned the robbery for three weeks.
(B) Dwayne shot Elena immediately after she drew her weapon.
(C) Dwayne had a history of violent behavior.
(D) Dwayne told Marcus, "If anyone gets in the way, I'll take them out."
(E) Dwayne shot Elena three times after she fell to the ground.

**Answer: (B)**

**Analysis:**

- **(B) Correct.** Premeditation requires some period of reflection, however brief. A spontaneous, reactive shooting in response to a sudden threat is the classic scenario for negating premeditation (arguing for second-degree murder or manslaughter). The fact that Dwayne shot "immediately" after Elena drew her weapon suggests a reflexive, heat-of-the-moment reaction, not a considered decision to kill.

- **(A) Incorrect.** Planning the robbery for three weeks supports premeditation regarding the robbery, and by extension, the killing if it was part of the plan.

- **(C) Incorrect.** A history of violence is generally prejudicial and does not negate premeditation. It might even support a prosecution theory of a violent character.

- **(D) Incorrect.** This statement is direct evidence of premeditation. Dwayne announced his intent to kill in advance.

- **(E) Incorrect.** Shooting someone multiple times after they are incapacitated is strong evidence of a calculated, intentional killing, supporting premeditation.

**Question 6**

Marcus is charged with attempted murder for hitting Elena with the crowbar. The jurisdiction uses the "Model Penal Code" substantial step test. Which of the following is the *best* argument that Marcus is *not* guilty of attempted murder?

(A) Marcus only intended to disable Elena, not to kill her.
(B) Marcus did not complete the crime of murder.
(C) Marcus withdrew from the plan before the robbery.
(D) Hitting someone with a crowbar is not a substantial step toward murder.
(E) Marcus was acting under duress from Dwayne.

**Answer: (A)**

**Analysis:**

- **(A) Correct.** Attempt requires specific intent to commit the underlying offense. For attempted murder, the prosecution must prove Marcus specifically intended to kill Elena. If Marcus only intended to disable her (to complete the robbery), he lacks the specific intent for attempted murder. He could be guilty of attempted battery or assault with a deadly weapon, but not attempted murder.

- **(B) Incorrect.** Failure to complete the crime is the definition of attempt. This is not a defense; it is an element the prosecution must prove.

- **(C) Incorrect.** The facts state Marcus participated in the attack. He did not withdraw before the crowbar strike.

- **(D) Incorrect.** Hitting someone in the head with a crowbar is a substantial step toward killing them. It is conduct strongly corroborative of a criminal purpose.

- **(E) Incorrect.** Duress is a defense, but it is not the *best* argument here. The facts do not suggest duress. The specific intent argument is a direct challenge to an element of the crime.

**Question 7**

Assume the jurisdiction has adopted the Model Penal Code's definition of "causation." Elena dies from the gunshot wound. Dwayne argues that Elena's death was caused by the negligent paramedic who dropped the stretcher, not by the gunshot. Which of the following is the most accurate statement?

(A) Dwayne's argument will succeed because the paramedic's negligence was an intervening cause.
(B) Dwayne's argument will fail because the paramedic's negligence was a foreseeable risk.
(C) Dwayne's argument will succeed because the paramedic's negligence was a but-for cause of death.
(D) Dwayne's argument will fail because the gunshot was the direct cause of death.
(E) Dwayne's argument will succeed only if the paramedic is convicted of a crime.

**Answer: (B)**

**Analysis:**

- **(B) Correct.** Under the MPC § 2.03, a result is not caused by the defendant if it is "too remote or accidental in its occurrence to have a [just] bearing on the actor's liability or on the gravity of his offense." Negligent medical treatment is a foreseeable risk of inflicting a serious injury. It is not a "remote or accidental" intervening cause. Therefore, Dwayne is still the legal cause of Elena's death.

- **(A) Incorrect.** Negligent medical treatment is generally not considered a superseding cause that breaks the chain of causation. The defendant is liable for the consequences of his actions, including foreseeable intervening acts.

- **(C) Incorrect.** The paramedic's negligence might be a but-for cause, but that does not absolve Dwayne. There can be multiple but-for causes. The question is whether the intervening cause is superseding.

- **(D) Incorrect.** The gunshot is a factual cause, but the legal causation analysis requires examining intervening causes. The statement is too simplistic.

- **(E) Incorrect.** The paramedic's criminal liability is irrelevant to Dwayne's causation analysis.

**Question 8**

Dwayne is charged with robbery. Marcus testifies against Dwayne in exchange for immunity. At trial, the prosecutor asks Marcus, "Did Dwayne tell you he was going to bring a gun?" Dwayne's attorney objects. The objection should be:

(A) Sustained, because it calls for hearsay.
(B) Overruled, because it is a statement of a co-conspirator in furtherance of the conspiracy.
(C) Sustained, because it violates Dwayne's right to confrontation.
(D) Overruled, because it is an admission by a party opponent.
(E) Sustained, because Marcus is an accomplice whose testimony is inherently unreliable.

**Answer: (D)**

**Analysis:**

- **(D) Correct.** Dwayne's statement to Marcus is an out-of-court statement offered to prove the truth of the matter asserted (that Dwayne brought a gun). However, it is an admission by a party opponent (FRE 801(d)(2)(A)). Dwayne's own statement is not hearsay. Marcus is testifying about what Dwayne said.

- **(A) Incorrect.** While it is hearsay in form, it falls under the party-opponent exclusion, which is not hearsay.

- **(B) Incorrect.** The co-conspirator exception (FRE 801(d)(2)(E)) applies to statements made *by* a co-conspirator, not *to* a co-conspirator. Marcus is testifying about Dwayne's statement, not his own. The party-opponent exclusion is the correct basis.

- **(C) Incorrect.** The Confrontation Clause applies to testimonial hearsay. Since this is an admission by a party opponent, it is not hearsay, and the Confrontation Clause is not implicated in the same way. The defendant's own statements are admissible against him.

- **(E) Incorrect.** The reliability of an accomplice's testimony is a matter for the jury to weigh, not a basis for excluding the evidence entirely.

**Question 9**

The jurisdiction defines "deadly weapon" as "any object that is readily capable of causing death or serious bodily injury." Marcus used a crowbar. Dwayne used a gun. Which of the following is the most accurate statement?

(A) The crowbar is a deadly weapon as a matter of law.

(E) The gun is a deadly weapon, but the crowbar is not.

**Answer: (B)**

**Analysis:**

- **(B) Correct.** Under this definition, some objects are deadly weapons per se (e.g., guns, swords). Others are deadly weapons depending on their use. A crowbar is not a per se deadly weapon; it is an object that can be used as one. The jury must determine if Marcus used it in a way "readily capable of causing death or serious bodily injury" (e.g., striking someone in the head).

- **(A) Incorrect.** A crowbar is not a per se deadly weapon like a firearm. Its classification depends on the manner of use.

- **(C) Incorrect.** A gun is almost universally considered a deadly weapon per se, regardless of whether it is loaded. The capability to cause death is inherent in the instrument. An unloaded gun can be used as a bludgeon.

- **(D) Incorrect.** The definition focuses on the *capability* of the object, not the actual result. An object can be a deadly weapon even if no death occurs.

- **(E) Incorrect.** Both can be deadly weapons. The gun is per se; the crowbar is potentially so.

**Question 10**

During the robbery, a bystander, Victor, sees Dwayne shoot Elena. Victor suffers extreme emotional distress and later has a heart attack. Victor dies. Dwayne is charged with Victor's murder under the felony murder rule. The jurisdiction follows the "proximate cause" theory of felony murder. Which of the following is the most accurate statement?

(A) Dwayne is guilty of felony murder because Victor's death was a foreseeable consequence of the robbery.
(B) Dwayne is not guilty of felony murder because Victor was not a co-felon or a police officer.
(C) Dwayne is not guilty of felony murder because Victor's death was too remote and accidental.
(D) Dwayne is guilty of felony murder because the felony murder rule imposes strict liability for all deaths.
(E) Dwayne is not guilty of felony murder because he did not intend to harm Victor.

**Answer: (C)**

**Analysis:**

- **(C) Correct.** Under the proximate cause theory, a defendant is liable for deaths that are the foreseeable consequence of the felony. However, there are limits. A death caused by a heart attack in a bystander who merely *witnesses* the crime is generally considered too remote and accidental to be proximately caused by the felony. The chain of causation is too attenuated. The death was not a foreseeable risk of the robbery itself.

- **(A) Incorrect.** While proximate cause requires foreseeability, a heart attack in a remote bystander is not a foreseeable risk of an armed robbery. The risk is of violent confrontation with victims or security.

- **(B) Incorrect.** The proximate cause theory does not limit liability to co-felons or police officers. It can extend to innocent bystanders, but only if the death is a foreseeable result of the felony.

- **(D) Incorrect.** Felony murder is not strict liability for *all* deaths. The proximate cause theory imposes a foreseeability limitation.

- **(E) Incorrect.** Intent to harm is not required for felony murder. The issue is causation, not mens rea regarding the death.

**Question 11**

Marcus is charged with robbery. His defense is that he was acting under duress because Dwayne threatened to kill him if he did not participate. Which of the following is the most accurate statement regarding the duress defense?

**Answer: (C)**

- **(C) Correct.** The common law defense of duress requires: (1) a threat of imminent death or serious bodily injury, (2) a reasonable belief that the threat will be carried out, and (3) no reasonable opportunity to escape. Marcus must show Dwayne's threat was immediate and he had no safe way out.

- **(A) Incorrect.** Duress is generally not a defense to murder (killing an innocent person). It is a defense to most other crimes, including robbery.

- **(B) Incorrect.** Duress is a defense to robbery. Robbery is a crime against property and person, but it is not an exception to the duress defense (unlike murder).

- **(D) Incorrect.** Reporting to the police is not a universal requirement for duress. It may be relevant to showing Marcus had no reasonable escape, but it is not an independent element.

- **(E) Incorrect.** Duress is a justification for participating in the crime. Marcus must have participated under the threat. If he did not participate, he would not need the defense.

### PART II: ESSAY 1 – POLICY MODEL ANSWER (800-1000 words)

**Question:** "The primary purpose of criminal punishment is to give offenders what they deserve. All other goals are secondary." Critically evaluate this statement with reference to retributive, utilitarian, and mixed theories of punishment. In your answer, explain how a "three strikes" law might be justified or criticized from each perspective.

---

**Model Answer:**

The statement that giving offenders their "just deserts" is the primary purpose of punishment reflects a **retributivist** philosophy. This view stands in sharp contrast to **utilitarian** theories, which see punishment as a means to achieve future social benefits. A **mixed theory** attempts to synthesize these competing values. This essay will critically evaluate the statement, arguing that while retribution is a necessary and foundational principle, a purely retributive system is incomplete and potentially unjust. A mixed theory, which incorporates desert as a limiting principle while pursuing utilitarian goals, offers a more coherent and humane framework for criminal justice. The analysis will be grounded in the context of "three strikes" laws, which serve as a powerful test case for these competing theories.

**Retributive Theory: The Primacy of Desert**

The statement aligns perfectly with retributivism, most famously articulated by Immanuel Kant. Retributivism holds that punishment is justified because it is *deserved*. The criminal has gained an unfair advantage by violating the law, and punishment restores the moral equilibrium. Punishment is an end in itself, not a means to another end. The severity of punishment must be proportionate to the moral wrongness of the crime ("lex talionis" – an eye for an eye).

From a retributive perspective, a "three strikes" law is deeply problematic. Retributivism demands proportionality. Punishing a third felony, such as stealing a bicycle, with a life sentence is grossly disproportionate to the moral wrong of that individual act. The punishment is not "deserved" for that crime. A retributivist would argue that the offender should be punished for each crime in proportion to its severity. The prior convictions are relevant to *culpability* (a repeat offender knows the law and is more blameworthy), but they do not justify a punishment that is wildly out of proportion to the current offense. A retributivist would likely criticize three strikes laws as a form of state vengeance, not justice.

**Utilitarian Theory: Punishment as a Means to an End**

Utilitarianism, associated with Jeremy Bentham, rejects the idea that punishment is an end in itself. Punishment is an evil (it inflicts pain), and it is only justified if it produces a greater good. The primary goals are **deterrence** (general and specific), **incapacitation**, and **rehabilitation**. The severity of punishment should be the minimum necessary to achieve these goals.

A utilitarian could justify a "three strikes" law on several grounds. First, **specific deterrence**: the offender has proven immune to lesser punishments, so a severe sanction is needed to deter *that specific individual* from reoffending. Second, and more powerfully, **incapacitation**: a life sentence physically prevents a repeat offender from committing future crimes against society. This is a direct, measurable benefit. Third, **general deterrence**: the law sends a powerful message to all potential repeat offenders that the consequences will be severe.

However, a utilitarian would also criticize the law. If the law does not actually deter (because repeat offenders may not be rational calculators), or if it leads to other social costs (e.g., overcrowded prisons, the cost of housing elderly inmates, the injustice of punishing minor crimes severely), the net utility may be negative. A utilitarian would ask: does the pain inflicted by the law (on the offender, on taxpayers) outweigh the pleasure of crime prevented? The answer is an empirical question. Furthermore, a utilitarian might prefer rehabilitation for the first two strikes, reserving incapacitation only for the truly incorrigible.

**Mixed Theory: A Synthesis**

A mixed theory, such as that proposed by H.L.A. Hart, seeks to combine the strengths of both approaches. Hart argued that the *general justifying aim* of the institution of punishment is utilitarian (to reduce crime), but the *distribution* of punishment (who gets punished and how much) must be governed by retributive principles. In other words, we punish to achieve social benefits, but we can only punish those who are *deserving* (guilty), and we can only punish them to the extent they *deserve* (proportionality).

From a mixed theory perspective, the "three strikes" law is a flawed policy. The utilitarian goal of incapacitation is legitimate. However, the law violates the retributive principle of proportionality. A mixed theorist would argue that the law's mandatory nature removes judicial discretion, leading to unjust and disproportionate sentences. A better approach would be to use prior convictions as an *aggravating factor* that allows a judge to impose a longer sentence, but within a range that remains proportionate to the current offense. The desert of the offender is a *limiting principle* on the pursuit of utilitarian goals.

**Conclusion**

The statement that giving offenders what they deserve is the "primary" purpose of punishment is a strong articulation of retributivism. However, a purely retributive system ignores the practical, crime-reducing functions of punishment that are essential for a functioning society. A purely utilitarian system, on the other hand, risks sacrificing individual justice for the greater good, leading to draconian policies like mandatory three strikes laws. The most defensible position is a mixed theory that recognizes retribution as a necessary foundation—punishment must be deserved and proportionate—while allowing the system to pursue the utilitarian goals of deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation within those desert-based limits. The "three strikes" law, when viewed through this lens, is a cautionary tale of what happens when utilitarian goals are pursued without a robust commitment to proportionality and individual justice.

### PART III: ESSAY 2 – FACT PATTERN MODEL ANSWER

**Question:** Analyze the potential criminal liability of Dwayne, Marcus, and Elena for the death of Elena and any other relevant offenses. Consider all possible charges, including homicide, robbery, attempt, conspiracy, and any defenses. Apply the common law principles discussed in this course.

---

**Model Answer:**

This fact pattern presents a classic felony murder scenario with complex issues of accomplice liability, withdrawal, and causation. The potential defendants are Dwayne, Marcus, and Elena (the security guard). This analysis will examine their liability under common law principles.

**I. Dwayne's Liability**

**A. Homicide of Elena**

1.  **Felony Murder:** Dwayne is almost certainly guilty of felony murder. The elements are: (1) a killing, (2) caused by the defendant, (3) during the commission of an inherently dangerous felony. Robbery with a deadly weapon is an inherently dangerous felony. Dwayne shot Elena during the robbery. The killing was in furtherance of the felony (to overcome resistance). Under the "agency" theory, Dwayne is liable for his own act. The "independent felony" limitation is satisfied because the robbery is separate from the killing. No intent to kill is required. The malice is implied from the intent to commit the robbery. Dwayne is guilty of murder.

2.  **Premeditated Murder (First-Degree):** The prosecutor could also argue for first-degree murder. The key is premeditation. The facts are ambiguous. Planning the robbery for three weeks and Dwayne's statement "If anyone gets in the way, I'll take them out" support premeditation. However, the shooting was "immediately" after Elena drew her weapon, suggesting a reactive, spontaneous act. A jury could find premeditation (any period of reflection, however brief) or could find it was a second-degree murder (intentional killing without premeditation). The safest charge is felony murder, which does not require proving premeditation.

3.  **Attempted Murder:** If Elena had survived, Dwayne would be guilty of attempted murder. He took a substantial step (shooting her) with the specific intent to kill.

**B. Robbery**

Dwayne is guilty of robbery. Robbery is larceny by force or threat of force from a person's presence. Dwayne and Marcus attempted to take money from the armored car. Dwayne used a gun to overcome Elena's resistance. The elements are satisfied.

**C. Conspiracy to Commit Robbery**

Dwayne is guilty of conspiracy. An agreement between Dwayne and Marcus to commit a crime, plus an overt act (planning, approaching the car). The agreement is clear.

**D. Defenses**

Dwayne has no viable defenses. Duress is not applicable. Self-defense is not applicable because Elena was lawfully performing her duty as a security guard. Dwayne was the initial aggressor.

**II. Marcus's Liability**

**A. Homicide of Elena**

1.  **Felony Murder (Accomplice Liability):** Marcus is also guilty of felony murder as an accomplice. Under the common law, an accomplice is liable for all crimes that are the natural and probable consequence of the conspiracy. Killing a security guard during an armed robbery is a natural and probable consequence. Marcus actively participated by bringing a crowbar and striking Elena. He is liable for the death caused by his co-felon, Dwayne. The "agency" theory holds co-felons liable for killings committed by their accomplices in furtherance of the common purpose.

2.  **Attempted Murder:** Marcus could also be charged with attempted murder for hitting Elena with the crowbar. The prosecution must prove he had the specific intent to kill. This is a difficult burden. Hitting someone with a crowbar is a substantial step toward killing, but Marcus may argue he only intended to disable her. A jury could infer intent to kill from the use of a deadly weapon, but it is not automatic. This is a weaker charge than felony murder.

**B. Robbery**

Marcus is guilty of robbery as a principal or accomplice. He participated in the taking by force.

**C. Conspiracy**

Marcus is guilty of conspiracy, subject to the withdrawal analysis below.

**D. Withdrawal as a Defense**

Marcus's claim that he "withdrew" by telling Dwayne he was "out" is a critical issue. Under the common law, effective withdrawal requires: (1) a communication of abandonment to all co-conspirators, (2) in time for them to abandon the plan, and (3) no affirmative acts in furtherance after withdrawal. Marcus communicated his withdrawal to Dwayne. However, the facts are ambiguous about *when* this occurred. If Marcus withdrew *before* the robbery began, and Dwayne acknowledged it, then Marcus is not liable for the subsequent robbery or the murder. He would only be liable for the conspiracy up to the point of withdrawal.

However, if Marcus withdrew *after* the robbery had begun (e.g., after they approached the car), his withdrawal is too late. He is already an accomplice. Once a crime is underway, a defendant cannot unilaterally withdraw by mere communication; he must take affirmative steps to prevent the crime (e.g., call the police, actively try to stop Dwayne). The facts are unclear. The best argument for the defense is that Marcus withdrew before any overt act. The prosecution will argue he was a full participant.

**E. Duress**

Marcus's claim of duress (Dwayne threatened to kill him) is a potential defense. The elements are: (1) a threat of imminent death or serious bodily injury, (2) a reasonable belief the threat will be carried out, and (3) no reasonable escape. If Marcus can show Dwayne's threat was immediate and he had no safe way out, duress could negate his culpability for robbery and felony murder. However, duress is generally not a defense to murder (killing an innocent person). Dwayne is clearly guilty of felony murder and robbery. Marcus is also likely guilty of felony murder as an accomplice, but his withdrawal and duress claims are viable defenses that a jury must weigh.
